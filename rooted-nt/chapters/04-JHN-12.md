# John 12
theme: The Hour Has Come: Anointed, Welcomed, and Lifted Up
main: Six days before Passover, Mary anoints Jesus' feet with costly perfume in an act of extravagant love that prepares Him for burial. The next day crowds welcome Him into Jerusalem as King, and when Greeks come asking to see Him, Jesus announces that the hour of His glorification has arrived. Like a grain of wheat that must die to bear fruit, He will be lifted up on the cross and draw all people to Himself. The chapter closes the first half of John's Gospel with a sober reflection on unbelief and a final appeal to believe in the light.

## Chapter Background and Story

John 12 is a hinge in the Gospel. It concludes Jesus' public ministry, which John has presented through seven signs and many conversations. From chapter 13 onward, Jesus will speak privately to His disciples and then go to the cross. Throughout the Gospel, Jesus has said, "My hour has not yet come" (John 2:4; 7:30; 8:20). In this chapter, He finally says, "The time has come" (12:23).

The chapter moves through three scenes: a dinner in Bethany, a royal welcome in Jerusalem, and a moment when Greeks seek Jesus. Then John reflects on why so many did not believe and records Jesus' final public appeal.

### Mary Anoints Jesus (12:1–11)

Six days before Passover, Jesus returned to Bethany, the village where He had raised Lazarus. A dinner was given in His honor. Mark and Matthew place it in the house of Simon the leper (Mark 14:3), and John adds the details of the family: [[v2|Martha served, but Lazarus was one of those who sat at the table with him]]. The man who had been dead was now eating with Jesus.

Then Mary took a pound of very expensive perfume made from pure nard, a fragrant oil imported from the Himalayas, and anointed Jesus' feet, wiping them with her hair. This is studied below. (Matthew and Mark describe her pouring perfume on His head; she likely anointed both His head and His feet. Luke 7 records a different anointing earlier in Jesus' ministry by a sinful woman in Galilee.)

Judas Iscariot objected: [[v5|Why wasn’t this ointment sold for three hundred denarii and given to the poor?]] Three hundred denarii was about a year's wages for a laborer. Judas's words sounded generous, but John explains his real motive: [[v6|he was a thief, and having the money box, used to steal what was put into it]]. Judas, the treasurer of the group, was already showing the greed that would lead to betrayal.

Jesus defended Mary: [[v7|Leave her alone. She has kept this for the day of my burial]]. And He said, [[v8|For you always have the poor with you, but you don’t always have me]]. Jesus was not dismissing care for the poor; His words echo Deuteronomy 15:11, which commands generosity to the poor precisely because they will always be present. He was saying that this was a unique moment, the last opportunity to honor Him before His death.

A large crowd came to Bethany to see both Jesus and Lazarus. The chief priests then planned to kill Lazarus too, because many people were believing in Jesus because of him. Lazarus himself had become evidence that could not be ignored.

### The King Enters Jerusalem (12:12–19)

The next day, a great crowd of Passover pilgrims heard that Jesus was coming to Jerusalem. They took palm branches and went out to meet Him, shouting, [[v13|Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, the King of Israel!]] Palm branches had become a symbol of national victory since the days of the Maccabees, who had freed Jerusalem from foreign rule. The words come from Psalm 118:25–26, a psalm sung at the great festivals. This event is studied at Matthew 21:9.

Jesus found a young donkey and sat on it, fulfilling Zechariah 9:9: [[v15|Don’t be afraid, daughter of Zion. Behold, your King comes, sitting on a donkey’s colt]]. A conquering king might ride a warhorse; Jesus came humbly, as a king of peace. John adds an honest note: [[v16|His disciples didn’t understand these things at first, but when Jesus was glorified, then they remembered that these things were written about him]]. Understanding came after the resurrection.

The crowd that had seen Lazarus raised kept telling about it, and many came out because of this sign. The Pharisees said to one another, [[v19|See how you accomplish nothing. Behold, the world has gone after him]]. Their words were an exaggeration, but also, unknowingly, a prophecy. The very next verses show "the world" coming.

### Greeks Seek Jesus (12:20–36)

Among the worshipers at the feast were some Greeks, probably Gentiles who worshiped the God of Israel. They came to Philip, who had a Greek name and came from Bethsaida, a town with Gentile influence, and said, [[v21|Sir, we want to see Jesus]]. Philip told Andrew, and together they told Jesus.

Jesus' answer seems at first not to address their request: [[v23|The time has come for the Son of Man to be glorified]]. But the arrival of Gentiles seeking Him signaled that the moment had come for His death, which would open the way for all nations. He explained with the image of a grain of wheat, studied below.

Then Jesus revealed His inner anguish: [[v27|Now my soul is troubled. What shall I say? ‘Father, save me from this time’? But I came to this time for this cause]]. This is John's equivalent of the Gethsemane prayer in the other Gospels (studied at Mark 14:36). Jesus felt the weight of what lay ahead, yet He chose the Father's will. He prayed, [[v28|Father, glorify your name!]] A voice came from heaven: [[v28|I have both glorified it and will glorify it again]]. Some in the crowd thought it was thunder; others thought an angel had spoken. Jesus said that the voice had come for their sake.

[[v31|Now is the judgment of this world. Now the prince of this world will be cast out]]. The cross, which looked like Satan's victory, would actually be his defeat. Then Jesus spoke of being lifted up, studied below.

The crowd was confused. They had learned from Scripture that the Messiah would remain forever (perhaps from texts like Psalm 89:36 and Isaiah 9:7). How could the Son of Man be lifted up? Jesus did not argue but urged them: [[v35|Yet a little while the light is with you. Walk while you have the light, that darkness doesn’t overtake you]]. [[v36|While you have the light, believe in the light, that you may become children of light]]. Then He left and hid Himself.

### The Tragedy of Unbelief (12:37–43)

John now steps back to reflect on Jesus' public ministry: [[v37|But though he had done so many signs before them, yet they didn’t believe in him]]. He explains this unbelief by quoting two passages from Isaiah. The first is Isaiah 53:1: [[v38|Lord, who has believed our report? To whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed?]] The second is Isaiah 6:10, describing hearts hardened and eyes blinded. The same God who sends light also judges those who persistently reject it, so that their refusal becomes inability.

John adds a remarkable statement: [[v41|Isaiah said these things when he saw his glory, and spoke of him]]. In Isaiah 6, the prophet saw the Lord, high and lifted up, in the temple. John says that the glory Isaiah saw was the glory of Jesus. This is a powerful testimony to Jesus' divine identity.

Yet not all rejected Him. Many even among the rulers believed, though they kept their faith secret. This is studied below.

### Jesus' Final Public Appeal (12:44–50)

John closes the section with a summary of Jesus' message. Jesus cried out, [[v44|Whoever believes in me, believes not in me, but in him who sent me]]. [[v45|He who sees me sees him who sent me]]. [[v46|I have come as a light into the world, that whoever believes in me may not remain in the darkness]].

Jesus had come not to judge the world but to save it. Yet those who rejected Him would be judged by the very word He had spoken. His words were not His own invention; they came from the Father, [[v50|I know that his commandment is eternal life]]. The public ministry of Jesus ends with an invitation to believe and a warning not to refuse the light.

## Important Verses Explained

### John 12:3 — The House Filled with Fragrance

verse: John 12:3

#### Simple Explanation

This scene took place at a dinner in Bethany six days before Passover, given in Jesus' honor. Lazarus, whom Jesus had raised from the dead, was at the table. Martha was serving. Mary, the sister who had once sat at Jesus' feet to listen to His teaching (Luke 10:39), and who had fallen at His feet in grief after Lazarus died (John 11:32), now came to His feet again.

[[v3|Therefore Mary took a pound of ointment of pure nard, very precious]]. A Roman pound was about twelve ounces, a large amount of an extremely costly perfume. Nard came from a plant grown in the Himalayan mountains of India and had to be imported. Judas valued it at three hundred denarii, roughly a year's wages for a laborer. This may have been Mary's most valuable possession, perhaps kept for her own future.

[[v3|and anointed Jesus’ feet and wiped his feet with her hair]]. Anointing the feet was unusual; it was a humble act normally done by servants. A respectable woman in that culture did not usually let down her hair in public. Mary set aside her dignity in an act of humble, wholehearted devotion.

[[v3|The house was filled with the fragrance of the ointment]]. Mary's act could not be hidden. The fragrance filled the whole house, touching everyone present.

Jesus explained the deeper meaning of her action: [[v7|She has kept this for the day of my burial]]. Whether Mary fully understood or not, she was preparing Jesus' body for His death. The other Gospels record Jesus' promise that her act would be remembered wherever the gospel is preached (studied at Matthew 26:13).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Mary's act is a picture of worship that holds nothing back. Love does not calculate the minimum required; it gives generously. Mary gave what was costly because she understood, more than the others, the worth of Jesus. Her gift expressed gratitude for her brother's life and devotion to the One who was about to die.

The contrast with Judas is striking. Both looked at the same perfume. Mary saw a way to honor Jesus; Judas saw money. The scene reveals that the heart's attitude toward Jesus shows itself in how a person uses what they have. Paul writes that believers are [[2 Corinthians 2:15|a sweet aroma of Christ to God]], spreading the fragrance of the knowledge of Him everywhere.

The anointing also points to Jesus' identity. The title "Christ" or "Messiah" means "Anointed One." Kings and priests were anointed with oil. Mary anointed Jesus just before He entered Jerusalem as King and went to the cross as the Priest who offered Himself.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Mary's devotion invites believers to consider what they are offering to Jesus. Some acts of love for Him may seem wasteful to others: time spent in worship, generosity toward His work, careers or plans surrendered to His call. When Jesus is worth everything, giving Him our best is not a waste.

The fragrance filling the house also reminds believers that genuine devotion affects others. A life given to Christ spreads His presence in families, workplaces, and communities, often in ways that cannot be measured.

Finally, the scene warns against criticizing the devotion of others. Judas used concern for the poor as a cover for his own greed. Believers can sometimes be quick to judge how others worship or give. Jesus defended Mary. He receives sincere love offered to Him, even when others misunderstand it.

### John 12:24–26 — Unless a Grain of Wheat Dies

verse: John 12:24-26

#### Simple Explanation

Some Greeks had come to Jerusalem for the Passover and asked to see Jesus. When Philip and Andrew brought the request to Him, Jesus announced that His hour had come to be glorified. Then He explained what that glory would involve.

[[v24|unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains by itself alone]]. A seed of wheat kept in a jar remains a single seed. It may be preserved, but it produces nothing. [[v24|But if it dies, it bears much fruit]]. When the seed is buried in the ground and its outer shell breaks down, new life sprouts, and a single seed becomes a stalk with many grains.

Jesus was speaking about His own death. He would be "buried in the earth," and through His death, He would bring life to many, including the Greeks who were seeking Him and people from every nation.

Then He applied the principle to His followers: [[v25|He who loves his life will lose it. He who hates his life in this world will keep it to eternal life]]. "Hating" one's life is a Hebrew way of expressing strong preference. It means loving God so much more than one's own life in this world that self-interest is no longer the controlling priority. Those who cling to their lives for themselves will lose what matters most; those who give their lives to God will gain eternal life.

[[v26|If anyone serves me, let him follow me. Where I am, there my servant will also be. If anyone serves me, the Father will honor him]]. To serve Jesus is to follow Him, even on the path of self-giving. And there is a promise: those who serve Him will be with Him and will be honored by the Father.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The image of the grain of wheat reveals the meaning of the cross. Jesus' death was not a tragic end but the planting of a seed that would produce an enormous harvest. His death and resurrection opened the way for people of all nations to receive eternal life. The church throughout history is the fruit of the seed that fell into the ground at Calvary.

The principle also shapes the life of every disciple. The pattern of Jesus' life, death leading to life, becomes the pattern of the believer's life. Similar teaching appears in the other Gospels (studied at Mark 8:36 and Matthew 16:24), but here it is tied directly to the image of fruitful dying. Self-giving is not loss but the way to fruitfulness.

Finally, the promise of verse 26 shows God's generous heart. Those who follow Jesus in self-giving service are not forgotten. The Father Himself honors them. This is not earned merit but the delight of a Father who honors those who honor His Son.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage challenges a culture that values self-preservation and self-promotion above all. Jesus teaches that fruitfulness comes through self-giving. Believers bear fruit when they lay down their own preferences, comfort, and ambitions to serve God and others.

It also brings meaning to sacrifice. Parents who give years to raising children, believers who serve quietly in their churches, people who care for aging relatives, and missionaries who leave home for the sake of the gospel are following the pattern of the grain of wheat. Their sacrifices are not wasted; they bear fruit.

Finally, the passage gives hope in times of loss. Sometimes God allows things in believers' lives to "die," such as plans, dreams, or comforts. These experiences are painful, but in God's hands they can become seeds of new life and greater fruitfulness.

### John 12:32 — I Will Draw All People to Myself

verse: John 12:32

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus had just heard a voice from heaven responding to His prayer, "Father, glorify your name." He told the crowd that the voice had come for their benefit and that now was the time for judgment on this world and for its ruler to be cast out. Then He said, [[v32|And I, if I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself]].

John explains what Jesus meant: [[v33|But he said this, signifying by what kind of death he should die]]. Being "lifted up from the earth" refers to crucifixion. A crucified person was raised up on a cross above the ground. Jesus used this phrase earlier in John's Gospel too, comparing His being lifted up to the bronze serpent Moses lifted up in the wilderness (John 3:14).

The phrase also has a double meaning. "Lifted up" can mean "exalted." In John's Gospel, the cross is not only Jesus' humiliation but also His glorification. Through His death, Jesus would be exalted, leading to His resurrection and return to the Father.

[[v32|will draw all people to myself]]. This does not mean that every single person will be saved, since John's Gospel clearly teaches that some reject Jesus (12:48). It means that Jesus will draw people of all kinds, from every nation, not only Jews but also Gentiles like the Greeks who had just asked to see Him. The cross would become the place where people from every background are drawn to God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the power of the cross. The cross was intended by Jesus' enemies to destroy Him and His movement. Instead, it became the center of His saving work and the means by which He draws people to Himself. Paul writes that the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to those who are being saved [[1 Corinthians 1:18|it is the power of God]].

It also shows the universal scope of the gospel. The Greeks who came to see Jesus represent the nations of the world. Through His death, Jesus would break down the barrier between Jews and Gentiles and gather one people for God from every tribe and language (Ephesians 2:14–16; Revelation 5:9).

Finally, the verse shows that salvation is Jesus' work. He says, "I will draw." Earlier He said that no one can come to Him unless the Father draws them (John 6:44). The Father and the Son work together to draw people through the message of the cross.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to keep the cross at the center of their message. It is tempting to present Christianity mainly as a source of practical wisdom or moral improvement. But Jesus said that He would draw people through being lifted up on the cross. The message of His sacrificial death and resurrection has power to draw people to God.

It also gives hope for those who seem far from God. Jesus draws all kinds of people to Himself. No background, nationality, or history puts someone beyond the reach of the cross.

Finally, this verse invites each reader to respond to Christ's drawing. Many people feel a pull toward Jesus, a sense that He is calling them. That pull is His drawing, and it is an invitation to come to Him.

### John 12:42–43 — Loving Human Praise

verse: John 12:42-43

#### Simple Explanation

John has just explained that many people did not believe in Jesus despite all the signs He had done. He quoted Isaiah to show that their unbelief had been foretold. Then he added a qualification: [[v42|Nevertheless, even many of the rulers believed in him]]. Not everyone among the leaders rejected Jesus. Some recognized that He was from God.

[[v42|but because of the Pharisees they didn’t confess it, so that they wouldn’t be put out of the synagogue]]. These rulers kept their faith secret. They feared the consequences of openly confessing Jesus. John 9 showed what could happen: the man born blind was thrown out of the synagogue for speaking in Jesus' favor. Being expelled meant losing one's place in religious and social life, and for rulers it would mean losing their position and influence.

Then John gives the reason behind their silence: [[v43|for they loved men’s praise more than God’s praise]]. Their problem was not only fear but misplaced love. They valued the approval of people more than the approval of God. Jesus had diagnosed the same problem earlier: [[John 5:44|How can you believe, who receive glory from one another, and you don’t seek the glory that comes from the only God?]]

John does not say whether these secret believers ever came into the open. But he does tell of two rulers who later did: Joseph of Arimathea, who was a disciple [[John 19:38|but secretly for fear of the Jews]], and Nicodemus. After the crucifixion, both came forward to give Jesus an honorable burial.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal that the desire for human approval can be a powerful barrier to faithfulness. It is possible to believe something is true and still refuse to stand for it because of what others might think. Jesus taught that those who are ashamed of Him before others will find Him ashamed of them (Mark 8:38), and that those who confess Him before others will be confessed before the Father (Matthew 10:32).

They also show the difference between private conviction and public confession. Faith in Scripture is not only inward belief but also outward allegiance. Paul writes that [[Romans 10:10|with the mouth confession is made resulting in salvation]]. True faith eventually shows itself in a willingness to be identified with Jesus.

Yet the example of Joseph and Nicodemus offers hope. Even fearful, secret believers can grow in courage. The cross that seemed to end everything became the moment when they stepped into the open.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to examine whose approval matters most to them. Many Christians feel pressure to hide their faith at work, at school, among friends, or in their families. Fear of rejection, ridicule, or loss can lead to silence. John's words expose the root issue: loving human praise more than God's praise.

They also encourage believers who struggle with fear. Courage often grows step by step. Believers can ask God to help them value His approval above all and to give them boldness to speak and live openly for Christ, with gentleness and respect.

Finally, these verses remind believers that standing for Christ may cost something, but His approval is worth far more than anything the world can offer. The praise of people is temporary; God's "well done" lasts forever.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

John 12 invites believers to wholehearted devotion. Mary poured out her most valuable possession at Jesus' feet, and the whole house was filled with fragrance. Love for Jesus does not calculate the least it can give. Believers are called to offer Him their best, their time, gifts, resources, and lives, trusting that nothing given to Him is wasted, even when others do not understand.

The chapter also teaches the pattern of the grain of wheat. Jesus' death brought life to the world, and His followers are called to a similar pattern of self-giving. Fruitfulness comes not through clinging to life but through giving it away in love and service. The sacrifices made for Christ and for others are seeds that God uses to bring a harvest.

At the center of the chapter is the cross. Jesus was lifted up to draw all people to Himself. The message of His death and resurrection still has power to draw people from every background to God. Believers are called to keep the cross central in their faith and witness, trusting that Jesus Himself does the drawing.

Finally, John 12 warns against the love of human praise. Many rulers believed in Jesus but stayed silent for fear of losing their place. Believers today face similar pressures. The chapter calls them to walk in the light while they have it, to seek God's approval above all, and to grow in courage to confess Christ openly.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the King who came humbly, riding on a donkey, and the grain of wheat who fell into the ground and died to bring life to the world. Thank You for being lifted up on the cross to draw people of every nation to Yourself, and for drawing me. Give me a heart like Mary's, ready to pour out my best for You without counting the cost. Let my life spread the fragrance of Your love wherever I go. Teach me the way of the grain of wheat, so that I stop clinging to my own life and give myself in love and service. When I am tempted to hide my faith because I fear what others will think, forgive me, and help me to love Your praise more than the praise of people. Keep me walking in Your light and believing in You while I have the light. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
