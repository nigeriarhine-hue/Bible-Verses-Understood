# Philippians 3
theme: Knowing Christ and Pressing On
main: Paul warns the Philippians against those who insist on circumcision and confidence in the flesh. He lists his own impressive religious credentials but declares that he counts them all as loss compared with the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus his Lord. He wants to gain Christ and be found in Him, with a righteousness that comes from God through faith, and to know the power of Christ's resurrection and the fellowship of His sufferings. Not yet perfect, Paul forgets what lies behind and presses on toward the goal of God's upward call in Christ. He urges the Philippians to follow his example, warns against those whose minds are set on earthly things, and reminds them that their citizenship is in heaven, from where they await the Savior who will transform their bodies to be like His glorious body.

## Chapter Background and Story

Chapter 3 begins with "Finally," as if Paul were about to end the letter, but then he turns sharply to a warning. Some scholars think Paul received news that prompted this change of tone, or that he simply turned to a concern he had been meaning to address. Either way, the warning is serious.

The danger Paul names is similar to the one he confronted in Galatians. Teachers were traveling among the churches insisting that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses in order to be fully right with God. There is no clear sign that these teachers had already gained influence in Philippi, but Paul wants to protect the church before they do.

Paul responds by telling his own story. He had more reason than anyone to trust in his religious achievements. Yet he had discovered something infinitely better: knowing Christ. This chapter contains some of the most personal and passionate words Paul ever wrote, revealing the heart of his faith.

### A Warning (3:1–3)

[[v1|Finally, my brothers, rejoice in the Lord! To write the same things to you, to me indeed is not tiresome, but for you it is safe]]. Joy in the Lord is a safeguard against false teaching. Those who rejoice in Christ are less likely to look for security elsewhere.

[[v2|Beware of the dogs; beware of the evil workers; beware of the false circumcision]]. Paul's language is sharp. Some Jewish people of the time used "dogs" as an insulting term for Gentiles, whom they regarded as unclean. Paul turns the term around and applies it to the false teachers themselves. "Evil workers" points to their emphasis on works of the Law. "False circumcision" translates a word meaning "mutilation," a biting wordplay on "circumcision." Paul is not attacking the Jewish people in general, whom he loved deeply (Romans 9:1–5), nor Jewish believers in Jesus. He is warning against particular teachers who were distorting the gospel by requiring Gentile believers to be circumcised to be saved.

[[v3|For we are the circumcision, who worship God in the Spirit, and rejoice in Christ Jesus, and have no confidence in the flesh]]. True circumcision is not a physical mark but a heart transformed by God (Deuteronomy 30:6; Romans 2:28–29). Believers worship by the Spirit, boast in Christ, and put no confidence in human achievement.

### Paul's Credentials (3:4–6)

[[v4|though I myself might have confidence even in the flesh. If any other man thinks that he has confidence in the flesh, I yet more]]. Paul lists seven credentials, the first four inherited and the last three chosen.

[[v5|circumcised the eighth day, of the stock of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews; concerning the law, a Pharisee]]. He was circumcised on the eighth day as the Law required (Leviticus 12:3), so he was not a convert. He was born an Israelite. He came from the tribe of Benjamin, which had produced Israel's first king, Saul, after whom Paul was named, and which remained loyal to David's line. He was "a Hebrew of Hebrews," raised in a family that kept its Hebrew language and customs. He was a Pharisee, a member of the strictest party in keeping the Law.

[[v6|concerning zeal, persecuting the assembly; concerning the righteousness which is in the law, found blameless]]. His zeal was so intense that he persecuted the church. By the standards of outward Law-keeping, no one could fault him.

### Counting All as Loss (3:7–11)

Paul's dramatic reassessment of his credentials, and his longing to know Christ, are studied below.

[[v9|and be found in him, not having a righteousness of my own, that which is of the law, but that which is through faith in Christ, the righteousness which is from God by faith]]. This verse summarizes the gospel Paul preached. There are two kinds of righteousness: one that comes from one's own Law-keeping, and one that comes from God as a gift through faith in Christ. Paul had once relied on the first. Now he rejoices in the second.

### Pressing On (3:12–16)

[[v12|Not that I have already obtained, or am already made perfect; but I press on, that I may take hold of that for which also I was taken hold of by Christ Jesus]]. Paul is honest. He has not arrived. He is still growing. But he presses on, because Christ first took hold of him on the road to Damascus. His pursuit of Christ is a response to Christ's pursuit of him. His determination to keep running is studied below.

[[v15|Let us therefore, as many as are perfect, think this way. If in anything you think otherwise, God will also reveal that to you]]. "Perfect" here means mature. Mature believers know they are not yet perfect in the absolute sense. Paul trusts God to correct those who see things differently. [[v16|Nevertheless, to the extent that we have already attained, let’s walk by the same rule. Let’s be of the same mind]]. Believers should live consistently with what they already understand.

### Citizens of Heaven (3:17–21)

[[v17|Brothers, be imitators together of me, and note those who walk this way, even as you have us for an example]]. Paul invites them to follow his example, not because he is perfect, but because he is pressing toward Christ. Mature believers provide models for others.

[[v18|For many walk, of whom I told you often, and now tell you even weeping, as the enemies of the cross of Christ]], [[v19|whose end is destruction, whose god is the belly, and whose glory is in their shame, who think about earthly things]]. Paul now warns against a different danger: people who lived self-indulgently, controlled by their appetites. They may have claimed to be Christians but rejected the way of the cross. Notice that Paul speaks of them with tears. He does not condemn them with satisfaction but grieves over them. Heavenly citizenship and the hope of resurrection are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Philippians 3:7–8 — Counting All Things Loss for Christ

verse: Philippians 3:7-8

#### Simple Explanation

Paul has just listed his impressive religious credentials. Now he makes a stunning declaration. "However, I consider those things that were gain to me as a loss for Christ." Paul uses the language of an accountant. Before meeting Christ, he placed these achievements in the "profit" column. Now he has moved them to the "loss" column.

In verse 8, he goes even further. "I count all things to be a loss for the excellency of the knowledge of Christ Jesus, my Lord." Not just his religious achievements, but everything, all things, is loss compared with the surpassing value of knowing Christ. Notice how personal it is: "Christ Jesus, my Lord."

"For whom I suffered the loss of all things." Paul had literally lost much for Christ: his status as a respected Pharisee, his career, perhaps his family's support, his comfort and safety.

"And count them nothing but refuse." The word translated "refuse" was a strong, even crude term, meaning garbage, dung, or what is thrown to the dogs. Paul uses it deliberately. Compared with Christ, everything else is worthless.

"That I may gain Christ." The goal is not just to give things up but to gain something infinitely better: Christ Himself.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul is not saying that his heritage, education, and devotion were evil in themselves. Being an Israelite was a privilege (Romans 9:4–5). The problem was that he had trusted in these things for his standing before God. When he met Christ, he saw that his own righteousness could never compare with the righteousness God gives through Christ.

Jesus described the kingdom of God as a treasure hidden in a field. A man who found it sold everything he had with joy to buy that field (Matthew 13:44). Paul had found that treasure. Giving up everything was not a grim sacrifice; it was a joyful exchange.

The phrase "knowledge of Christ Jesus, my Lord" refers not just to knowing facts about Christ but knowing Him personally. In the Bible, to "know" someone often means to have an intimate relationship with them. This relationship is what Paul valued above everything.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Every believer has things they might be tempted to trust in or treasure above Christ: achievements, reputation, education, wealth, family background, religious activity, or moral record. This passage invites honest reflection: What am I relying on for my worth and my standing before God?

These things are not necessarily bad. Education, family, and work can be good gifts. But they become dangerous when they replace Christ as the source of identity and security. Paul invites believers to hold everything loosely and to treasure Christ above all.

For those who have lost much, through illness, financial hardship, broken relationships, or persecution, this passage offers comfort. No loss can take away the surpassing worth of knowing Christ. In Him, believers have a treasure that cannot be lost.

### Philippians 3:10–11 — Knowing Christ and the Power of His Resurrection

verse: Philippians 3:10-11

#### Simple Explanation

Paul now describes what he longs for. "That I may know him." Paul had known Christ for many years, yet he still longed to know Him more. The Christian life is a growing relationship.

"And the power of his resurrection." Paul wants to experience the same power that raised Jesus from the dead, power that gives new life, overcomes sin, and sustains believers in hardship.

"And the fellowship of his sufferings." Paul also wants to share in Christ's sufferings. This may seem strange, but Paul understood that following Christ means walking His path, which includes suffering for His sake. Sharing in His sufferings brings a deep fellowship with Him.

"Becoming conformed to his death." Paul wants his life to be shaped like Christ's death: self-giving, humble, and obedient to the Father. This echoes the mind of Christ described in Philippians 2:5–8.

"If by any means I may attain to the resurrection from the dead." Paul's hope is the final resurrection. The words "if by any means" do not express doubt about his salvation but humility and eager longing. He presses on toward that day.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal the pattern of the Christian life: death and resurrection. Just as Christ suffered, died, and rose again, believers share in His sufferings and death, and they will share in His resurrection. Paul expresses the same idea in Romans 8:17: believers are heirs with Christ, if they suffer with Him so that they may also be glorified with Him.

The order here is interesting. Paul mentions resurrection power before sufferings. Believers do not endure suffering in their own strength. The power of Christ's resurrection enables them to share in His sufferings with faith and hope.

This passage does not mean that believers should seek out suffering or that all suffering is noble. Suffering caused by abuse, injustice, or neglect is evil and should be resisted and addressed. Paul's focus is on suffering that comes because of faithfulness to Christ, and on the way God uses all hardships to conform believers to the image of His Son.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

The desire to know Christ more is at the heart of spiritual growth. Believers can nurture this desire through Scripture, prayer, worship, and obedience. Knowing Christ is not a task to be completed but a lifelong journey.

When believers face hardship, these verses offer a different perspective. Suffering is not a sign that God has abandoned them. It can be a place of deep fellowship with Christ, who also suffered. And resurrection power is available to sustain them through it.

Finally, these verses point to hope. Whatever believers endure now, the resurrection is coming. Death and suffering do not have the final word.

### Philippians 3:13–14 — Forgetting What Is Behind, Pressing On

verse: Philippians 3:13-14

#### Simple Explanation

Paul uses the image of a runner in a race. "Brothers, I don’t regard myself as yet having taken hold." He has not yet reached the finish line.

"But one thing I do." Paul is single-minded. His whole life is focused on one goal.

"Forgetting the things which are behind." A runner who keeps looking back will lose speed and may stumble. Paul does not dwell on the past, neither his past achievements, which might make him proud, nor his past failures, such as his persecution of the church, which might weigh him down. This does not mean erasing memory, but refusing to let the past control the present.

"And stretching forward to the things which are before." The image is of a runner straining forward, every muscle stretched toward the finish.

"I press on toward the goal for the prize of the high calling of God in Christ Jesus." The goal is the finish line, and the prize is what awaits there: the fullness of life with Christ. "The high calling" may refer to God's call to come up to receive the prize, as winners in ancient games were called up to the platform to receive their crowns.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses describe the Christian life as a race that requires perseverance and focus. Hebrews 12:1–2 uses the same image, calling believers to run with endurance the race set before them, looking to Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith. The goal is Christ Himself.

"Forgetting the things which are behind" is profoundly freeing. Many believers are held back by past failures, regrets, or wounds. Paul had a past that could have crushed him with guilt: he had persecuted and imprisoned believers. But he had received God's forgiveness, and he refused to let that past define him. Others are held back by past successes, resting on what they once achieved. Paul refused that too.

This does not mean ignoring the past entirely. Remembering God's faithfulness, learning from mistakes, and making amends for harm done are all important. Healing from trauma may require carefully revisiting painful memories with wise help. The point is not to be paralyzed or defined by the past, but to move forward in Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers who feel stuck in guilt over past sins can take hold of God's forgiveness and move forward. Christ's grace covers the past, and the future is open.

Those who are tempted to coast on past spiritual experiences or achievements can hear Paul's call to keep pressing on. Spiritual growth does not stop; there is always more of Christ to know.

Single-mindedness also matters. Many things compete for believers' attention and energy. Paul's "one thing I do" invites believers to examine their priorities and focus on what matters most: knowing and following Christ.

### Philippians 3:20–21 — Our Citizenship Is in Heaven

verse: Philippians 3:20-21

#### Simple Explanation

In contrast to those whose minds are set on earthly things (v19), Paul reminds believers of their true identity. "For our citizenship is in heaven." Philippi was a Roman colony. Its citizens, though living far from Rome, held Roman citizenship, followed Roman laws, and took pride in their Roman identity. Paul says believers are like a colony of heaven on earth. Their true homeland is heaven, and they live by its values.

"From where we also wait for a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ." The citizens of Philippi looked to the Roman emperor, who was often called "savior" and "lord." Believers look to a greater Savior and Lord: Jesus Christ. They wait eagerly for His return.

"Who will change the body of our humiliation to be conformed to the body of his glory." When Christ returns, He will transform believers' bodies. The "body of our humiliation" refers to the present body, weak, subject to sickness, aging, and death. It will be changed to be like Christ's resurrection body, glorious and imperishable (1 Corinthians 15:42–44).

"According to the working by which he is able even to subject all things to himself." The same power by which Christ will bring all things under His rule will be used to transform His people.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses express the hope that sustains believers. Their identity is not defined by their earthly nationality, social status, or circumstances, but by their citizenship in God's kingdom. This gives them both freedom and responsibility. They are free from being controlled by the world's values, and they are called to live as faithful representatives of heaven.

Heavenly citizenship does not mean withdrawing from earthly life. Paul himself used his Roman citizenship when it served the gospel (Acts 16:37; 22:25–29), and he encouraged believers to be good citizens (Romans 13:1–7). But their ultimate loyalty belongs to Christ.

The promise of a transformed body affirms that God cares about the physical body. Christian hope is not escape from the body but its redemption. The resurrection body will be real and physical, yet free from weakness, sin, and death. This hope gives dignity to the present body and comfort to those who suffer from illness, disability, or aging.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can ask whether their lives reflect their heavenly citizenship. Are their priorities, values, and choices shaped by God's kingdom or by the culture around them? Living as citizens of heaven means honoring Christ as King in every area of life.

This passage also offers hope to those who struggle with physical weakness, chronic pain, disability, or the effects of aging. The present body is not the final word. Christ will transform it into a glorious body like His own.

Finally, believers can live with eager expectation. The Savior is coming. That hope can strengthen them to endure hardship, resist temptation, and live faithfully in the meantime.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Philippians 3 calls believers to find their deepest joy, security, and identity in Christ alone. Paul warns against trusting in religious achievements or human credentials for standing before God. He himself had every reason to boast, yet he counted all of it as loss compared with the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus his Lord. True righteousness does not come from human effort but from God through faith in Christ.

This invites honest reflection on what each believer relies on. Achievements, reputation, family background, education, and religious activity can all become substitutes for Christ. Paul calls believers to treasure Christ above all, to long to know Him more, to experience the power of His resurrection, and to share in His sufferings.

Paul also models perseverance. He had not yet arrived, but he pressed on, forgetting what lay behind and straining toward the goal. Believers can let go of past failures, receiving God's forgiveness, and refuse to rest on past successes. The race is not finished, and there is always more of Christ to know.

Finally, the chapter lifts believers' eyes to their true home. Their citizenship is in heaven, and they eagerly wait for their Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who will transform their weak bodies into glorious bodies like His. This hope gives strength to live faithfully now, to endure suffering, and to reject the pull of a life focused only on earthly things. Christ is the treasure, the goal, and the hope of every believer.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, I want to know You. Help me to count everything as loss compared with the surpassing worth of knowing You as my Lord. Forgive me for trusting in my own achievements, reputation, or goodness. Thank You that my righteousness comes not from myself but from God through faith in You. Let me know the power of Your resurrection and the fellowship of Your sufferings. Help me to let go of what lies behind, both my failures and my successes, and to press on toward the goal of Your upward call. Keep my mind from being set on earthly things. Remind me that my citizenship is in heaven, and fill me with eager hope as I wait for Your return, when You will transform my weak body to be like Your glorious body. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
