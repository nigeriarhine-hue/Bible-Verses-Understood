# Matthew 23
theme: Warnings Against Hypocrisy and the Lament Over Jerusalem
main: In His final public teaching, Jesus warns the crowds and His disciples about religious leaders who teach God's law but do not live it, who seek honor, burden others, and focus on small details while neglecting justice, mercy, and faithfulness. He pronounces a series of woes on their hypocrisy, then grieves over Jerusalem with the heart of a mother hen longing to gather her chicks, warning that their house will be left desolate.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 23 is Jesus' last public address in Matthew's Gospel. It comes at the end of a series of confrontations in the temple courts during His final week (Matthew 21–22). The chief priests, elders, Pharisees, Sadducees, and Herodians had all tried to trap Him, and each time He silenced them. Now Jesus turns to the crowds and His disciples and speaks openly about the scribes and Pharisees. After this chapter, He leaves the temple for the last time (Matthew 24:1).

It is important to read this chapter carefully. Jesus' sharp words are aimed at the hypocrisy of particular religious leaders who were opposing Him, not at Jewish people as a whole or even at every Pharisee. Some Pharisees were sincere seekers, such as Nicodemus (John 3) and Joseph of Arimathea, and Paul himself was a Pharisee before following Christ. Christians have sometimes misused this chapter to fuel prejudice against Jewish people, which is a terrible distortion. The warnings here apply to religious hypocrisy wherever it is found, including in the church.

### Practice What They Preach, Not What They Do (23:1–12)

Jesus began, [[v2|The scribes and the Pharisees sit on Moses’ seat]]. In synagogues, a special stone chair was sometimes reserved for the teacher who read and explained the law of Moses. "Sitting on Moses' seat" meant having the role of teaching the law. Jesus told the people to observe what they taught, insofar as it faithfully conveyed God's law, but not to follow their example, because [[v3|they say, and don’t do]].

He gave several examples. They tied up heavy burdens, rules and requirements, and laid them on others without lifting a finger to help. They did their works to be seen. They made their [[v5|phylacteries]] broad and lengthened the tassels on their garments. Phylacteries were small leather boxes containing Scripture passages, worn on the forehead and arm in obedience to Deuteronomy 6:8. Tassels were worn on the corners of garments according to Numbers 15:38–39. Both were meant to remind the wearer of God's commands, but some made them larger to display their devotion. They loved the places of honor at banquets, the best seats in the synagogues, respectful greetings in the marketplaces, and being called "Rabbi," meaning "my great one" or "teacher."

Jesus told His disciples not to seek such titles. They should not be called "Rabbi," because they have one Teacher, the Christ, and they are all brothers. They should call no one on earth "father" in the sense of a spiritual authority who replaces God, because they have one Father in heaven. They should not be called "masters" or "instructors," because they have one Master, the Christ. Jesus was not banning ordinary uses of these words (Paul calls himself a spiritual father in 1 Corinthians 4:15), but warning against titles that elevate some believers over others and take honor that belongs to God.

He concluded with the principle He had taught before: [[v11|But he who is greatest among you will be your servant]]. Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.

### The Seven Woes (23:13–36)

Jesus then pronounced a series of "woes." A woe is a solemn declaration of grief and coming judgment, used often by the Old Testament prophets (Isaiah 5:8–23). It expresses both sorrow and warning. (The WEB places one woe as verse 13 about devouring widows' houses; this verse is not found in many early manuscripts of Matthew and appears in Mark 12:40 and Luke 20:47.)

The first woe concerns shutting the kingdom of heaven in people's faces. The religious leaders did not enter themselves, and by their teaching and opposition to Jesus they kept others from entering. The next concerns their zeal for converts. They would travel over land and sea to make one convert, a Gentile who became a full Jewish proselyte, but then make that person [[v15|twice as much a son of Gehenna]] as themselves, passing on their own legalism and hypocrisy.

Another woe addresses their rules about oaths. They taught that swearing by the temple meant nothing, but swearing by the gold of the temple was binding; swearing by the altar meant nothing, but swearing by the gift on the altar was binding. These distinctions created loopholes for dishonesty. Jesus called them [[v17|blind fools]], pointing out that the temple makes the gold holy and the altar makes the gift holy, and that all oaths are ultimately made before God. This echoes His teaching in Matthew 5:33–37 about simple truthfulness.

The next woe concerns tithing. The law required giving a tenth of the harvest (Leviticus 27:30; Deuteronomy 14:22). The Pharisees tithed even small garden herbs like mint, dill, and cumin, but neglected [[v23|the weightier matters of the law: justice, mercy, and faith]]. Jesus said they should have practiced the latter without neglecting the former. He compared them to people who carefully strain a tiny gnat out of their drink (gnats were unclean under the food laws) but swallow a camel, the largest unclean animal. This woe is studied below.

Two woes contrast outward appearance with inner reality. They cleaned the outside of the cup and dish, but inside they were full of greed and self-indulgence. Jesus told them to clean the inside first, and the outside would also be clean. They were like [[v27|whitened tombs]]. Before Passover, tombs were often painted with whitewash so that pilgrims would not accidentally touch them and become ritually unclean (Numbers 19:16). The tombs looked clean and bright on the outside, but inside they were full of dead bones. In the same way, the leaders appeared righteous outwardly but were full of hypocrisy and lawlessness inside.

The final woe concerns the tombs of the prophets. The leaders built and decorated monuments to prophets whom earlier generations had killed, and they claimed they would never have done such things. But by plotting against Jesus, they were showing that they were truly the descendants of those who killed the prophets. Jesus said, [[v32|Fill up, then, the measure of your fathers]], meaning they would complete their ancestors' sin by rejecting and killing Him. He called them [[v33|You serpents, you offspring of vipers]], the same words John the Baptist had used (Matthew 3:7).

Jesus said He would send prophets, wise men, and teachers, His own messengers, and they would kill some, crucify some, flog some in synagogues, and pursue others from town to town. The book of Acts records such persecution. All the righteous blood shed on earth, from Abel (the first murder victim, Genesis 4) to Zechariah (whose murder in the temple court is recorded in 2 Chronicles 24:20–22, the last book in the traditional order of the Hebrew Bible), would come upon this generation. In other words, the whole history of rejecting God's messengers was reaching its climax in the rejection of His Son. (Matthew calls Zechariah "son of Barachiah," while 2 Chronicles calls him the son of Jehoiada; scholars have offered several explanations, including that Jehoiada was his grandfather or that two figures are being linked.)

### The Lament Over Jerusalem (23:37–39)

The chapter ends not with anger but with grief. Jesus cried out, [[v37|Jerusalem, Jerusalem, who kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to her!]] He longed to gather the city's children together as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, but they were not willing. As a result, [[v38|your house is left to you desolate]]. "Your house" likely refers to the temple, and perhaps to the city as a whole. About forty years later, in A.D. 70, the Romans destroyed the temple and much of Jerusalem.

Jesus concluded that they would not see Him again until they said, [[v39|Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!]], the words of Psalm 118:26 that the crowds had shouted at His entry into Jerusalem. This may point to His return, when He will be recognized as Lord. Paul expresses hope for Israel's future turning to Christ (Romans 11:25–27), and many interpreters see that hope reflected here. These words close Jesus' public ministry with both judgment and a door of hope left open.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 23:11–12 — Greatness Through Humble Service

verse: Matthew 23:11-12

#### Simple Explanation

These verses conclude the first part of Jesus' teaching in this chapter, where He warns the crowds and His disciples about the behavior of the scribes and Pharisees. Jesus has just described how these leaders loved public recognition: wearing enlarged phylacteries and long tassels, taking the best seats at banquets and in synagogues, receiving respectful greetings in public, and being called "Rabbi." Their religion had become a way of gaining honor.

Jesus told His disciples not to seek such titles and recognition, because they have only one Teacher, one Father, and one Master. They are all brothers and sisters, equals before God. Then He gave the principle that should guide them: [[v11|But he who is greatest among you will be your servant]].

This repeats what Jesus taught just days earlier when James and John asked for the best positions in His kingdom (Matthew 20:26–28). Greatness in God's kingdom is measured not by status, titles, or honor, but by humble service to others.

Verse 12 adds a promise and a warning: [[v12|Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted]]. Those who promote themselves and seek honor will eventually be brought low, whether in this life or at the final judgment. Those who humble themselves will be lifted up by God. This principle appears often in Scripture, from Proverbs 29:23 to James 4:10.

The scribes and Pharisees in Jesus' day were respected and honored. His disciples were mostly ordinary people with no status. Jesus was telling them not to envy or imitate the leaders' pursuit of honor, but to follow a different path.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses describe a reversal that runs throughout Scripture. Mary sang that God [[Luke 1:52|has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly]]. Jesus taught that the last will be first and the first last. This reversal reflects God's character: He opposes the proud and gives grace to the humble.

Jesus is the supreme example of this principle. Though He was the Son of God, He humbled Himself to become a servant and died on a cross. Paul writes that because of this, [[Philippians 2:9|God also highly exalted him]]. The path to exaltation in God's kingdom always passes through humility.

These verses also warn against the danger of religious leadership becoming a platform for pride. Those who teach and lead God's people can easily begin to seek honor and recognition. Jesus calls leaders to remember that they are servants and that all believers are equals before God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses call believers to examine their desire for recognition. It is natural to want to be noticed, respected, and honored. But Jesus calls His followers to find their worth in God's approval, not in human praise. Believers can ask whether they serve in order to be seen and whether they feel hurt when their contributions go unrecognized.

They also encourage believers to look for ways to serve others, especially in tasks that are not glamorous or noticed. Serving in humble ways, helping those who cannot return the favor, and putting others' needs first reflect the character of Christ.

Finally, these verses offer encouragement to those who serve faithfully without recognition. God sees and will honor them. Humility is not a loss; it is the path God blesses. Believers can trust that God will lift them up in His time and in His way.

### Matthew 23:23 — Justice, Mercy, and Faithfulness

verse: Matthew 23:23

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is one of the woes Jesus pronounced on the scribes and Pharisees. It focuses on how they practiced the law of tithing. The law of Moses required Israelites to give a tenth of their produce, such as grain, wine, and oil, to support the Levites and the temple (Deuteronomy 14:22–29). The Pharisees applied this command with great care, even tithing small herbs grown in their gardens: [[v23|mint, dill, and cumin]]. They would count out one leaf of every ten to give to God.

Jesus did not criticize their careful tithing in itself. He said they ought to do it. The problem was that they [[v23|have left undone the weightier matters of the law: justice, mercy, and faith]]. "Weightier" means more important, more central. "Justice" means fairness and right treatment of others, especially the poor and powerless. "Mercy" means compassion and kindness, especially toward those in need. "Faith" can also be translated "faithfulness," meaning loyalty to God and trustworthiness in relationships.

These three qualities echo the prophet Micah, who said that God requires His people [[Micah 6:8|to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God]]. The prophets often condemned Israel for keeping religious rituals while neglecting justice and compassion.

Jesus followed this with a vivid image: [[v24|You blind guides, who strain out a gnat, and swallow a camel!]] Strict Pharisees would strain wine through cloth to make sure no tiny gnat, an unclean insect, got into their drink. Yet they were "swallowing" a camel, the largest unclean animal, by neglecting justice and mercy. The exaggeration made the point unforgettable.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that God's commands are not all equal in weight. Some matters are central to God's heart, and others are secondary. Faithful obedience pays attention to small things, but never at the expense of the great things. The center of God's law is love for God and neighbor (Matthew 22:37–40), expressed through justice, mercy, and faithfulness.

It also shows how religious people can lose sight of what matters most. Careful attention to rules can give a sense of righteousness while the heart grows cold toward people. This danger is not limited to the Pharisees; it affects every religious tradition, including Christian churches. People can be precise about doctrine, worship styles, or personal habits while neglecting the poor, treating others unfairly, or being unkind.

At the same time, Jesus' words, [[v23|But you ought to have done these, and not to have left the other undone]], show that He did not dismiss the smaller matters. Faithfulness in small things is good. The problem is when small things replace the weightier ones.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to examine their priorities. Are they careful about the details of religious practice but neglectful of justice, mercy, and faithfulness? Believers can ask whether they treat others fairly in business and work, whether they show compassion to people in need, and whether they are trustworthy in their relationships and commitments.

It also encourages believers to care about justice in their communities. This includes treating employees fairly, paying debts, being honest in dealings, standing up for those who are mistreated, and supporting efforts to help the poor and vulnerable.

Finally, the image of straining out a gnat while swallowing a camel invites believers to notice where they major on minor issues. Churches and individuals can become consumed with secondary matters, arguing over small differences while neglecting love, mercy, and the needs of the world. Keeping the "weightier matters" at the center helps believers keep a right perspective.

### Matthew 23:37 — The Mother Hen's Lament

verse: Matthew 23:37

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes at the very end of Jesus' public teaching, right after His long series of woes against the scribes and Pharisees. Having spoken sharply about their hypocrisy and warned of judgment, Jesus now expresses deep grief over Jerusalem.

[[v37|Jerusalem, Jerusalem]]. The repetition of the name expresses strong emotion, like David's lament over his son: [[2 Samuel 18:33|Absalom, my son, my son]]. Jerusalem was the holy city, the place where God had chosen to put His name and where the temple stood. Yet Jesus describes it as the city [[v37|who kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to her]]. Throughout Israel's history, prophets sent by God had been rejected and sometimes killed in Jerusalem. Jeremiah was threatened with death there; Zechariah was stoned in the temple court (2 Chronicles 24:21). Now the city was about to reject and kill God's own Son.

Then Jesus uses a tender image: [[v37|How often I would have gathered your children together, even as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, and you would not!]] A mother hen spreads her wings over her chicks to protect them from danger, such as a predator or fire, even at the cost of her own life. The Old Testament uses similar language for God's protection: [[Psalm 91:4|He will cover you with his feathers. Under his wings you will take refuge]]. Jesus speaks as God would speak, longing to protect and gather His people.

"How often" suggests that Jesus had repeatedly sought to gather Jerusalem, through His own ministry and through the prophets who came before. But the response was, "you would not." The people were unwilling.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of God toward those who reject Him. Jesus had just pronounced severe judgment, but His words were not those of a harsh judge eager to condemn. They came from a grieving heart that longed to save. God's judgment is real, but it is never His first desire. Through Ezekiel, God said, [[Ezekiel 33:11|I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live]].

The verse also shows the reality of human responsibility. Jesus wanted to gather Jerusalem, "and you would not." God does not force people into His care. Those who refuse His invitation bear responsibility for their choice. The following verse warns that their house would be left desolate, a prediction fulfilled in the destruction of the temple in A.D. 70.

Finally, the image of the mother hen points toward the cross. A hen protects her chicks by covering them with her own body. Jesus would soon give His life to protect and save His people. His willingness to gather them meant His willingness to die for them.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites anyone who has been resisting God to come under His protection. Jesus' longing to gather Jerusalem shows His longing to gather every person. Whatever someone's history of rejecting God, His invitation remains open. The question is whether they will be willing.

It also shapes how believers view people who reject God. Jesus grieved over Jerusalem rather than gloating over its coming judgment. Believers can approach those who reject the faith with compassion and prayer rather than anger or contempt. Even when they must speak hard truths, they can do so with a grieving heart that longs for others to come to God.

Finally, this image offers comfort to believers. Those who come to Jesus find shelter under His wings. In times of danger, fear, or uncertainty, believers can rest in His protection, trusting that the One who gave His life for them will keep them safe in His care.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 23 is a sobering chapter that challenges anyone involved in religious life. Jesus' warnings were aimed at leaders who taught God's law but did not live it, who sought honor and recognition, who burdened others without helping them, and who focused on small rules while neglecting justice, mercy, and faithfulness. These temptations are present in every generation and every church. Believers, and especially those who teach or lead, can examine themselves honestly in light of Jesus' words.

The chapter calls for integrity, the match between what one says and what one does. Hypocrisy is dangerous because it deceives others and the hypocrite himself. Believers can ask God to show them where their outward life does not match their inner reality and to cleanse them from the inside out. Being the same person in private as in public is a mark of genuine faith.

The chapter also calls for humility and service. The desire for titles, recognition, and the best seats is common, but Jesus teaches that greatness comes through serving others. Churches can reflect this by treating all believers as brothers and sisters, refusing to build hierarchies of prestige, and honoring humble service.

Jesus' emphasis on justice, mercy, and faithfulness reminds believers of what matters most to God. Careful religious practices are good, but they must never replace fairness, compassion, and trustworthiness. Finally, the lament over Jerusalem shows that God's warnings come from a heart of love. Jesus longs to gather people under His protection. Believers can respond to His invitation, find shelter in Him, and share His compassion with those who are still resisting.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You see past outward appearances to the heart. Forgive me for the times I have said one thing and done another, sought recognition for my service, or placed burdens on others without helping them. Cleanse me from the inside out, so that my private life matches what I profess in public.

Teach me to serve humbly rather than seek honor, remembering that I have one Teacher, one Father, and one Master. Help me not to strain out gnats while swallowing camels, but to give my attention to justice, mercy, and faithfulness. Thank You for Your heart that grieves over those who reject You and longs to gather them like a mother hen gathers her chicks. Gather me under Your wings, and give me Your compassion for those who are still far from You. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
