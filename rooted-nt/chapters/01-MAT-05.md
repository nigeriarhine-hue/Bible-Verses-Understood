# Matthew 5
theme: The Blessed Life and the Deeper Righteousness of the Kingdom
main: In the opening of the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus describes the kind of people who are blessed in God's kingdom and calls His followers to be salt and light in the world. He explains that He came to fulfill the Law, and He shows that God's will reaches beyond outward actions to the heart, calling His disciples to a righteousness marked by reconciliation, purity, faithfulness, truthfulness, and love even for enemies.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 5–7 is known as the Sermon on the Mount, the longest single block of Jesus' teaching in the Gospels. At the end of chapter 4, Matthew described large crowds following Jesus from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, and beyond the Jordan, drawn by His teaching and healing. Now Jesus, seeing those crowds, goes up onto a mountainside, sits down (the normal posture for a Jewish teacher), and His disciples gather around Him. The crowds are listening too (Matthew 7:28), but the sermon is addressed first to those who have begun to follow Him. It describes the life of people who belong to God's kingdom.

Many readers have noticed the parallel with Moses. Just as Moses went up Mount Sinai and brought God's law to Israel, Jesus goes up a mountain and teaches what God's will means for His people. But Jesus does not speak as Moses did, relaying God's words. He speaks with His own authority: "You have heard that it was said... but I tell you." Matthew wants readers to see Jesus as the one greater than Moses, who reveals the true meaning of God's law.

### The Beatitudes (5:1–12)

Jesus begins with a series of statements each starting with "Blessed are." These are called the Beatitudes, from the Latin word for "blessed." The word "blessed" does not mean simply "happy" in a passing emotional sense. It describes people who are in a good and enviable position because God's favor rests on them. Jesus is not giving a list of requirements to earn blessing; He is announcing who is truly well off in God's kingdom, often turning the world's values upside down.

He begins with [[v3|the poor in spirit]], those who recognize their spiritual poverty and dependence on God. He continues with those who mourn (over sin and the brokenness of the world), the gentle (humble people who do not grasp for power), those who hunger and thirst for righteousness (who long for God's will to be done in their lives and in the world), the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers, and those persecuted for righteousness. Each statement includes a promise: comfort, inheriting the earth, being filled, receiving mercy, seeing God, being called God's children. The first and eighth beatitudes share the same promise, [[v3|theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven]], framing the list.

Many of these phrases echo the Old Testament. "The gentle shall inherit the earth" comes from Psalm 37:11. The comfort for those who mourn recalls Isaiah 61:1–3, where the Lord's anointed one is sent to comfort all who mourn and proclaim good news to the poor. Jesus is announcing that the promises of the prophets are being fulfilled in His ministry.

The final beatitude shifts from "those who" to "you." Jesus tells His disciples that they will be insulted and persecuted because of Him, and that they should rejoice because their reward in heaven is great, just as the prophets before them were persecuted. He is preparing them for the reality that following Him will bring opposition.

### Salt and Light (5:13–16)

Jesus then tells His disciples, [[v13|You are the salt of the earth]] and [[v14|You are the light of the world]]. In the ancient world, salt was used to flavor food and to preserve it from decay. Salt in Palestine was often taken from the Dead Sea area mixed with other minerals; if the sodium chloride dissolved away, what remained was tasteless and useless. Jesus warns that disciples who lose their distinctive character are like salt that has lost its flavor.

Light, similarly, exists to be seen. A city on a hill cannot be hidden, and nobody lights an oil lamp and puts it under a basket. Disciples are to let their light shine so that others see their good works and glorify their Father in heaven. Israel had been called to be a light to the nations (Isaiah 42:6; 49:6). Now Jesus gives this calling to His followers.

### Jesus and the Law (5:17–20)

Jesus then addresses a question His listeners may have had. His authoritative teaching and His conflicts with religious leaders might suggest that He was setting aside the Scriptures. He says plainly, [[v17|Don’t think that I came to destroy the law or the prophets. I didn’t come to destroy, but to fulfill]]. "The law and the prophets" was a way of referring to the whole Old Testament.

He insists that not even [[v18|one smallest letter or one tiny pen stroke]] will pass from the law until everything is accomplished. The "smallest letter" refers to the Hebrew letter yod, the smallest in the alphabet, and the "pen stroke" to the tiny marks that distinguish some Hebrew letters from others. Jesus takes the Scriptures with complete seriousness.

He then says something that would have astonished His hearers: unless their righteousness exceeded that of the scribes and Pharisees, they would never enter the kingdom. The Pharisees were known for their careful observance of the law. Jesus is not calling for more rules but for a deeper righteousness, one that reaches the heart and reflects the true intention of God's commands. The rest of the chapter shows what that looks like.

### Six Illustrations of Deeper Righteousness (5:21–48)

Jesus gives six examples, each following the pattern "You have heard that it was said... but I tell you." In each case He addresses a commandment or a common interpretation of it, and then shows God's deeper purpose.

**Anger and reconciliation (5:21–26).** The command [[Exodus 20:13|You shall not murder]] forbids taking life. Jesus says that anger, contempt, and insulting words are also deadly in God's sight. "Raca" was an Aramaic insult meaning something like "empty-head." "Gehenna" was the name of the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, once associated with child sacrifice and later used as a symbol of final judgment. Jesus then gives two practical examples: if you are bringing an offering to the altar and remember that someone has something against you, leave the gift and go be reconciled first; and settle disputes quickly before they reach court. Relationships with others matter so much that they take priority over religious ritual.

**Lust and purity (5:27–30).** The command [[Exodus 20:14|You shall not commit adultery]] forbids sexual unfaithfulness. Jesus says that looking at someone with the intention of lusting after them is already adultery in the heart. He then uses vivid exaggeration: if your right eye or right hand causes you to sin, tear it out or cut it off. Jesus is not commanding literal self-harm. He is using a well-known teaching method of overstatement to show how seriously sin must be dealt with. Whatever leads us into sin should be removed decisively.

**Divorce (5:31–32).** Deuteronomy 24:1–4 regulated divorce by requiring a man to give his wife a certificate of divorce, which protected her by allowing her to remarry. In Jesus' day, some teachers interpreted the grounds for divorce very broadly, allowing a man to divorce his wife for almost any reason. Jesus says that divorcing a wife, except for sexual immorality, makes her an adulteress (since she would likely be forced to remarry for survival), and whoever marries her commits adultery. His point is that marriage is a lifelong covenant, not something to be discarded casually, and that the casual divorce of His day harmed women in particular. Christians have differed on how to apply this teaching, including whether other grounds such as abandonment (1 Corinthians 7:15) or abuse may justify separation or divorce. The passage itself focuses on protecting the permanence of marriage against easy divorce; it should not be used to trap someone in a situation of danger or abuse. Jesus returns to this subject in more detail in Matthew 19.

**Oaths and truthfulness (5:33–37).** The law required that vows made to the Lord be kept (Numbers 30:2). In Jesus' day, people had developed systems of oaths, swearing by heaven, by earth, by Jerusalem, or by one's own head, with some oaths considered more binding than others. This created loopholes for dishonesty. Jesus says not to swear at all in this manipulative way, but to let "yes" mean yes and "no" mean no. Truthfulness should be so consistent that oaths become unnecessary. (Many Christians understand this as forbidding casual or evasive oaths rather than formal oaths in a court of law; others, such as Quakers and Mennonites, have historically refused all oaths.)

**Retaliation (5:38–42).** [[Matthew 5:38|An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth]] (see Exodus 21:24) was a principle for courts, meant to limit punishment so that it fit the crime rather than escalating into revenge. Some had turned it into a justification for personal retaliation. Jesus tells His disciples not to retaliate against an evil person. He gives examples: turning the other cheek when struck on the right cheek (a backhanded slap, which was an insult rather than a violent assault), giving your cloak as well as your coat when sued, and going a second mile when compelled to go one (Roman soldiers could legally force civilians to carry their gear for one mile). Jesus is teaching His followers to refuse revenge and to respond to insult and imposition with generous, surprising goodness. This teaching is about personal retaliation, not about whether governments should maintain justice (see Romans 13:1–4), and it does not require a person to remain in an abusive situation or to refuse protection. It calls disciples to break the cycle of revenge.

**Love for enemies (5:43–48).** The law commanded, [[Leviticus 19:18|You shall love your neighbor as yourself]]. The words "and hate your enemy" are not in the Old Testament, but some people had concluded that if you must love your neighbor, you may hate those who are not your neighbor. Jesus says, [[v44|love your enemies]] and pray for those who persecute you. This reflects the character of God, who sends sun and rain on both the good and the evil. Jesus concludes, [[v48|Therefore you shall be perfect, just as your Father in heaven is perfect]]. The word "perfect" means complete, whole, or mature. In context, it refers especially to love that is complete, not partial, extending even to enemies as God's love does.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 5:3 — Blessed Are the Poor in Spirit

verse: Matthew 5:3

#### Simple Explanation

This is the first statement of the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus has gone up the mountainside, sat down to teach, and begun to speak to His disciples while the crowds listen. Many in the crowd were poor, sick, or burdened, people who had followed Him because He healed and taught with compassion (Matthew 4:23–25). Jesus begins His teaching by telling them who is truly blessed.

"Blessed" describes a state of well-being that comes from God's favor. Jesus is not describing a feeling but a reality: these people are in a good position before God, even if they do not appear to be by worldly standards.

"Poor in spirit" refers to people who recognize their spiritual need. In the Old Testament, "the poor" often referred to humble, faithful people who were oppressed and depended entirely on God for help (for example, Psalm 34:6 and Isaiah 66:2). Being poor in spirit means acknowledging that we have nothing to offer God on our own merit. It is the opposite of spiritual pride and self-sufficiency. Luke's version of this beatitude says simply [[Luke 6:20|Blessed are you who are poor]], showing that Jesus cared about the materially poor as well. Matthew emphasizes the inner attitude of humble dependence.

The promise is that [[v3|theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven]]. The kingdom does not belong to those who think they deserve it, but to those who know they do not. Notice that this promise is in the present tense: the kingdom already belongs to the poor in spirit.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This beatitude stands at the head of the list because it describes the doorway into the kingdom. Every other quality in the Beatitudes grows from this recognition of need. A person cannot mourn over sin, hunger for righteousness, or show mercy without first knowing their own poverty before God.

This truth runs throughout the Bible. God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble (James 4:6; Proverbs 3:34). Jesus later tells a parable about a Pharisee who boasted of his goodness and a tax collector who simply prayed, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner," and says it was the tax collector who went home justified (Luke 18:9–14). Salvation is received, not achieved.

This beatitude also turns the world's values upside down. The world tends to honor the strong, the confident, and the self-sufficient. Jesus says that the kingdom belongs to those who know they are weak and needy. This is not a call to poor self-esteem but to honest humility before God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This beatitude invites believers to come to God with empty hands. Prayer, worship, and confession begin not with what we bring to God but with what we need from Him. Recognizing our dependence on God is not weakness; it is the beginning of blessing.

It also guards against spiritual pride. Believers who grow in knowledge and service can slowly begin to think of themselves as spiritually rich and look down on others. Remembering that every blessing in the kingdom is a gift keeps believers humble and gracious toward others who are struggling.

Finally, those who feel spiritually poor, who struggle with doubt, weakness, or a sense of failure, can take comfort here. Jesus does not begin by blessing the spiritually accomplished. He begins with those who know they need Him. That need is not a barrier to the kingdom; it is the way in.

### Matthew 5:16 — Let Your Light Shine

verse: Matthew 5:16

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes Jesus' short teaching about salt and light. He has just told His disciples, [[v14|You are the light of the world]], and He has used two images: a city built on a hill, whose lights can be seen from far away at night, and a household oil lamp, which is placed on a stand so it can light the whole room rather than hidden under a measuring basket. In a small one-room house of that time, a single lamp on a stand could provide light for everyone.

Now Jesus applies the images: [[v16|let your light shine before men]]. The disciples' light is their "good works," their visible acts of goodness, faithfulness, and love. These are not meant to be hidden. The purpose, however, is crucial: [[v16|that they may see your good works and glorify your Father who is in heaven]]. The goal is not that people will admire the disciples but that they will give glory to God.

This verse comes right after the Beatitudes, which describe the inner character of kingdom people, and just before the passages showing what that character looks like in action. It also comes right after Jesus warns that disciples will be persecuted. Jesus does not tell His followers to withdraw from the world to avoid opposition. They are to remain visible, showing God's goodness through their lives.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Light is a major biblical image for God's presence, truth, and salvation. God created light at the beginning (Genesis 1:3). Israel was called to be a light to the nations, displaying God's character to the world (Isaiah 49:6). Jesus Himself is described as the light of the world (John 8:12), and in Matthew 4:16 He is the great light dawning on those in darkness. His followers reflect His light, as the moon reflects the sun.

There seems to be a tension between this verse and Matthew 6:1, where Jesus warns against doing good deeds to be seen by others. The difference lies in motive and purpose. In Matthew 6, the problem is seeking praise for oneself. Here, the purpose is that God would be glorified. Believers are not to hide their faith or their good works, but neither should they perform them for applause.

The verse also shows that good works have a witnessing function. Peter makes the same point in 1 Peter 2:12, urging believers to live honorably among unbelievers so that, seeing their good deeds, they may glorify God. The credibility of the gospel is often connected to the lives of those who proclaim it.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to live visibly good lives in ordinary settings: at work, at home, in neighborhoods, and online. Honesty, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, and care for those in need are light that others can see. Believers do not need a special platform to shine; they need to live consistently where God has placed them.

It also calls for examining motives. Good works done to build a reputation or gain approval point attention to ourselves. Good works done in love and gratitude to God point others to Him. Believers can ask God to purify their motives and to keep them from seeking credit.

Finally, the image of a lamp under a basket warns against hiding faith out of fear or embarrassment. Believers need wisdom about when and how to speak about their faith, but they should not conceal who they are. When people notice something different and ask about it, believers can gently point them to the Father who is the source of all goodness.

### Matthew 5:17 — Jesus Came to Fulfill the Law

verse: Matthew 5:17

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words right after describing His disciples as salt and light, and right before He begins to explain the deeper meaning of the commandments. His listeners may have wondered whether Jesus' new teaching was meant to replace the Scriptures they knew. Some religious leaders were already suspicious of Him. Jesus addresses the issue directly: [[v17|Don’t think that I came to destroy the law or the prophets]].

"The law or the prophets" refers to the Hebrew Scriptures, what Christians call the Old Testament. The "law" (in Hebrew, Torah) refers especially to the first five books, including the commandments given through Moses. The "prophets" includes the historical and prophetic books. Together they represent all of God's revelation to Israel up to that time.

Jesus says, [[v17|I didn’t come to destroy, but to fulfill]]. To "fulfill" means more than simply to obey. It means to bring to completion, to accomplish the purpose, and to reveal the full meaning. Jesus fulfills the prophets by being the one they pointed to. He fulfills the law by perfectly obeying it, by revealing its deepest intention (as He does in the rest of this chapter), and by accomplishing what the sacrifices and rituals of the law anticipated.

In the following verses, Jesus affirms that the law will not pass away until everything is accomplished, and He warns against teaching others to break even the least commandment. He also says His disciples' righteousness must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees, not by keeping more rules, but by obeying from the heart.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is important for understanding how Christians relate to the Old Testament. Jesus does not discard God's earlier revelation. He completes it. The whole Old Testament story, with its covenants, laws, sacrifices, and prophecies, points to Him. After His resurrection, Jesus will explain to His disciples how everything written about Him in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms had to be fulfilled (Luke 24:44).

Christians have long discussed how the various parts of the law apply after Christ's coming. The New Testament teaches that some aspects of the law, such as the sacrificial system, food laws, and rituals that set Israel apart, have been fulfilled in Christ and are no longer binding in the same way (see Hebrews 10:1–14; Mark 7:19; Acts 10). The moral heart of the law, such as love for God and neighbor, faithfulness, truthfulness, and justice, continues to express God's will and is deepened by Jesus' teaching. Christians in different traditions explain this relationship in somewhat different ways, but all agree that Jesus is the key to understanding the law.

This verse also shows that grace does not mean God's moral standards have been lowered. Jesus calls His disciples to a higher righteousness than the Pharisees, one that reaches the heart. That righteousness is impossible by human effort alone; it is the fruit of a new heart given by God's Spirit, as the prophets promised (Jeremiah 31:33; Ezekiel 36:26–27).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to value the whole Bible, including the Old Testament. Its stories, laws, psalms, and prophecies are not outdated; they reveal God's character and point to Christ. Reading them in light of Jesus gives them their full meaning and enriches faith.

It also calls believers to take God's moral will seriously. Some people assume that because salvation is by grace, obedience does not matter much. Jesus teaches the opposite: those who belong to His kingdom will seek to do God's will from the heart. Grace frees believers not to ignore God's commands but to obey them out of love.

Finally, Jesus' approach guards against legalism. The scribes and Pharisees focused on external rules and sometimes created loopholes. Jesus looks at the heart. Believers can ask not only "What am I allowed to do?" but "What does God desire, and what reflects His love?" That question leads to deeper and more joyful obedience.

### Matthew 5:44 — Love Your Enemies

verse: Matthew 5:44

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the final and most demanding of the six illustrations in which Jesus contrasts what His listeners had heard with what He teaches. In verse 43 He quotes a common saying: [[v43|You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy]]. The first half comes from Leviticus 19:18. The second half is not in Scripture; it was a conclusion some people drew, assuming that if they were commanded to love their neighbor (understood as a fellow Israelite or a member of their own group), they were free to hate outsiders and opponents.

Jesus responds with four commands: love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat and persecute you. (Some of these phrases are not found in all ancient manuscripts of Matthew but appear in Luke 6:27–28.) Each command describes an active response. Love here is not merely a feeling of affection but a commitment to seek another person's good. Jesus asks His disciples to bless rather than curse, to do good rather than harm, and to pray rather than plot revenge.

His listeners lived under Roman occupation. They knew enemies who taxed them, insulted them, and sometimes brutalized them. Some groups of the time hoped to drive out the Romans by force. Jesus' teaching was radical in that setting. He also knew His disciples would face persecution because of Him (vv. 10–12), and He was preparing them to respond as children of God.

In the next verse, Jesus gives the reason: to be [[v45|children of your Father who is in heaven]], who sends sun and rain on both the evil and the good. God shows kindness even to those who reject Him, and His children are to reflect His character.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Love for enemies is one of the most distinctive features of Jesus' teaching, and He lived it out Himself. On the cross He prayed for those who crucified Him: [[Luke 23:34|Father, forgive them, for they don’t know what they are doing]]. Paul writes that God showed His love for us [[Romans 5:8|while we were yet sinners]], and that we were reconciled to God while we were His enemies (Romans 5:10). The gospel itself is the story of God loving His enemies.

This teaching reveals that love for enemies is not natural human behavior. Jesus says that even tax collectors, who were despised as collaborators, love those who love them. Loving enemies requires a love that comes from God. It reflects the character of the Father and is possible only through His grace.

It is important to understand what this command does not mean. Loving an enemy does not mean approving of their wrongdoing, pretending harm did not happen, or remaining in a situation where we are endangered. It does not cancel the role of governments and courts in restraining evil and pursuing justice. Love may include setting boundaries, seeking legal protection, or confronting wrongdoing. What it rules out is hatred, revenge, and the desire to see an enemy destroyed. It calls believers to want the enemy's ultimate good, including their repentance and reconciliation with God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to identify the people they consider enemies or opponents: those who have hurt them, insulted them, opposed them, or simply disagree with them deeply. Jesus asks His followers to respond to such people not with hatred but with active goodwill. That may begin with refusing to speak contemptuously about them, refusing to wish them harm, and choosing to treat them with basic respect.

Prayer is one of the most practical ways to obey this command. Praying sincerely for someone who has wronged us, asking God to bless them and bring them to good, gradually changes our own hearts. It is difficult to keep hating someone we are honestly praying for.

Finally, loving enemies takes wisdom. In some situations, love means maintaining distance and boundaries for safety, while refusing bitterness. In others, it means acts of kindness that surprise and disarm. In a world full of division, especially political and social hostility, followers of Jesus can show a different way: firm in truth, yet refusing to dehumanize or hate those on the other side.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 5 presents a picture of life in God's kingdom that is both beautiful and challenging. The Beatitudes show that God's blessing rests on those the world often overlooks: the humble, the grieving, the gentle, the merciful, the peacemakers, and the persecuted. Believers can measure their lives not by the world's standards of success but by these qualities, asking God to make them more poor in spirit, more hungry for righteousness, and more merciful.

The calling to be salt and light reminds believers that their faith is not private. It is meant to affect the world around them, preserving what is good, resisting decay, and showing God's goodness in practical ways. This happens through ordinary faithfulness: honesty at work, kindness in the family, fairness in business, and care for neighbors.

The six illustrations show that God cares about the heart. Avoiding murder is not enough if we harbor contempt. Avoiding adultery is not enough if lust controls our thoughts. Keeping technical promises is not enough if we are habitually dishonest. Jesus calls His followers to examine their anger, their desires, their commitments, their speech, their responses to mistreatment, and their attitudes toward enemies. In each area He offers a better way: seeking reconciliation quickly, dealing decisively with sources of temptation, honoring the covenant of marriage, speaking plainly and truthfully, refusing revenge, and actively loving those who oppose us.

No one can live this way perfectly by willpower. The Sermon on the Mount shows the depth of our need for grace, which brings readers back to the first beatitude: blessed are the poor in spirit. Jesus came to fulfill the law, and through Him believers receive both forgiveness for falling short and the Spirit who changes hearts. The right response is to come to Him humbly, to ask for His help, and to take concrete steps of obedience in the areas where His words have touched us most directly.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for describing so clearly the life of Your kingdom, and for blessing people who are poor in spirit, who mourn, and who hunger for righteousness. I confess that I often value what the world values. Make me humble and dependent on You, merciful toward others, and pure in heart.

Help me to be salt and light where You have placed me, so that people may see goodness in my life and glorify my Father in heaven, not me. Search my heart for hidden anger, lust, dishonesty, and the desire for revenge. Give me courage to seek reconciliation quickly, to keep my word, and to answer insult with grace. Teach me to love my enemies and pray for those who hurt me, with wisdom to set right boundaries and without bitterness. Thank You that You fulfilled the law perfectly for me; fill me with Your Spirit so that I may obey from the heart. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
