# 1 Peter 3
theme: Blessing Instead of Insult, Hope Ready to Explain
main: Peter instructs wives to submit to their own husbands so that unbelieving husbands may be won over by their pure and reverent conduct, and to cultivate the lasting beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit rather than relying on outward adornment, following the example of holy women like Sarah, doing good without giving way to fear. Husbands are to live with their wives in an understanding way, showing them honor as fellow heirs of the gracious gift of life, so that nothing hinders their prayers. All believers are to be like-minded, sympathetic, loving, compassionate, and humble, not repaying evil for evil or insult for insult, but blessing, because they were called to inherit a blessing, as Psalm 34 teaches. Even if they suffer for doing right, they are blessed and need not fear. They are to honor Christ as Lord in their hearts, always ready to give a reason for their hope with gentleness and respect, keeping a clear conscience so that slanderers are put to shame. It is better to suffer for doing good than for doing evil, for Christ suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring us to God, being put to death in the flesh but made alive in the Spirit. Peter speaks of Christ proclaiming to the spirits in prison, of Noah's family saved through water as a picture of baptism, which saves through the resurrection of Jesus Christ, who is now at God's right hand with all powers subject to Him.

## Chapter Background and Story

First Peter 3 continues the section on how believers should live within the social structures of their world (2:13–3:7). Peter has addressed relationships with governing authorities and with masters. Now he addresses wives and husbands. He then broadens his instructions to all believers, especially regarding how to respond to hostility and suffering.

Peter's original readers lived in a world where a wife was expected to share her husband's religion. A woman who became a Christian while her husband remained a pagan was in a difficult and sometimes dangerous position. Her faith might be seen as rebellion against her husband and against social order. Peter's advice is aimed at helping such women live faithfully and wisely, in a way that might win their husbands to Christ.

The chapter ends with one of the most difficult passages in the New Testament (vv18–22), about Christ preaching to "the spirits in prison." Interpreters have offered several explanations, which are presented below. The main point is clear: Christ's suffering led to victory, and believers who suffer with Him share in that victory.

### Wives and Husbands (3:1–7)

[[v1|In the same way, wives, be in subjection to your own husbands, so that, even if any don’t obey the Word, they may be won by the behavior of their wives without a word]], [[v2|seeing your pure behavior in fear]]. Peter has in mind especially wives whose husbands did not believe. Rather than trying to argue them into faith, these wives could win them through godly lives. "Without a word" does not mean the wives could never speak of their faith, but that their conduct would speak powerfully.

[[v3|Let your beauty come not from the outward adorning of braiding your hair, and of wearing gold ornaments or of putting on fine clothing]], [[v4|but from the hidden person of the heart, in the incorruptible adornment of a gentle and quiet spirit, which is very precious in God’s sight]]. In the Roman world, elaborate hairstyles and jewelry were marks of wealth and status (see 1 Timothy 2:9–10). Peter is not forbidding all care for appearance, but redirecting attention to inner beauty, which never fades and which God treasures. "A gentle and quiet spirit" describes a calm, trusting heart, not a weak personality. It is a quality Jesus Himself displayed (Matthew 11:29), and it is fitting for every believer.

[[v5|For this is how in the past the holy women who hoped in God also adorned themselves, being in subjection to their own husbands]]. [[v6|So Sarah obeyed Abraham, calling him lord, whose children you now are if you do well and are not put in fear by any terror]]. Sarah is presented as an example of a woman who hoped in God. Notice the closing phrase: "not put in fear by any terror." Peter acknowledges that these women might face intimidation, and he encourages them not to be ruled by fear.

Christians understand the instructions to wives in different ways, as they do the similar passages in Ephesians 5 and Colossians 3. Some see a lasting pattern of a wife's voluntary respect for her husband's loving leadership. Others see Peter applying the gospel within first-century household structures, with the lasting principles being faithfulness, respect, inner beauty, and courage. Both views agree on several crucial points. A wife's submission is [[1 Peter 2:13|for the Lord’s sake]] and never requires her to sin. It never requires her to accept abuse. Peter himself recognizes that wives might face "terror" and tells them not to be ruled by fear. A wife facing abuse should seek safety and help, and the church must protect and support her rather than urging her to remain in danger.

[[v7|You husbands, in the same way, live with your wives according to knowledge, giving honor to the woman as to the weaker vessel, as also being joint heirs of the grace of life, that your prayers may not be hindered]]. The instructions to husbands are studied below.

### Blessing Instead of Retaliation (3:8–12)

The call to unity and blessing is studied below. [[v10|For, “He who would love life and see good days, let him keep his tongue from evil and his lips from speaking deceit.]] [[v11|Let him turn away from evil and do good. Let him seek peace and pursue it.]] [[v12|For the eyes of the Lord are on the righteous, and his ears open to their prayer; but the face of the Lord is against those who do evil.”]] Peter quotes Psalm 34:12–16, a psalm David wrote after escaping danger. A good life involves controlling the tongue, turning from evil, doing good, and actively pursuing peace. God watches over the righteous and hears their prayers.

### Suffering for Doing Good (3:13–17)

[[v13|Now who will harm you if you become imitators of that which is good?]] Generally, those who do good are less likely to be harmed. [[v14|But even if you should suffer for righteousness’ sake, you are blessed. “Don’t fear what they fear, neither be troubled.”]] Even if they do suffer, they are blessed. Jesus said, [[Matthew 5:10|Blessed are those who have been persecuted for righteousness' sake]]. Peter adapts Isaiah 8:12: believers need not share the fears of those around them. The call to be ready to explain their hope is studied below.

[[v17|For it is better, if it is God’s will, that you suffer for doing what is right than for doing evil]]. Suffering for wrongdoing is deserved; suffering for doing good shares in Christ's experience and is commendable to God.

### Christ's Suffering and Victory (3:18–22)

Christ's once-for-all suffering is studied below.

[[v19|in whom he also went and preached to the spirits in prison]], [[v20|who before were disobedient when God waited patiently in the days of Noah while the ship was being built. In it, few, that is, eight souls, were saved through water]]. These verses have puzzled readers throughout church history. There are three main interpretations. Some believe that between His death and resurrection, Christ went to the realm of the dead and proclaimed His victory, either announcing judgment or offering the gospel, to the spirits of those who died in Noah's time. Others believe that the pre-incarnate Christ, through the Spirit, preached through Noah to the people of Noah's day, who are now "spirits in prison." Still others, noting that "spirits" in the New Testament often refers to angelic beings, believe that the risen Christ proclaimed His victory to fallen angels, connected in Jewish tradition with the days of Noah (Genesis 6:1–4; 2 Peter 2:4–5; Jude 1:6). Whichever view is correct, the main point is that Christ's suffering led to triumph over every hostile power.

[[v21|This is a symbol of baptism, which now saves you—not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God—through the resurrection of Jesus Christ]]. Noah's family passed through the waters of the flood and were brought safely to a new world. Baptism is a picture of this: passing through water into new life. Peter clarifies that baptism does not save by physically washing the body. What saves is the reality baptism signifies: a pledge or appeal to God from a good conscience, made possible "through the resurrection of Jesus Christ." Christians understand the role of baptism in salvation differently, but all agree that its power comes from Christ's death and resurrection, received by faith.

[[v22|who is at the right hand of God, having gone into heaven, angels and authorities and powers being made subject to him]]. Christ has ascended and reigns over every spiritual power. Believers who suffer can take courage: their Lord is victorious.

## Important Verses Explained

### 1 Peter 3:7 — Husbands, Honor Your Wives

verse: 1 Peter 3:7

#### Simple Explanation

"You husbands, in the same way, live with your wives according to knowledge." Husbands are to live with their wives in an understanding way, knowing them, considering their needs, perspectives, and feelings, and treating them wisely and thoughtfully.

"Giving honor to the woman." Husbands are to show their wives honor, respect, and value. In a culture where women were often treated as inferior, this was a significant command.

"As to the weaker vessel." This phrase likely refers to physical strength, since women are generally physically weaker than men, and perhaps to women's more vulnerable social and legal position in the ancient world. It does not mean women are morally, intellectually, or spiritually weaker. The point is that husbands must never take advantage of greater physical or social power, but use it to protect and honor their wives.

"As also being joint heirs of the grace of life." Husbands and wives are equal heirs of God's grace and eternal life. Spiritually, they stand side by side before God.

"That your prayers may not be hindered." A husband who mistreats his wife damages his relationship with God. His prayers will be hindered.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse balances the instructions to wives. While wives are called to respect their husbands, husbands are called to understand and honor their wives. Both are accountable to God for how they treat each other.

The phrase "joint heirs of the grace of life" is a powerful affirmation of equality. In God's eyes, husband and wife are equally His children and equally heirs of His promises (Galatians 3:28). This truth transforms how a husband should treat his wife: not as a possession or subordinate, but as a fellow heir of grace.

The warning that prayers will be hindered shows how seriously God takes the treatment of wives. A man cannot mistreat his wife and expect to have a healthy relationship with God. Malachi 2:13–16 also connects God's refusal to accept offerings with men's unfaithfulness to their wives.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Husbands can ask whether they truly understand their wives: listening to them, considering their needs, and seeking to know their hearts. Understanding requires time, attention, and humility.

Husbands can show honor through words of appreciation, respect for their wives' opinions and gifts, faithfulness, and protection. Any form of control, harshness, or abuse is completely contrary to this verse and dishonors God.

Married couples can remember that they are joint heirs of grace, equally loved by God. Praying together, serving together, and growing together in faith reflects this shared inheritance.

### 1 Peter 3:8–9 — Blessing Instead of Insult

verse: 1 Peter 3:8-9

#### Simple Explanation

Peter now addresses all believers. "Finally, all of you be like-minded." Believers should share a common purpose and commitment to Christ.

"Compassionate": sharing in others' feelings, rejoicing with those who rejoice and weeping with those who weep.

"Loving as brothers": showing the affection of family members.

"Tenderhearted": having a heart of mercy and kindness.

"Courteous": or humble-minded, not proud or arrogant.

Verse 9: "not rendering evil for evil or insult for insult." When believers are wronged or insulted, they are not to retaliate.

"But instead blessing." Instead of repaying evil, they are to bless, speaking well of those who hurt them, praying for them, and seeking their good.

"Knowing that you were called to this, that you may inherit a blessing." Believers are called to bless others, and they themselves will inherit God's blessing.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This teaching echoes Jesus' words: [[Luke 6:28|bless those who curse you, and pray for those who mistreat you]]. Paul taught the same: [[Romans 12:14|Bless those who persecute you; bless, and don't curse]], and [[Romans 12:17|Repay no one evil for evil]].

Peter had personally learned this lesson. When soldiers came to arrest Jesus, Peter drew a sword and cut off a man's ear (John 18:10). Jesus rebuked him and healed the man. Later, Peter watched Jesus endure insults and suffering without retaliation (1 Peter 2:23). Now Peter teaches what he learned.

Blessing those who insult us is not natural. It requires the Spirit's power. But it reflects the character of God, who blessed us when we were His enemies (Romans 5:10). It also breaks the cycle of retaliation and opens the door to reconciliation.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

When believers are insulted, criticized, or mistreated, they can choose not to respond in kind. This might mean pausing before reacting, refusing to speak harshly, and praying for the person who hurt them.

Blessing those who hurt us does not mean pretending the hurt did not happen or allowing ongoing abuse. Believers can set boundaries and seek justice while still refusing hatred and revenge.

Believers can also cultivate the qualities Peter lists: unity, compassion, love, tenderness, and humility, especially in their church communities, where these qualities build strong relationships.

### 1 Peter 3:15–16 — Always Ready to Give an Answer

verse: 1 Peter 3:15-16

#### Simple Explanation

"But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts." Many manuscripts read "sanctify Christ as Lord in your hearts." To "sanctify" means to set apart, to honor as holy. Believers are to give Christ the place of supreme honor in their hearts. When Christ is Lord, fear of others loses its power.

"Always be ready to give an answer to everyone who asks you a reason concerning the hope that is in you." The word "answer" (*apologia*) means a defense or explanation, as in a court of law. Believers should be prepared to explain why they have hope. People will notice their hope, especially in suffering, and some will ask about it.

"With humility and fear." Believers should explain their faith with gentleness, not arrogance or aggression, and with respect, both for the person asking and for God.

Verse 16: "having a good conscience." Believers should live in a way that is consistent with their message.

"Thus, while you are spoken against as evildoers, they may be disappointed who curse your good way of life in Christ." When believers live with integrity, those who slander them will be put to shame, because their accusations will be shown to be false.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage has been foundational for Christian "apologetics," the practice of explaining and defending the faith. But notice the context. Peter is writing to believers facing hostility. The questions they receive may come from curious people or from critics. In either case, believers should be ready.

What they are to explain is not primarily a philosophical system but "the hope that is in you." Their hope, rooted in Christ's resurrection (1 Peter 1:3), is what makes them different. When people see believers facing suffering with hope, they will want to know why.

The manner of the answer is as important as its content. Gentleness and respect reflect Christ's character. An argumentative, arrogant, or harsh defense of the faith contradicts the message. Paul similarly urges believers to let their speech be gracious, [[Colossians 4:6|seasoned with salt]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can prepare to explain their hope. This might involve thinking through how they came to faith, what Christ means to them, and why they believe the gospel is true. They do not need to be experts, but they should be ready to share honestly.

Believers can also ask whether their lives display hope. Do people see something different in how they face difficulties?

Finally, when believers talk about their faith, they can do so with gentleness and respect, listening well, answering honestly, and treating others with dignity, even those who disagree or criticize.

### 1 Peter 3:18 — The Righteous for the Unrighteous

verse: 1 Peter 3:18

#### Simple Explanation

"Because Christ also suffered for sins once." Christ's suffering was "for sins," dealing with sin through His death. And it happened "once," never needing to be repeated, as Hebrews also emphasizes (Hebrews 9:26–28; 10:10).

"The righteous for the unrighteous." Christ, who was completely righteous, suffered in the place of the unrighteous. He took the penalty that sinners deserved.

"That he might bring you to God." The purpose of Christ's death was reconciliation, bringing people into God's presence and relationship with Him. Sin had separated people from God; Christ's death removes the barrier.

"Being put to death in the flesh, but made alive in the Spirit." Christ died physically, in His human body. But He was raised to life by the Spirit, or in the realm of the Spirit, His resurrection life.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is one of the clearest summaries of the gospel in the New Testament. It explains the meaning of the cross: substitution ("the righteous for the unrighteous"), sufficiency ("once"), and purpose ([[v18|that he might bring you to God]]).

The phrase "the righteous for the unrighteous" expresses what theologians call substitutionary atonement. Christ took the place of sinners. Isaiah 53:5–6, which Peter quoted in chapter 2, says the Servant was wounded for our transgressions and the LORD laid on Him the iniquity of us all. Paul writes that God made Christ, who knew no sin, to be sin for us (2 Corinthians 5:21).

"That he might bring you to God" shows that salvation is not only about forgiveness but about relationship. Christ's death opens the way for sinners to come home to God.

In the context of 1 Peter, this verse also serves as encouragement. Christ suffered unjustly, but His suffering led to victory and resurrection. Believers who suffer for doing good follow the same path, from suffering to glory.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can rest in the truth that Christ suffered once for all their sins. Nothing needs to be added. They are forgiven and brought to God.

This verse also invites believers to marvel at Christ's love. He, the righteous one, suffered for the unrighteous, including them. That love can inspire gratitude and devotion.

Finally, when believers suffer unjustly, they can remember that Christ did too, and that His suffering led to resurrection. Their suffering is not the end of the story.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

First Peter 3 shows how faith shapes the closest relationships and how believers should respond to hostility. Wives with unbelieving husbands are encouraged to win them through godly conduct and the lasting beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit, not ruled by fear. Christians differ in how they apply these instructions today, but all agree that they never require a woman to sin or to remain in danger. Husbands are called to live with their wives in an understanding way, honoring them as equal heirs of God's grace, knowing that mistreating their wives hinders their prayers.

All believers are called to unity, compassion, love, tenderness, and humility. When they are insulted or wronged, they are to bless instead of retaliating, because they were called to inherit a blessing. They can seek peace, control their tongues, and trust that God's eyes are on the righteous.

Even when believers suffer for doing good, they are blessed and need not fear. They are to honor Christ as Lord in their hearts and always be ready to explain the hope within them, with gentleness and respect, living with a clear conscience so that slander is put to shame.

At the center of the chapter is Christ, who suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring us to God. He died, was raised, and now reigns at God's right hand over every power. Whatever believers face, they belong to the victorious Christ, and their suffering, like His, leads to glory.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring me to God. Thank You for Your love and for Your victory over every power. Bless marriages with understanding, honor, faithfulness, and mutual respect, and protect all who live in fear in their homes. Give me the beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit, precious in Your sight. Make me like-minded with Your people, compassionate, loving, tenderhearted, and humble. When I am insulted or wronged, help me to bless rather than retaliate. Teach me to keep my tongue from evil and to seek peace. Help me to honor You as Lord in my heart and to be ready to explain the hope I have, with gentleness and respect. When I suffer for doing good, keep me from fear, and remind me that You reign at the right hand of God. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
