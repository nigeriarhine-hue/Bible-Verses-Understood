# Luke 20
theme: The Beloved Son, the Rejected Stone, and the Children of the Resurrection
main: Teaching daily in the temple, Jesus is challenged about His authority and answers with a parable in which a vineyard owner sends his beloved son to tenants who kill him. He declares that the stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone. He silences traps about taxes and the resurrection, teaching that those raised from the dead become children of the resurrection, alive to God. He then points to the Messiah as David's Lord and warns against religious leaders who exploit the vulnerable.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 20 takes place in the temple courts during Jesus' final days in Jerusalem. At the end of chapter 19, Jesus had wept over the city, cleansed the temple, and begun teaching there every day, while the chief priests, scribes, and leaders sought to destroy Him but could not, because [[Luke 19:48|all the people hung on to every word that he said]]. In chapter 20, the leaders try a different approach: public challenges designed to discredit Jesus or give them grounds to hand Him over to the Roman governor.

Each of the events in this chapter also appears in Matthew and Mark, where they are studied in depth: the question of authority (Matthew 21:23–27), the parable of the tenants (Matthew 21:33–46; Mark 12:10), taxes to Caesar (Matthew 22:21), the resurrection question (Matthew 22:32), David's Lord (Matthew 22:41–46), and the warning about scribes (Mark 12:38–40). This chapter focuses on the details Luke emphasizes.

### The Question of Authority (20:1–8)

Luke notes that Jesus was [[v1|teaching the people in the temple and preaching the Good News]]. Even in the midst of mounting danger, Jesus continued His mission of proclaiming the good news.

The chief priests and scribes, together with the elders, the three groups that made up the Sanhedrin, the ruling council, came and asked, [[v2|by what authority do you do these things? Or who is giving you this authority?]] "These things" probably included His cleansing of the temple and His teaching. They held official authority over the temple, and Jesus had acted without their permission.

Jesus answered with a question about John's baptism: was it from heaven or from men? The leaders realized they were trapped. If they said "from heaven," Jesus would ask why they had not believed John. If they said "from men," Luke adds, [[v6|all the people will stone us, for they are persuaded that John was a prophet]]. So they said they did not know. Jesus replied that He would not tell them by what authority He acted. Their refusal to answer honestly showed that they were not genuinely seeking the truth.

### The Parable of the Tenants (20:9–19)

Jesus then told the people a parable. A man planted a vineyard, leased it to tenant farmers, and went away for a long time. At harvest, he sent a servant to collect his share of the fruit, but the tenants beat him and sent him away empty-handed. He sent a second servant, whom they beat and treated shamefully, and a third, whom they wounded and threw out. These servants represent the prophets God sent to Israel, many of whom were mistreated and rejected.

Then the owner said, [[v13|What shall I do? I will send my beloved son. It may be that seeing him, they will respect him]]. This verse is studied below. But the tenants reasoned, [[v14|This is the heir. Come, let’s kill him, that the inheritance may be ours]]. They threw him out of the vineyard and killed him. Jesus asked what the owner would do: he would come, destroy those tenants, and give the vineyard to others.

When the people heard this, they said, [[v16|May that never be!]] Luke alone records this horrified response. They understood the seriousness of what Jesus was describing. Then Jesus looked directly at them and quoted Psalm 118:22, studied below.

The chief priests and scribes wanted to arrest Him at that very hour, because they knew the parable was about them. But they feared the people.

### Taxes to Caesar (20:20–26)

The leaders watched Jesus closely and sent spies who [[v20|pretended to be righteous]], hoping to trap Him in His words so that they could hand Him over to the governor, Pilate. Luke makes their purpose explicit: they wanted a political charge that Rome would take seriously.

The spies flattered Jesus and asked whether it was lawful to pay taxes to Caesar. Jesus saw through their trickery. He asked to see a denarius and asked whose image and inscription were on it. They said, "Caesar's." He said, [[v25|Then give to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s]]. They could not trap Him, and they were amazed and fell silent. Ironically, at His trial before Pilate, the leaders would falsely accuse Jesus of [[Luke 23:2|forbidding paying taxes to Caesar]], the very thing He had refused to do.

### The Question About Resurrection (20:27–40)

Some Sadducees, who denied the resurrection, came with a scenario about a woman who married seven brothers in turn and asked whose wife she would be in the resurrection. Luke's version of Jesus' answer includes details not found in Matthew or Mark, studied below. Jesus then pointed to the burning bush, where God called Himself "the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob," and concluded, [[v38|Now he is not the God of the dead, but of the living, for all are alive to him]]. Luke alone adds "for all are alive to him."

Some of the scribes, who as Pharisees believed in the resurrection, said, [[v39|Teacher, you speak well]]. After that, no one dared to ask Him any more questions.

### David's Son and David's Lord (20:41–44)

Jesus then asked how people could say that the Messiah is David's son, when David himself calls Him "Lord" in Psalm 110:1: [[v42|The Lord said to my Lord, “Sit at my right hand,]] [[v43|until I make your enemies the footstool of your feet.”]] If David calls the Messiah "Lord," how can He merely be David's son? Jesus was pointing to His own identity as greater than David, the Son of God who would be exalted to God's right hand. Peter later quoted this same psalm at Pentecost to explain Jesus' resurrection and ascension (Acts 2:34–36).

### Beware of the Scribes (20:45–47)

In the hearing of all the people, Jesus warned His disciples about scribes who liked to walk around in long robes, loved respectful greetings in the marketplaces, the best seats in the synagogues, and places of honor at banquets. They [[v47|devour widows’ houses, and for a pretense make long prayers]]. They would receive greater condemnation. This warning leads directly into the next scene in Luke 21, where Jesus notices a poor widow giving her last coins.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 20:13 — "I Will Send My Beloved Son"

verse: Luke 20:13

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the turning point of the parable of the tenants, which Jesus told in the temple courts shortly after the religious leaders challenged His authority. In the parable, a man planted a vineyard and rented it to tenant farmers before going away for a long time. When harvest came, he sent servants to collect his share of the fruit. One after another, the tenants beat the servants, treated them shamefully, wounded them, and sent them away with nothing.

Then [[v13|The lord of the vineyard said, ‘What shall I do?’]]. The owner paused to consider. This question expresses the patience and the pain of the owner, who had been rejected again and again.

[[v13|I will send my beloved son]]. The owner decided to send the one closest to his heart. The phrase "beloved son" is the same phrase the Father used at Jesus' baptism (Luke 3:22) and transfiguration (Luke 9:35). Jesus' listeners who knew those events, and Luke's readers, would recognize that Jesus was speaking about Himself.

[[v13|It may be that seeing him, they will respect him]]. Luke alone includes the word "It may be" (or "perhaps"). The owner hoped the tenants would respect his son even though they had rejected the servants. This expresses God's patient hope that His people would receive His Son.

But the tenants saw the son as an opportunity. They said, "This is the heir. Come, let's kill him, that the inheritance may be ours." They threw him out of the vineyard and killed him. Jesus was predicting His own death at the hands of the religious leaders, outside the city walls of Jerusalem.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the depth of God's patience and love. Throughout Israel's history, God sent prophet after prophet, and many were rejected. Yet God did not give up. He sent His own Son. Hebrews 1:1–2 describes this: God spoke through the prophets in many ways, but in these last days He has spoken through His Son.

It also shows that sending the Son was God's greatest act of love. The owner sent the one dearest to him. John 3:16 says that God so loved the world that He gave His only Son. The sending of the beloved Son was costly; it led to his death.

Finally, the verse shows the tragedy of rejecting God's Son. The tenants saw the son as an obstacle to their own control and possession. Many people today reject Jesus for similar reasons: they want to keep control of their lives, rather than submit to God as the rightful owner.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to receive God's Son with honor and gratitude. God sent His beloved Son to us. The right response is to respect Him, listen to Him, and give Him the fruit of our lives.

It also reminds believers that everything belongs to God. The tenants acted as though the vineyard was theirs. Believers are stewards, not owners, of their lives, gifts, and resources. They can give God the "fruit" He deserves: worship, obedience, and lives that honor Him.

Finally, this verse invites believers to marvel at God's patience. Despite repeated rejection, God kept reaching out. He continues to show patience today, giving people time to turn to Him. Believers can respond to that patience with repentance and gratitude, and they can share God's patient love with others.

### Luke 20:17–18 — The Rejected Stone

verse: Luke 20:17-18

#### Simple Explanation

These verses come immediately after the parable of the tenants. Jesus had described tenant farmers who killed the owner's beloved son, and He said the owner would destroy them and give the vineyard to others. The people listening were horrified and said, "May that never be!"

Then [[v17|But he looked at them]]. Luke emphasizes Jesus' direct, searching gaze, which gave His next words great weight.

[[v17|Then what is this that is written, ‘The stone which the builders rejected was made the chief cornerstone’?]] Jesus quoted Psalm 118:22. The same psalm had been sung by the crowds at His entry into Jerusalem a few days earlier ("Blessed is the King who comes in the name of the Lord," Psalm 118:26). In the psalm, the builders rejected a stone as unsuitable, but God made it the most important stone in the building. The cornerstone held the walls together and set the alignment for the entire structure.

Jesus was applying this to Himself. The "builders," Israel's religious leaders, were rejecting Him. But God would make Him the cornerstone of His new work. The parable ended with the son's death; the psalm pointed beyond death to vindication. Jesus' rejection and death would lead to His exaltation.

Then Jesus added a warning in verse 18, using imagery that may draw on Isaiah 8:14–15 and Daniel 2:34–35: [[v18|Everyone who falls on that stone will be broken to pieces, but it will crush whomever it falls on to dust]]. Those who stumble over Jesus, rejecting Him, will be broken. And when the stone falls in judgment, it will crush those who oppose it. In Daniel, a stone cut without hands strikes and destroys earthly kingdoms and becomes a great mountain, representing God's everlasting kingdom.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal that God turns human rejection into His triumph. The leaders thought they could get rid of Jesus by killing Him. Instead, His death and resurrection made Him the cornerstone of God's people. Peter later proclaimed this very verse to the Sanhedrin (Acts 4:10–12) and in his letter (1 Peter 2:4–8).

They also show that no one can remain neutral toward Jesus. He is either the cornerstone on which people build their lives or the stone over which they stumble. Rejecting Him has serious consequences.

Finally, these verses connect with the hope of God's kingdom. Like the stone in Daniel's vision, Jesus' kingdom will ultimately prevail over every earthly power.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses call believers to build their lives on Jesus as the cornerstone. Their values, decisions, and relationships should be aligned with Him. A life built on any other foundation will not stand.

They also warn those who are rejecting Jesus. Stumbling over Him leads to brokenness. The invitation remains to turn to Him while there is time.

Finally, these verses encourage believers who feel rejected for their faith. Jesus was rejected by the powerful, but God exalted Him. Believers can trust that God sees and will vindicate those who belong to Him.

### Luke 20:35–36 — Children of the Resurrection

verse: Luke 20:35-36

#### Simple Explanation

These verses are part of Jesus' answer to the Sadducees, a group of religious leaders who denied the resurrection of the dead. They had presented a scenario about a woman who married seven brothers in succession according to the law of levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5–6), asking whose wife she would be in the resurrection. They intended to show that belief in resurrection was absurd.

Jesus first said, [[v34|The children of this age marry and are given in marriage]]. Marriage belongs to life in the present age. Then He described the age to come: [[v35|But those who are considered worthy to attain to that age and the resurrection from the dead neither marry nor are given in marriage]].

"Considered worthy" does not mean that people earn the resurrection by their merit. It describes those whom God counts as belonging to the age to come, those who are His. "That age" refers to the coming age of God's kingdom, after the resurrection.

[[v36|For they can’t die any more]]. Luke alone includes this explanation. In this present age, marriage and children are needed partly because people die; new generations carry on life. In the age to come, death will be no more, so marriage in its earthly form will no longer serve the same purpose.

[[v36|for they are like the angels]]. Jesus does not say people become angels; He says they will be like angels in that they will not die and will not marry.

[[v36|and are children of God, being children of the resurrection]]. Those raised to eternal life will be fully revealed as God's children. Their identity will be defined by resurrection life.

Jesus' answer shows that the Sadducees' scenario was based on a misunderstanding. They assumed that resurrection life would simply continue earthly arrangements. But God's power will transform life in the age to come.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses affirm the reality of the resurrection. Jesus taught clearly that the dead will be raised and that those who belong to God will live forever. His own resurrection a few days later would confirm this teaching. Paul writes that believers will be raised with transformed, imperishable bodies (1 Corinthians 15:42–44).

They also show that resurrection life is not merely a continuation of earthly life but a transformation. Relationships, including marriage, will be transformed. This does not mean that the love between spouses will be lost; it means that all relationships will be fulfilled in the greater reality of being God's children together in His presence.

Finally, the phrase "children of the resurrection" describes believers' ultimate identity. They are God's children, destined for resurrection life. This identity gives hope that death is not the end and that God's family will be gathered forever.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses offer hope to believers facing death or grieving loved ones. Death is not final for those who belong to God. They will be raised, will never die again, and will live as God's children forever.

They also help believers hold earthly relationships in proper perspective. Marriage and family are precious gifts in this age, but they are not ultimate. Single believers, widows, widowers, and those whose marriages have been painful can find comfort in knowing that their deepest identity is as children of God and children of the resurrection.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to live now in light of the age to come. Knowing that they are destined for resurrection life can shape how they spend their time, treat others, and face suffering, with hope and confidence in God's power.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 20 shows Jesus facing mounting opposition with wisdom, courage, and truth. The religious leaders refused to answer honestly about John's baptism, revealing that they were not seeking truth. Believers can examine whether they are open to truth even when it is uncomfortable or costly.

The parable of the tenants reveals God's patience and love, sending prophet after prophet and finally His beloved Son. It also shows the tragedy of rejecting Him to keep control of what belongs to God. Believers can receive Jesus with honor, give God the fruit of their lives, and build on Him as the cornerstone.

The question about taxes teaches that believers can fulfill their responsibilities in society while giving their whole selves to God, in whose image they are made. The answer to the Sadducees gives believers confident hope in the resurrection: those who belong to God are alive to Him and will be raised as children of the resurrection, never to die again.

Finally, Jesus' question about David's Lord points to His divine identity, and His warning about the scribes calls believers to humility and to the protection of the vulnerable. Religious leaders who seek honor while exploiting widows face greater judgment. Believers, especially those in leadership, can serve humbly, protect those who are weak, and pray sincerely rather than for show.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, thank You for Your patience in sending prophet after prophet, and finally Your beloved Son. Forgive me for the times I have acted as though my life were my own. I receive Jesus with honor, and I want to give You the fruit You deserve. Help me to build my life on Jesus, the cornerstone, and never to stumble over Him.

Help me to fulfill my responsibilities in the world while giving my whole self to You. Thank You for the hope of the resurrection, and that all who belong to You are alive to You. Comfort me and all who grieve with the promise that Your children will never die again. Keep me humble, honest, and caring toward the vulnerable, and free me from seeking honor for myself. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
