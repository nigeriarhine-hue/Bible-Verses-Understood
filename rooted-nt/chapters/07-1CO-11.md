# 1 Corinthians 11
theme: Honor in Worship and the Meaning of the Lord's Supper
main: Paul addresses two problems in the Corinthians' worship gatherings. First, he gives instruction about head coverings for men and women who pray and prophesy, grounding it in honor, creation, and the mutual dependence of men and women in the Lord. Second, he rebukes the church because their gatherings for the Lord's Supper have become occasions of division, where the wealthy feast while the poor go hungry. He reminds them of the words Jesus spoke on the night He was betrayed, explains that the Supper proclaims the Lord's death until He comes, and calls each believer to examine themselves and discern the Lord's body, so that they come together in love rather than for judgment.

## Chapter Background and Story

1 Corinthians 11 begins a section of the letter (chapters 11–14) about the church's worship gatherings. In Corinth, believers met in homes, often in the houses of wealthier members. Worship included prayer, prophecy, teaching, singing, and a shared meal that included the Lord's Supper.

The first issue Paul addresses, head coverings, is one of the most difficult passages in Paul's letters for modern readers. The customs involved were closely tied to the culture of the first century, and scholars still debate exactly what Paul meant by several phrases. The second issue, the abuse of the Lord's Supper, is clearer and contains the earliest written account of Jesus' words at the Last Supper.

The chapter begins with a sentence that probably belongs with chapter 10: [[v1|Be imitators of me, even as I also am of Christ]]. Paul had just described how he gave up his rights to seek the good of others. He invites the Corinthians to follow his example as he follows Christ.

### Head Coverings in Worship (11:2–16)

Paul begins with praise: [[v2|Now I praise you, brothers, that you remember me in all things, and hold firm the traditions, even as I delivered them to you]]. Then he explains: [[v3|But I would have you know that the head of every man is Christ, and the head of the woman is man, and the head of Christ is God]]. The word "head" has been understood in different ways. Many interpreters understand it to mean authority, so that Paul is describing an order of leadership: Christ, man, woman. Others understand "head" to mean source or origin, since Paul goes on to say that woman came from man in creation (11:8, 12) and Christ came from God. Notice that the relationship between Christ and God involves both equality and order; Christ is fully God, yet He submitted to the Father. Whatever "head" means, it does not imply that women are of lesser worth.

Paul then speaks of men and women praying and prophesying. [[v4|Every man praying or prophesying, having his head covered, dishonors his head]]. [[v5|But every woman praying or prophesying with her head uncovered dishonors her head]]. Notice that Paul assumes women pray and prophesy in the gathered church. His concern is how they do so. In Corinthian culture, a married woman's head covering was a sign of modesty and respectability. A woman appearing publicly without it might be seen as immodest or as rejecting her marriage. For men, covering the head in worship was associated with certain pagan religious practices.

Paul appeals to creation: [[v7|For a man indeed ought not to have his head covered, because he is the image and glory of God, but the woman is the glory of the man]]. [[v8|For man is not from woman, but woman from man]]; [[v9|for man wasn’t created for the woman, but woman for the man]]. Paul refers to Genesis 2, where the woman was created from the man to be his partner. [[v10|For this cause the woman ought to have authority over her own head, because of the angels]]. This difficult verse has been interpreted in many ways. Some think the angels observe the church's worship and care about proper order (compare 1 Corinthians 4:9; Ephesians 3:10). Many note that the Greek says the woman ought to have authority "over" her head, which may mean she has the right to pray and prophesy, exercising her own authority with appropriate honor. Paul then balances his teaching, as studied below.

Paul also appeals to what was natural and customary: [[v14|Doesn’t even nature itself teach you that if a man has long hair, it is a dishonor to him?]] [[v15|But if a woman has long hair, it is a glory to her, for her hair is given to her for a covering]]. "Nature" here may mean the natural order of things or the customs that had become second nature in their culture. He concludes, [[v16|But if any man seems to be contentious, we have no such custom, neither do God’s assemblies]].

Christians today apply this passage in different ways. Some churches practice head coverings for women in worship, believing the instruction is still binding. Most understand the head covering as a cultural expression of a deeper principle, honoring God and one another, maintaining appropriate distinctions between men and women, and avoiding behavior that would bring shame on the church, which can be expressed in different ways in different cultures. Christians also differ on how this passage relates to women's roles in church leadership. What is clear is that Paul cares about honor, order, and the mutual dependence of men and women in the Lord.

### Abuses at the Lord's Supper (11:17–22)

Paul's tone changes: [[v17|But in giving you this command I don’t praise you, because you come together not for the better but for the worse]]. Their gatherings were doing more harm than good. [[v18|For first of all, when you come together in the assembly, I hear that divisions exist among you]]. The divisions here seem to be social and economic. [[v19|For there also must be factions among you, that those who are approved may be revealed among you]]. Divisions reveal who is genuinely faithful.

[[v20|When therefore you assemble yourselves together, it is not the Lord’s supper that you eat]]. [[v21|For in your eating each one takes his own supper first. One is hungry, and another is drunken]]. In a Roman home, the wealthier guests might eat in the dining room, arriving early with plenty of food and wine, while poorer members, including slaves who had to work until evening, arrived late and found little or nothing left. The meal meant to express unity in Christ had become a display of social inequality.

[[v22|What, don’t you have houses to eat and to drink in? Or do you despise God’s assembly and put them to shame who don’t have enough?]] By humiliating the poor, they were despising the church itself. Paul refuses to praise them.

### Examine Yourselves (11:27–34)

Paul's warning about eating unworthily is studied below. He adds, [[v30|For this cause many among you are weak and sickly, and not a few sleep]]. Paul links some illness and even death in the church to their abuse of the Supper. This does not mean all sickness is caused by sin (John 9:2–3), but in this case, God was disciplining His people. [[v32|But when we are judged, we are disciplined by the Lord, that we may not be condemned with the world]]. God's discipline is meant to restore, not to destroy.

Paul gives a practical solution: [[v33|Therefore, my brothers, when you come together to eat, wait for one another]]. [[v34|But if anyone is hungry, let him eat at home, lest your coming together be for judgment]]. The gathering should reflect the unity and love of Christ's body.

## Important Verses Explained

### 1 Corinthians 11:11–12 — Not Independent, but All from God

verse: 1 Corinthians 11:11-12

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been discussing head coverings in worship and had pointed to the order of creation, saying that woman was created from man and for man (Genesis 2). Some readers might have taken this to mean that men are superior to women. Paul immediately guards against that misunderstanding.

[[v11|Nevertheless, neither is the woman independent of the man, nor the man independent of the woman, in the Lord]]. The word "nevertheless" signals a strong balancing statement. "In the Lord," in the new life believers share in Christ, men and women are not independent of each other. They belong together. They need each other.

[[v12|For as woman came from man, so a man also comes through a woman]]. In creation, the first woman came from the first man. But ever since, every man has been born of a woman. If one were to argue for male priority from creation, one would have to admit that every man owes his life to a mother. The relationship is mutual.

[[v12|but all things are from God]]. Ultimately, both men and women come from God. Neither is the source of the other's worth. Both are created by God, in His image (Genesis 1:27), and both depend on Him.

These verses show that whatever distinctions Paul has in mind, they exist within a deeper equality and mutual dependence. Men and women are partners, not rivals.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reflect the creation account, where God created humanity as male and female, both in His image: [[Genesis 1:27|God created man in his own image. In God’s image he created him; male and female he created them]]. Both men and women bear the full dignity of God's image.

They also reflect the new creation in Christ. Paul writes, [[Galatians 3:28|There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus]]. In Christ, men and women share equally in salvation, the Spirit, and the inheritance of God's children.

Finally, these verses show that God is the source of all. Human relationships, including those between men and women, find their meaning in God, who made all things and to whom all things belong.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage mutual respect between men and women. Believers can honor one another as equally created in God's image and equally redeemed by Christ, recognizing that they need each other.

They also guard against using Scripture to belittle women. Any interpretation of this chapter that treats women as inferior contradicts Paul's own words here. Churches can value, include, and honor women's gifts and contributions.

Finally, these verses encourage partnership in marriage, family, and church. Men and women can work together, each contributing their gifts, in humble dependence on God and one another.

### 1 Corinthians 11:23–25 — "Do This in Memory of Me"

verse: 1 Corinthians 11:23-25

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had rebuked the Corinthians for turning the Lord's Supper into a selfish meal where some went hungry while others got drunk. To correct them, he reminded them of what the Lord's Supper truly means.

[[v23|For I received from the Lord that which also I delivered to you]]. Paul passed on what he had received. The words "received" and "delivered" were used for passing on sacred tradition. Paul received this teaching through the earliest believers, and ultimately from the Lord Himself. This passage, written around A.D. 54, is the earliest written account of the Last Supper, older than the Gospels.

[[v23|that the Lord Jesus on the night in which he was betrayed took bread]]. Paul sets the scene: the night of betrayal. Even as Judas was betraying Him, Jesus gave His disciples this gift.

[[v24|When he had given thanks, he broke it and said, “Take, eat. This is my body, which is broken for you. Do this in memory of me.”]] Jesus gave thanks, broke the bread, and identified it with His body given for His disciples. The word for "given thanks" is eucharisteo, from which comes "Eucharist," a name many Christians use for the Lord's Supper.

[[v25|In the same way he also took the cup after supper, saying, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink, in memory of me.”]] The cup represents the new covenant, the promise God made through Jeremiah to forgive His people's sins and write His law on their hearts (Jeremiah 31:31–34). This covenant is sealed by Jesus' blood, His death on the cross.

Twice Jesus said, "Do this in memory of me." The Supper is a remembrance. But in Scripture, remembering is more than recalling a past event. It is bringing it into the present, participating in its meaning, as Israel "remembered" the Passover by celebrating it each year.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the Lord's Supper is centered on Christ's sacrifice. His body was broken and His blood poured out for His people. The Supper keeps the cross at the center of the church's life. The meaning of Jesus' covenant blood is studied at Matthew 26:28 and Mark 14:24.

They also show that the Supper is a gift from Jesus Himself. It was not invented by the church; Jesus instituted it and commanded His followers to continue it. Christians across all traditions celebrate it, though they understand Christ's presence in it in different ways.

Finally, these verses show the grace of Jesus. On the night He was betrayed, abandoned, and arrested, He gave His disciples a meal of love. He gave Himself for those who would fail Him.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to participate faithfully in the Lord's Supper. Jesus commanded it, and believers can obey with gratitude, remembering His sacrifice.

They also encourage believers to remember Christ's death often, not only at the Lord's Supper. Believers can reflect regularly on the cross and what it means for their lives.

Finally, these verses call believers to approach the Supper with the same self-giving love Jesus showed. The Corinthians were using the meal selfishly. Believers can come to the table in a spirit of humility, gratitude, and love for one another.

### 1 Corinthians 11:26 — Proclaiming the Lord's Death Until He Comes

verse: 1 Corinthians 11:26

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had just recounted Jesus' words at the Last Supper. Now he explains what happens when believers celebrate the Supper.

[[v26|For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord’s death]]. The Lord's Supper is a proclamation. Every time believers share the bread and cup, they declare the message of the cross. The Supper is a visible sermon, announcing that Jesus died for sinners.

The word "proclaim" is the same word used for preaching the gospel. The Supper does not merely remind believers privately; it announces publicly that Christ died.

[[v26|until he comes]]. The Supper also looks forward. Believers celebrate it "until he comes," until Jesus returns. It is a meal for the time between His first coming and His second coming. Jesus said at the Last Supper that He would not drink of the fruit of the vine again until He drank it new in His Father's kingdom (Matthew 26:29). The Supper anticipates the great banquet at the end of the age, the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9).

So the Lord's Supper looks in three directions: backward to the cross, upward to the risen Lord who is present with His people, and forward to His return.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that the Lord's Supper is a central act of Christian witness. In every generation, as believers gather at the table, they proclaim the death of Christ. This has continued for two thousand years, in every nation and language.

It also shows that the Christian life is lived in hope. Believers do not only look back to the cross; they look forward to Christ's return. The Supper is a foretaste of the future feast, when Christ's people will be gathered with Him forever.

Finally, the verse connects the Supper to the gospel. The death of Christ is the heart of the good news. Every celebration of the Supper is a reminder of that good news and an invitation to receive it.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to see the Lord's Supper as an act of proclamation. When believers share in it, they declare their faith in Christ's death. It is an opportunity to remember and to witness.

It also encourages believers to live in hope of Christ's return. The Supper reminds them that history is moving toward a glorious conclusion. Believers can live with expectation and readiness.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that their whole lives can proclaim Christ's death. The Supper is one way of proclaiming it, but believers can also proclaim it through words of witness and lives of self-giving love.

### 1 Corinthians 11:28–29 — Examine Yourself and Discern the Body

verse: 1 Corinthians 11:28-29

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had warned that [[v27|whoever eats this bread or drinks the Lord’s cup in a way unworthy of the Lord will be guilty of the body and the blood of the Lord]]. Eating "in an unworthy way" does not mean that only perfect people can take the Supper. No one is worthy in that sense; the Supper is for sinners saved by grace. In context, eating in an unworthy way meant treating the Supper carelessly and selfishly, as the Corinthians were doing by humiliating the poor and getting drunk.

[[v28|But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of the bread and drink of the cup]]. Before taking the Supper, each believer should examine their heart and life. Are they coming with faith in Christ? Are they treating fellow believers with love? Are there sins they need to confess or relationships they need to make right? Notice that after examining themselves, believers are to eat. Self-examination is not meant to keep believers away from the table but to prepare them to come rightly.

[[v29|For he who eats and drinks in an unworthy way eats and drinks judgment to himself if he doesn’t discern the Lord’s body]]. "Discerning the body" has been understood in two ways, and both are likely included. It may mean recognizing the significance of the bread as representing Christ's body given for them. It may also mean recognizing the church as the body of Christ (10:17; 12:27), and treating fellow believers with love. The Corinthians were failing to discern the body by despising the poor members of the church.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the Lord's Supper is holy. It is not an ordinary meal. It represents Christ's body and blood and the unity of His people. Treating it carelessly dishonors Christ.

They also show the importance of self-examination in the Christian life. Believers are called to regularly examine their hearts and lives. The psalmist prayed, [[Psalm 139:23|Search me, God, and know my heart. Try me, and know my thoughts]].

Finally, these verses show that love for fellow believers is connected to communion with Christ. Believers cannot rightly share the body and blood of Christ while despising His body, the church. Jesus taught that believers should be reconciled with others before bringing their offering to God (Matthew 5:23–24).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to prepare their hearts before taking the Lord's Supper. Believers can take time to pray, confess sin, and reflect on Christ's sacrifice.

They also encourage believers to examine their relationships. Before coming to the table, believers can ask whether there is anyone they need to forgive or seek forgiveness from, and whether they are treating all members of the church with love and honor.

Finally, these verses encourage believers who feel unworthy. The Supper is not for perfect people but for sinners who trust in Christ. Self-examination leads not to staying away but to coming humbly, receiving grace, and renewing commitment to Christ and His people.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

1 Corinthians 11 shows that worship should be marked by honor, order, and respect. Paul's teaching about head coverings reflected the customs of his day, and Christians apply it in different ways. But its underlying principles remain: honoring God in worship, avoiding what brings shame on the church, and recognizing that men and women are mutually dependent in the Lord, both coming from God and both created in His image.

The chapter also confronts division in the church. The Corinthians' gatherings had become places where the wealthy feasted and the poor went hungry. Believers today can examine whether their churches reflect social divisions or the unity of Christ's body, and they can make sure that every member, especially the poor and overlooked, is honored and cared for.

1 Corinthians 11 contains the earliest written account of Jesus' words at the Last Supper. The bread represents His body broken for His people; the cup represents the new covenant in His blood. Believers can celebrate the Supper faithfully, remembering Christ's sacrifice, proclaiming His death, and looking forward to His return.

Finally, the chapter calls believers to examine themselves before coming to the Lord's table and to discern the Lord's body. Believers can prepare their hearts through confession and reconciliation, coming not as perfect people but as forgiven sinners, and treating one another with the love that the Supper proclaims.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for the gift of Your Supper, given on the night You were betrayed. Thank You for Your body broken for me and Your blood poured out to seal the new covenant. Help me to remember You, not only at Your table but every day. As I share in the bread and cup, let me proclaim Your death until You come. Search my heart before I come, and show me any sin I need to confess or relationship I need to make right. Help me to discern Your body, both in the bread and in Your people, and to honor every brother and sister, especially the poor and overlooked.
