# Acts 15
theme: Saved by Grace: The Jerusalem Council and the Unity of Jews and Gentiles
main: Some teachers from Judea insist that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses to be saved. The church sends Paul and Barnabas to Jerusalem, where the apostles and elders meet to consider the question. Peter testifies that God gave the Gentiles the Holy Spirit and cleansed their hearts by faith, declaring that Jews and Gentiles alike are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus. James shows that the prophets foretold the inclusion of the nations and proposes that Gentiles not be burdened, while asking them to avoid practices that would especially offend their Jewish brothers and sisters. A letter brings joy to the Gentile churches. The chapter closes with a sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas over John Mark.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 15 records one of the most important meetings in church history, often called the Jerusalem Council. It took place around A.D. 49, after Paul's first missionary journey. The question before the church was fundamental: Do Gentiles have to become Jews in order to become Christians? Specifically, must Gentile men be circumcised and must Gentile believers keep the whole Law of Moses, including its food laws and festivals, in order to be saved?

This was not a minor question. If the answer were yes, Christianity would remain a movement within Judaism, and Gentiles would have to adopt Jewish identity to follow Jesus. If the answer were no, the church would become a fellowship of Jews and Gentiles united by faith in Jesus alone. The answer would shape the gospel itself. Paul's letter to the Galatians addresses the same issue with great urgency.

### The Dispute Arises (15:1–5)

[[v1|Some men came down from Judea and taught the brothers, “Unless you are circumcised after the custom of Moses, you can’t be saved.”]] These teachers claimed that faith in Jesus was not enough; Gentiles also had to be circumcised. Paul and Barnabas strongly disagreed, and there was [[v2|no small discord and discussion with them]]. The church in Antioch decided to send Paul, Barnabas, and others to Jerusalem to consult with the apostles and elders.

On the way, they passed through Phoenicia and Samaria, [[v3|declaring the conversion of the Gentiles. They caused great joy to all the brothers]]. In Jerusalem, they were welcomed and reported what God had done. But [[v5|some of the sect of the Pharisees who believed]] insisted that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses. These were genuine believers in Jesus who had come from the Pharisee tradition. They sincerely believed that God's covenant required circumcision, as Genesis 17 taught.

### Peter's Testimony (15:6–11)

The apostles and elders gathered to consider the matter. [[v7|When there had been much discussion]], Peter stood up. He reminded them of Cornelius: God had chosen Peter to bring the gospel to the Gentiles, and God, [[v8|who knows the heart, testified about them, giving them the Holy Spirit, just like he did to us]]. [[v9|He made no distinction between us and them, cleansing their hearts by faith]]. Peter's conclusion is studied below.

### Barnabas and Paul's Report (15:12)

[[v12|All the multitude kept silence, and they listened to Barnabas and Paul reporting what signs and wonders God had done among the nations through them]]. Their testimony confirmed Peter's: God Himself was at work among the Gentiles, without requiring circumcision.

### James's Judgment (15:13–21)

Then James spoke. This was James the brother of Jesus, the recognized leader of the Jerusalem church, known for his devotion to the Law. His support would carry great weight with the Jewish believers. He pointed to Scripture, studied below, and proposed a decision, also studied below.

James added, [[v21|For Moses from generations of old has in every city those who preach him, being read in the synagogues every Sabbath]]. Jewish communities existed throughout the Roman world, and Moses' Law was read every week. Gentile believers needed to be sensitive to their Jewish neighbors and fellow believers, avoiding practices that would be especially offensive to them.

### The Letter to the Gentile Believers (15:22–35)

The apostles, elders, and whole church agreed. They chose two respected leaders, Judas called Barsabbas and Silas, to accompany Paul and Barnabas back to Antioch with a letter. The letter acknowledged that some had [[v24|troubled you with words, unsettling your souls]], without authorization from the Jerusalem leaders. It commended Barnabas and Paul as [[v26|men who have risked their lives for the name of our Lord Jesus Christ]].

The letter's key statement was: [[v28|For it seemed good to the Holy Spirit, and to us, to lay no greater burden on you than these necessary things]]. The decision was reached by the church together, guided by the Holy Spirit. It listed four things to avoid: [[v29|things sacrificed to idols, from blood, from things strangled, and from sexual immorality]].

In Antioch, the church gathered and read the letter. [[v31|When they had read it, they rejoiced over the encouragement]]. The Gentile believers were relieved and joyful. They did not have to become Jews to belong to Christ. Judas and Silas, who were prophets, encouraged and strengthened the believers. (Verse 34, which says Silas stayed in Antioch, appears in some later manuscripts but not in the earliest ones.) Paul and Barnabas remained in Antioch, teaching and preaching with many others.

### Paul and Barnabas Separate (15:36–41)

Some time later, Paul suggested that they revisit the churches they had planted. Barnabas wanted to take John Mark, but Paul did not, because Mark had left them in Pamphylia (13:13). This disagreement is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 15:10–11 — Saved Through the Grace of the Lord Jesus

verse: Acts 15:10-11

#### Simple Explanation

The council in Jerusalem was debating whether Gentile believers needed to be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses. After much discussion, Peter stood up. He reminded the council how God had sent him to Cornelius, a Gentile, and how God had given the Holy Spirit to the Gentiles just as He had to the Jewish believers, cleansing their hearts by faith.

Then Peter asked, [[v10|Now therefore why do you tempt God, that you should put a yoke on the neck of the disciples which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear?]] A "yoke" was a wooden frame placed on the necks of oxen to make them work. Rabbis spoke of taking on "the yoke of the Law." Peter said that requiring Gentiles to keep the whole Law as a condition of salvation would place a burden on them that even the Jews had never been able to carry perfectly. To impose it would be to "tempt God," testing Him by questioning what He had clearly shown.

[[v11|But we believe that we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they are]]. This is the heart of Peter's argument. Salvation comes "through the grace of the Lord Jesus," God's undeserved favor shown in Christ. And notice the order: Peter does not say "they are saved just as we are," but "we are saved just as they are." Jewish believers are saved in the same way as Gentile believers: by grace, not by keeping the Law.

These are the last words Peter speaks in the book of Acts. It is fitting that his final recorded statement is a clear declaration of salvation by grace.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses express the core truth of the gospel: salvation is by grace through faith, not by works of the Law. Paul writes, [[Ephesians 2:8|for by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God]], [[Ephesians 2:9|not of works, that no one would boast]]. Both Jews and Gentiles stand before God on the same basis.

They also show the danger of adding requirements to the gospel. The teachers from Judea said, "Unless you are circumcised, you can't be saved." This added a human requirement to God's free gift. Whenever people add conditions to faith in Christ, whether religious rituals, cultural practices, or moral achievements, they distort the gospel. Paul wrote to the Galatians that there is no other gospel, but that some [[Galatians 1:7|want to pervert the Good News of Christ]].

Finally, these verses show that the Law, while holy and good, cannot save. Peter acknowledged that no one had been able to carry its yoke perfectly. The Law reveals sin and points to the need for a Savior. Jesus offered a different yoke: [[Matthew 11:30|For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses free believers from trying to earn salvation. Many people, including Christians, live with a sense that they must prove themselves to God through good works, religious activity, or moral performance. Peter declares that believers are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus. They can rest in that grace.

They also challenge churches not to add unnecessary burdens to the gospel. Churches can unintentionally suggest that people must adopt certain cultural practices, dress codes, political views, or traditions to truly belong. The gospel requires only faith in Jesus. Churches can welcome people as they are, trusting God to transform them.

Finally, these verses encourage humility. Jewish believers had a rich heritage, but Peter acknowledged that they too were saved only by grace. No believer has grounds for pride. All stand before God as recipients of the same undeserved favor.

### Acts 15:16–17 — Rebuilding the Tabernacle of David

verse: Acts 15:16-17

#### Simple Explanation

After Peter, Barnabas, and Paul had spoken, James, the leader of the Jerusalem church, addressed the council. He agreed with Peter that God had visited the Gentiles "to take out of them a people for his name" (15:14). Then he showed that this agreed with the words of the prophets.

James quoted Amos 9:11–12: [[v16|After these things I will return. I will again build the tabernacle of David, which has fallen. I will again build its ruins. I will set it up]]. The "tabernacle" (or "tent") of David refers to David's royal house, his dynasty and kingdom. By the time of Amos, David's kingdom had been divided and weakened, and later it fell completely. Amos prophesied that God would one day restore it.

[[v17|that the rest of men may seek after the Lord: all the Gentiles who are called by my name, says the Lord, who does all these things]]. The purpose of rebuilding David's house was that the Gentiles, the nations, would seek the Lord. The phrase "called by my name" means belonging to God, as His own people.

James understood that this prophecy was being fulfilled. Through Jesus, the Son of David, God was rebuilding David's house. Jesus' resurrection and reign as King restored David's fallen dynasty. And as a result, Gentiles were seeking the Lord and becoming His people, not by becoming Jews, but as Gentiles "called by my name."

James quoted the passage in a form close to the Greek translation of the Old Testament, which emphasizes the Gentiles seeking the Lord.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the inclusion of the Gentiles was always part of God's plan. It was not an afterthought or a departure from Israel's hope. The prophets foresaw that when God restored David's kingdom, the nations would come to Him. Isaiah, Micah, Zechariah, and others spoke of nations streaming to worship the Lord.

They also show the importance of Scripture in the church's decision-making. The council did not decide based only on experience. Peter's testimony and the reports of Paul and Barnabas showed what God was doing, but James confirmed it from the Scriptures. Experience and Scripture together guided the church.

Finally, these verses show that Jesus is the fulfillment of God's promises to David. He is the King who reigns forever. Under His rule, Jews and Gentiles together become one people for God's name.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to read Scripture in light of Christ. The promises of the Old Testament find their fulfillment in Jesus and His church. Believers can grow in understanding as they see how the whole Bible tells one story.

They also show a wise pattern for making decisions in the church. When believers face difficult questions, they can consider what God is doing, listen to testimony, and test everything by Scripture.

Finally, these verses invite believers from every nation to see themselves as part of God's people. Gentile believers are not second-class members of God's family. They are among those "called by my name," included in God's promises through Jesus.

### Acts 15:19–20 — Don't Trouble Those Who Turn to God

verse: Acts 15:19-20

#### Simple Explanation

After showing that the prophets foretold the inclusion of the Gentiles, James gave his judgment: [[v19|Therefore my judgment is that we don’t trouble those from among the Gentiles who turn to God]]. The word "trouble" means to make difficult or to burden. James concluded that the church should not make it harder for Gentiles to come to God by requiring circumcision and the full observance of the Law of Moses.

This was a decisive moment. The leader of the Jerusalem church, known for his devotion to the Law, agreed with Peter and Paul that Gentiles did not need to become Jews to be saved.

But James added a request: [[v20|but that we write to them that they abstain from the pollution of idols, from sexual immorality, from what is strangled, and from blood]]. Four things were named.

First, "the pollution of idols," meaning food offered to idols or participation in idol worship. Gentiles came from a world full of idol temples, and food sacrificed to idols was common.

Second, "sexual immorality." Pagan culture was often permissive about sexual behavior, and some pagan worship included sexual practices.

Third and fourth, "what is strangled" and "blood." These relate to food laws. Animals that were strangled still had their blood in them. The prohibition against eating blood went back to God's command to Noah (Genesis 9:4), before the Law of Moses, and was deeply important to Jewish believers.

These requests were not conditions for salvation. Salvation was by grace through faith. Rather, they were ways for Gentile believers to avoid practices that were connected to idolatry or that would deeply offend Jewish believers, making fellowship between them possible. Jews and Gentiles in the church often shared meals together; these guidelines helped make that table fellowship possible.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show how the church balanced freedom and love. Gentile believers were free from the requirements of the Law, but they were asked to limit their freedom in certain areas for the sake of unity with Jewish believers. Paul later taught the same principle: believers should not use their freedom in ways that cause others to stumble (Romans 14:13–21; 1 Corinthians 8:9–13).

They also show that some moral standards are not merely cultural. The prohibition of sexual immorality is not just a Jewish custom; it reflects God's design for all people. Throughout the New Testament, believers are called to sexual purity (1 Thessalonians 4:3–5). Christians have generally understood the food-related requests as practical guidelines for that time and context, while the prohibitions against idolatry and sexual immorality remain permanent moral commands.

Finally, these verses show wise leadership. James sought a solution that upheld the gospel of grace while addressing the real concerns of Jewish believers. The decision preserved both truth and unity.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage churches to avoid placing unnecessary obstacles in the way of people coming to God. Believers can examine whether any of their expectations or traditions make it harder for others to come to Christ.

They also teach believers to use their freedom in love. In matters where Scripture gives freedom, believers can still choose to limit their freedom out of love for others, especially to avoid causing offense or harm to fellow believers.

Finally, these verses remind believers that freedom in Christ is not freedom to sin. Gentile believers were free from the Law's ceremonies, but they were still called to turn from idolatry and sexual immorality. Grace frees believers from trying to earn salvation, and it also empowers them to live holy lives.

### Acts 15:39–40 — A Sharp Disagreement

verse: Acts 15:39-40

#### Simple Explanation

After the Jerusalem Council, Paul and Barnabas returned to Antioch. Some time later, Paul suggested that they go back and visit the churches they had planted on their first journey. Barnabas wanted to take his cousin John Mark with them (Colossians 4:10).

But Paul disagreed. Mark had left them partway through the first journey, at Pamphylia, and returned home (13:13). Paul did not think it wise to take someone who had abandoned the work before.

[[v39|Then the contention grew so sharp that they separated from each other]]. The disagreement was intense. These two men had worked closely together for years. Barnabas had vouched for Paul when others were afraid of him (9:27) and had brought him to Antioch (11:25–26). They had suffered together on their missionary journey. Now they parted ways.

[[v39|Barnabas took Mark with him and sailed away to Cyprus]]. Barnabas, true to his name as "Son of Encouragement," gave Mark another chance.

[[v40|but Paul chose Silas and went out, being commended by the brothers to the grace of God]]. Paul chose Silas, one of the leaders who had brought the council's letter to Antioch. The church commended them to God's grace.

Luke does not say who was right. Both men had good reasons. Paul was concerned about the mission's reliability; Barnabas was concerned about restoring a young man who had failed.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage shows the honesty of Scripture. Luke does not hide the conflict between two great leaders. Even godly believers can disagree sharply. The church is made up of real people with real differences.

It also shows that God can bring good out of disagreement. Instead of one missionary team, there were now two. Barnabas and Mark went to Cyprus, and Paul and Silas went through Syria and Cilicia. The mission expanded.

Finally, the story has a hopeful ending beyond this chapter. Mark grew into a faithful servant of Christ. He later worked with Peter, who called him "my son" (1 Peter 5:13), and is traditionally regarded as the author of the Gospel of Mark. Paul and Mark were reconciled. Near the end of his life, Paul wrote, [[2 Timothy 4:11|Take Mark, and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for service]]. Barnabas's patience with Mark bore fruit, and Paul's relationship with him was restored.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage reminds believers that disagreements are a normal part of life, even among faithful Christians. Believers do not need to pretend that conflict never happens. They can face disagreements honestly and seek to handle them with grace.

It also shows that sometimes believers may need to go separate ways in ministry, while still serving the same Lord. When believers cannot agree on a particular course of action, they can part with blessing rather than bitterness, trusting God to work through both paths.

Finally, the story encourages believers to give second chances. Mark had failed, but Barnabas believed in him, and he became a valuable servant of Christ. Believers can follow Barnabas's example, encouraging those who have stumbled. And they can follow Paul's example later in life, being willing to reconcile and to recognize growth in others.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 15 declares that salvation is by grace through faith in Jesus, not by keeping the Law or adopting a particular culture. Jews and Gentiles are saved in the same way. Believers can rest in this grace, free from the burden of trying to earn God's acceptance. They can also guard the gospel from additions that would turn it into a message of human effort.

The chapter also shows how the church can handle serious disagreements. The council listened to testimony, discussed openly, consulted Scripture, and reached a decision together with the guidance of the Holy Spirit. Churches today can follow this pattern when facing difficult questions, seeking both truth and unity.

Acts 15 teaches believers to use their freedom in love. Gentile believers were free from the Law, but they were asked to avoid practices connected to idolatry and sexual immorality, and to be sensitive to the convictions of Jewish believers. Believers today can exercise their freedom in ways that build up others rather than causing them to stumble.

Finally, the chapter shows that even godly leaders can disagree sharply. Paul and Barnabas parted ways, yet God used both of them, and Mark, who had failed, was later restored. Believers can face conflict honestly, extend second chances, and trust God to bring good out of difficult situations.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You that I am saved through Your grace, not by my own efforts. Thank You that Jews and Gentiles, people of every nation, are saved in the same way, by faith in You. Guard me from adding burdens to Your gospel, and help me to welcome others as You have welcomed me. Give Your church wisdom when we face hard questions, and help us to listen to one another, to search the Scriptures, and to follow Your Spirit. Teach me to use my freedom in love, never causing others to stumble. When I disagree with fellow believers, help me to act with grace and humility. Make me like Barnabas, giving second chances to those who have failed, and like Paul, willing to be reconciled. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
