# Hebrews 11
theme: The Great Cloud of Faithful Witnesses
main: Faith is the assurance of things hoped for and the conviction of things not seen, and by it the people of old received God's approval. By faith we understand that God created the universe by His word. The author then traces faith through the generations: Abel offered a better sacrifice, Enoch pleased God and was taken up, and Noah built the ark, for without faith it is impossible to please God, since whoever comes to Him must believe that He exists and rewards those who seek Him. Abraham obeyed God's call and went out not knowing where he was going, living as a foreigner in tents while looking for the city whose builder is God; Sarah received power to conceive because she considered God faithful. These all died in faith without receiving what was promised, seeing it from a distance and confessing that they were strangers on earth seeking a better, heavenly country, and God is not ashamed to be called their God. Abraham offered Isaac, trusting God could raise the dead; Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph looked to the future; Moses' parents hid him, and Moses chose mistreatment with God's people over the passing pleasures of sin, valuing the reproach of Christ above Egypt's treasures. By faith Israel crossed the Red Sea, Jericho's walls fell, and Rahab was spared. Many others conquered kingdoms and escaped death, while others were tortured, mocked, imprisoned, and killed, the world not being worthy of them. All of them were commended for their faith, yet they did not receive the promise, because God had planned something better, so that only together with us would they be made complete.

## Chapter Background and Story

At the end of chapter 10, the author quoted Habakkuk 2:4: "the righteous one will live by faith," and assured his readers that they were "of those who have faith to the saving of the soul" (Hebrews 10:38–39). Chapter 11 explains what that faith looks like by giving a long series of examples from Israel's history.

This chapter is sometimes called the "Hall of Faith." It moves from creation to the patriarchs, to Moses and the exodus, to the conquest of Canaan, and then to a rapid summary of judges, kings, prophets, and martyrs. The repeated phrase "by faith" ties it together.

The purpose of this chapter is pastoral. The readers were weary and tempted to give up because of hardship. The author reminds them that God's people have always lived by faith, trusting God's promises before seeing them fulfilled, often in the midst of suffering. Many of these heroes of faith did not see the fulfillment of God's promises in their lifetimes. Yet they persevered. The readers are called to do the same.

### What Faith Is (11:1–3)

The definition of faith is studied below. [[v2|For by this, the elders obtained approval]]. "The elders" are the people of faith in the Old Testament. God approved of them because of their faith.

[[v3|By faith we understand that the universe has been framed by the word of God, so that what is seen has not been made out of things which are visible]]. Faith begins with creation. God made the universe by His word (Genesis 1; Psalm 33:6). What is visible came from what is invisible: God's command. Believers know this not by observation but by faith in God's revelation.

### Faith Before the Flood (11:4–7)

[[v4|By faith Abel offered to God a more excellent sacrifice than Cain, through which he had testimony given to him that he was righteous, God testifying with respect to his gifts; and through it he, being dead, still speaks]]. Abel's offering was accepted and Cain's was not (Genesis 4:3–5). The difference lay in Abel's faith. Though Abel was murdered, his faith still speaks. Hebrews 12:24 will say that Jesus' blood speaks better than Abel's.

[[v5|By faith Enoch was taken away, so that he wouldn’t see death, and he was not found, because God translated him. For he has had testimony given to him that before his translation he had been well pleasing to God]]. Genesis 5:24 says that Enoch walked with God and was no more, because God took him. Enoch did not experience death. The principle that underlies his life is studied below.

[[v7|By faith Noah, being warned about things not yet seen, moved with godly fear, prepared a ship for the saving of his house, through which he condemned the world and became heir of the righteousness which is according to faith]]. Noah believed God's warning about a flood he had never seen and built the ark, a huge project that must have looked foolish to others. His obedient faith saved his family and exposed the unbelief of the world around him.

### The Faith of Abraham and Sarah (11:8–19)

[[v8|By faith Abraham, when he was called, obeyed to go out to the place which he was to receive for an inheritance. He went out, not knowing where he went]]. God called Abraham to leave his homeland for a land God would show him (Genesis 12:1–4). He obeyed without knowing his destination. Faith often means obeying God without seeing the whole path.

[[v9|By faith he lived as an alien in the land of promise, as in a land not his own, dwelling in tents with Isaac and Jacob, the heirs with him of the same promise]]. [[v10|For he was looking for the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God]]. Even in the promised land, Abraham lived in tents, like a foreigner. He never owned the land except for a burial plot (Genesis 23). His hope reached beyond Canaan to a permanent city built by God.

[[v11|By faith even Sarah herself received power to conceive, and she bore a child when she was past age, since she counted him faithful who had promised]]. Sarah had laughed in disbelief at first (Genesis 18:12), but she came to trust God's promise. The miracle of Isaac's birth rested on God's faithfulness. (Some interpreters read this verse as referring to Abraham's faith, with Sarah included; the Greek is ambiguous.) [[v12|Therefore as many as the stars of the sky in multitude, and as innumerable as the sand which is by the sea shore, were fathered by one man, and him as good as dead]]. From one elderly couple came a nation as numerous as the stars.

The pilgrim faith of the patriarchs is studied below.

[[v17|By faith, Abraham, being tested, offered up Isaac. Yes, he who had gladly received the promises was offering up his only born son]], [[v18|to whom it was said, “Your offspring will be accounted as from Isaac,”]] [[v19|concluding that God is able to raise up even from the dead. Figuratively speaking, he also did receive him back from the dead]]. God's command to offer Isaac (Genesis 22) seemed to contradict His promise that Abraham's descendants would come through Isaac. Abraham resolved the tension by trusting that God could raise Isaac from the dead. In a sense, he did receive Isaac back from death when God provided a ram instead. This story foreshadows God's own Son, offered and raised.

### Faith Looking Forward (11:20–22)

[[v20|By faith Isaac blessed Jacob and Esau, even concerning things to come]]. [[v21|By faith Jacob, when he was dying, blessed each of the sons of Joseph, and worshiped, leaning on the top of his staff]]. [[v22|By faith Joseph, when his end was near, made mention of the departure of the children of Israel, and gave instructions concerning his bones]]. At the end of their lives, the patriarchs looked forward to God's future. Joseph, though he had become a ruler in Egypt, asked that his bones be carried to the promised land (Genesis 50:24–25), trusting God would fulfill His promise.

### The Faith of Moses and Israel (11:23–31)

[[v23|By faith Moses, when he was born, was hidden for three months by his parents, because they saw that he was a beautiful child; and they were not afraid of the king’s commandment]]. Moses' parents defied Pharaoh's order to kill Hebrew baby boys (Exodus 1:22–2:2). Their faith gave them courage to risk their lives.

[[v24|By faith Moses, when he had grown up, refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter]], [[v25|choosing rather to share ill treatment with God’s people than to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a time]], [[v26|considering the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures of Egypt; for he looked to the reward]]. Moses could have enjoyed the wealth and status of Egypt's royal court. Instead, he identified with the enslaved Israelites. "The reproach of Christ" means the kind of disgrace that God's anointed people suffer, a disgrace Christ Himself would later bear fully. Moses valued it more than Egypt's treasures, because he looked to God's reward.

[[v27|By faith he left Egypt, not fearing the wrath of the king; for he endured, as seeing him who is invisible]]. Moses persevered because he saw, by faith, the invisible God. [[v28|By faith he kept the Passover and the sprinkling of the blood, that the destroyer of the firstborn should not touch them]]. The Passover lamb's blood protected Israel, pointing ahead to Christ, our Passover (1 Corinthians 5:7).

[[v29|By faith they passed through the Red Sea as on dry land. When the Egyptians tried to do so, they were swallowed up]]. [[v30|By faith the walls of Jericho fell down after they had been encircled for seven days]]. [[v31|By faith Rahab the prostitute didn’t perish with those who were disobedient, having received the spies in peace]]. Rahab was a Canaanite and a prostitute, an outsider in every way. Yet she trusted Israel's God, sheltered the spies, and was saved (Joshua 2; 6:22–25). She became an ancestor of King David and of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). Her inclusion shows that God welcomes anyone who turns to Him in faith.

### Triumph and Suffering (11:32–40)

[[v32|What more shall I say? For the time would fail me if I told of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel, and the prophets—]]. The author runs out of time to tell every story. Notice that some of these figures had serious flaws: Gideon was fearful, Samson was impulsive and immoral, and Jephthah made a rash and tragic vow. Their inclusion does not mean their sins are approved. It shows that God works through imperfect people who, at key moments, trusted Him.

[[v33|who through faith subdued kingdoms, worked out righteousness, obtained promises, stopped the mouths of lions]], [[v34|quenched the power of fire, escaped the edge of the sword, from weakness were made strong, grew mighty in war, and caused foreign armies to flee]]. These phrases recall victories and rescues: Daniel in the lions' den (Daniel 6), his friends in the fiery furnace (Daniel 3), and many others. The other side of faith's story, suffering without rescue, is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Hebrews 11:1 — The Assurance of Things Hoped For

verse: Hebrews 11:1

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes what faith is. It is not a complete definition, but it captures the essence of faith as Hebrews presents it.

"Now faith is assurance of things hoped for." "Assurance" translates a word that can mean foundation, confidence, or even a title deed, a legal document guaranteeing ownership. Faith gives substance and certainty to what believers hope for. Believers do not yet possess the fullness of God's promises, but faith makes them confident those promises are real and will be fulfilled.

"Proof of things not seen." "Proof" means conviction or evidence. Faith is convinced of realities that cannot be seen with physical eyes: God Himself, Christ in heaven, the future kingdom, and God's unseen work in the present.

Faith, then, looks in two directions: forward to what is promised but not yet received, and upward to what is real but not yet seen.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Faith in Hebrews is not blind belief or wishful thinking. It is confident trust in God, based on His character and His word. The heroes of this chapter believed God because they knew Him to be faithful (v11). Their faith rested on God's promises, which Hebrews 6:18 calls unchangeable.

Faith is also not merely intellectual agreement. In every example in this chapter, faith leads to action: offering, building, leaving, obeying, refusing, choosing. Faith that does not act is not the kind of faith Hebrews celebrates. James makes the same point: faith without works is dead (James 2:17).

Paul expresses a similar thought in 2 Corinthians 4:18: [[2 Corinthians 4:18|we don't look at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen. For the things which are seen are temporal, but the things which are not seen are eternal]]. Faith fixes its eyes on what lasts.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can ask what they are hoping for and whether their hope rests on God's promises. Faith gives them confidence that what God has promised, forgiveness, His presence, resurrection, and eternal life, is real and certain.

Faith also helps believers live in light of unseen realities. Though they cannot see God, they can trust that He is present, active, and good. This trust can shape decisions, give courage, and bring peace.

Finally, believers can let their faith lead to action. Like the people in this chapter, they can obey God's call, make sacrifices, and choose faithfulness, trusting in what they cannot yet see.

### Hebrews 11:6 — Without Faith It Is Impossible to Please God

verse: Hebrews 11:6

#### Simple Explanation

This verse explains why Enoch pleased God (v5) and states a principle for everyone.

"Without faith it is impossible to be well pleasing to him." No one can please God without faith. Good deeds, religious activities, or moral efforts that do not come from trust in God cannot please Him. Faith is essential.

"For he who comes to God must believe that he exists." The first requirement is to believe that God is real. Approaching God begins with trusting that He is there.

"And that he is a rewarder of those who seek him." The second requirement is to believe that God is good, that He responds to those who genuinely seek Him. God is not indifferent or hostile. He rewards those who come to Him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that faith is relational. It is not merely believing facts about God but coming to Him, seeking Him, and trusting Him. The two beliefs described here, that God exists and that He rewards seekers, are the foundation of a relationship with Him.

The idea that God rewards those who seek Him runs throughout Scripture. God said through Jeremiah, [[Jeremiah 29:13|You shall seek me, and find me, when you search for me with all your heart]]. Jesus said, [[Matthew 7:7|Seek, and you will find]]. The greatest reward is God Himself. He told Abraham, [[Genesis 15:1|I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward]].

Faith pleases God because it honors Him. It takes God at His word, trusts His character, and depends on Him. Unbelief, by contrast, treats God as unreliable or unimportant.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites everyone to seek God, believing that He is real and that He responds. For those who are searching or doubting, it offers hope: God rewards those who sincerely seek Him.

For believers, this verse reminds them that pleasing God is not about religious performance but about trusting Him. Their everyday trust, in prayer, in obedience, in waiting, pleases Him.

Finally, believers can grow in faith by continuing to seek God through prayer, Scripture, and worship, confident that He will reward them with Himself.

### Hebrews 11:13–16 — Strangers and Pilgrims Seeking a Better Country

verse: Hebrews 11:13-16

#### Simple Explanation

"These all died in faith, not having received the promises." Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Sarah died without seeing the full fulfillment of God's promises. They never saw their descendants become a great nation or possess the land fully.

"But having seen them and embraced them from afar." Like travelers glimpsing a distant city on the horizon, they saw the promises by faith and welcomed them.

"And having confessed that they were strangers and pilgrims on the earth." They openly acknowledged that this world was not their true home. Abraham called himself [[Genesis 23:4|a stranger and a foreigner]], and Jacob spoke of his life as a pilgrimage (Genesis 47:9).

Verse 14: "For those who say such things make it clear that they are seeking a country of their own." By calling themselves strangers, they showed that they were looking for a homeland.

Verse 15: "If indeed they had been thinking of that country from which they went out, they would have had enough time to return." They could have gone back to Mesopotamia. But they did not, because they were looking for something better.

Verse 16: "But now they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one." Their hope reached beyond any earthly land to a heavenly home.

"Therefore God is not ashamed of them, to be called their God." God proudly identified Himself as [[Exodus 3:6|the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob]].

"For he has prepared a city for them." God has a home ready for His people.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses describe the pilgrim nature of the life of faith. Believers live in this world but are not fully at home here. Their true citizenship is in heaven (Philippians 3:20). Peter calls believers [[1 Peter 2:11|foreigners and pilgrims]]. This identity frees them from clinging too tightly to earthly security and helps them endure hardship, knowing that something better awaits.

The fact that these patriarchs died without receiving the promises does not mean their faith failed. Faith does not require seeing the fulfillment. It trusts God's promises even when they lie beyond one's lifetime.

"God is not ashamed of them" is a beautiful phrase. These were imperfect people, yet God was glad to be called their God, because they trusted Him. The same is true for believers today. Hebrews 2:11 says Jesus is not ashamed to call them His brothers and sisters.

The "city" God has prepared is described at the end of Scripture as the new Jerusalem, coming down from heaven, where God dwells with His people (Revelation 21:2–3).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can hold earthly things loosely, recognizing that this world is not their final home. This perspective can bring freedom from materialism, anxiety about status, and fear of loss.

At the same time, being pilgrims does not mean withdrawing from the world. Abraham lived among the Canaanites, blessed others, and engaged with his neighbors. Believers can work, love, and serve faithfully here while looking forward to their true home.

For those who are waiting for God's promises and have not yet seen them fulfilled, these verses bring encouragement. The great heroes of faith also waited, and many died before seeing the fulfillment. God is faithful, and His promises are sure.

### Hebrews 11:35–38 — Of Whom the World Was Not Worthy

verse: Hebrews 11:35-38

#### Simple Explanation

After describing faith's victories (vv33–35a), the author turns to another side of faith's story. "Women received their dead by resurrection." This recalls the widow of Zarephath and the Shunammite woman, whose sons were raised by Elijah and Elisha (1 Kings 17:17–24; 2 Kings 4:18–37).

"Others were tortured, not accepting their deliverance, that they might obtain a better resurrection." Some people of faith were not rescued. They chose torture rather than deny God, because they trusted in a better resurrection, eternal life with God. This may recall the Jewish martyrs during the persecution under Antiochus IV in the second century B.C., whose stories were well known.

Verse 36: "Others were tried by mocking and scourging, yes, moreover by bonds and imprisonment." Jeremiah was beaten and imprisoned (Jeremiah 20:2; 37:15).

Verse 37: "They were stoned." Zechariah son of Jehoiada was stoned in the temple court (2 Chronicles 24:20–21). "They were sawn apart." Jewish tradition says the prophet Isaiah was killed this way. "They were tempted. They were slain with the sword." "They went around in sheep skins and in goat skins; being destitute, afflicted, ill-treated." Prophets like Elijah lived in poverty and hardship.

Verse 38: "of whom the world was not worthy." The world rejected and mistreated them, but in reality, the world was not worthy of such people. "Wandering in deserts, mountains, caves, and the holes of the earth." Many faithful people lived as fugitives.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses are a vital part of the chapter. They show that faith does not always lead to visible success or deliverance in this life. Some people of faith conquered kingdoms and escaped death; others were tortured and killed. Both groups had genuine faith. God's faithfulness is not measured by whether He rescues His people from every hardship in this life.

This truth corrects any teaching that suggests faith always produces health, wealth, or victory. Jesus Himself, the supreme example of faith, was crucified. Many of His followers have suffered and died for Him. Faith trusts God both in deliverance and in suffering, because its hope is ultimately in "a better resurrection."

"Of whom the world was not worthy" is a powerful reversal of values. The world may despise those who suffer for their faith, but in God's eyes, they are the truly honored ones.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers who are suffering, even after praying for deliverance, can find comfort here. Their suffering does not mean their faith is weak or that God has abandoned them. Many of the greatest believers in history suffered greatly.

These verses also call believers to remember and pray for persecuted Christians around the world, many of whom endure imprisonment, violence, and loss for their faith. They stand in this line of faithful witnesses.

Finally, these verses invite believers to see their lives in light of eternity. A "better resurrection" awaits. Whatever they lose for Christ's sake in this life will be more than restored in the life to come.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Hebrews 11 teaches what faith is and what it looks like. Faith is the assurance of things hoped for and the conviction of things not seen. It trusts God's promises before they are fulfilled and believes in unseen realities. Without faith it is impossible to please God, because whoever comes to Him must believe that He exists and rewards those who seek Him.

The chapter shows faith in action across many generations. Abel offered a better sacrifice; Enoch walked with God; Noah built the ark; Abraham left his home without knowing where he was going and offered Isaac, trusting God to raise the dead; Sarah trusted God's promise; Moses chose suffering with God's people over Egypt's treasures; Israel crossed the Red Sea; and Rahab, an outsider, was saved by faith. These were imperfect people, but they trusted God at crucial moments.

Many of them died without receiving what was promised. They saw it from a distance and lived as strangers and pilgrims, seeking a better, heavenly country. God was not ashamed to be called their God, and He has prepared a city for them. Believers today can live with the same pilgrim hope, holding earthly things loosely while serving faithfully in the world.

Finally, the chapter honestly acknowledges that faith does not always bring deliverance in this life. Some conquered kingdoms and escaped death; others were tortured, imprisoned, and killed, people of whom the world was not worthy. Both groups had true faith. All were commended, and all await the fulfillment of God's promises together with believers today. This great cloud of witnesses calls believers to keep trusting God, whatever comes.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord God, thank You for the faith You give, the assurance of things hoped for and the conviction of things not seen. Help me to believe that You exist and that You reward those who seek You. Give me faith like Abraham's, to obey when I do not know where You are leading. Give me faith like Moses', to choose faithfulness to You over the passing pleasures of sin. Help me to live as a stranger and pilgrim on earth, seeking the better country You have prepared. When I wait for promises I have not yet seen fulfilled, keep me trusting. When I face hardship that You do not remove, give me faith to endure, looking to the better resurrection. Strengthen Your persecuted people around the world, of whom the world is not worthy. Thank You that You are not ashamed to be called our God. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
