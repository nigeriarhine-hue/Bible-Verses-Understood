# Mark 3
theme: Hard Hearts, Chosen Friends, and the True Family of Jesus
main: Jesus heals a man's withered hand on the Sabbath, grieved and angered by the hard hearts of those watching to accuse Him, and they begin plotting His death. As crowds pour in from every direction, He appoints twelve apostles to be with Him and to be sent out. His own family thinks He is out of His mind, and scribes from Jerusalem say He works by Satan's power, but Jesus shows that He has come to bind the strong man and declares that whoever does God's will is His true family.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 3 completes the series of five conflict stories that began in Mark 2. Jesus had forgiven sins, eaten with tax collectors, explained why His disciples did not fast, and defended them for picking grain on the Sabbath. Each time, religious leaders objected. In the first scene of chapter 3, the opposition reaches a turning point: the Pharisees join with their political rivals to plan Jesus' death.

The rest of the chapter shows two very different circles around Jesus. On the one hand, huge crowds come to Him, and He chooses twelve men to be His closest companions. On the other hand, His own family misunderstands Him, and religious authorities from Jerusalem accuse Him of being in league with the devil. Mark uses a favorite technique here, called a "sandwich," in which he begins one story, interrupts it with another, and then finishes the first. The account of Jesus' family (vv. 20–21, 31–35) surrounds the accusation by the scribes (vv. 22–30), inviting readers to compare the two kinds of misunderstanding.

### The Man With the Withered Hand (3:1–6)

Jesus went into a synagogue on the Sabbath, where there was a man with a withered, possibly paralyzed hand. Some people were watching Jesus closely to see whether He would heal on the Sabbath, so that they could accuse Him. Mark later identifies them as Pharisees (v. 6). In their view, healing a non-life-threatening condition was work that should wait until after the Sabbath.

Jesus told the man, [[v3|Stand up]], literally "rise into the middle," bringing him before everyone. Then He asked the watchers, [[v4|Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good or to do harm? To save a life or to kill?]] The question exposed the real issue. Failing to do good when one can is a form of harm. And the question carried an irony: Jesus was seeking to heal on the Sabbath, while they were seeking a way to destroy Him. They remained silent.

Jesus looked around at them [[v5|with anger, being grieved at the hardening of their hearts]]. This is studied below. He told the man to stretch out his hand, and it was completely restored.

Immediately the Pharisees went out and began plotting with the Herodians how to destroy Jesus. The Herodians were supporters of the Herod family, especially Herod Antipas, who ruled Galilee under Roman authority. The Pharisees normally opposed them, since they saw cooperation with Rome as compromise. Their alliance against Jesus shows how threatened both groups felt. Herod had already arrested John the Baptist, so this was a dangerous partnership.

### Crowds From Every Region (3:7–12)

Jesus withdrew with His disciples to the Sea of Galilee. A huge crowd followed Him, not only from Galilee but from Judea, Jerusalem, Idumea (the region far to the south), across the Jordan, and around Tyre and Sidon (Gentile cities to the northwest). Mark's list covers nearly every direction, showing that Jesus' reputation had spread throughout the land and beyond Jewish territory.

The crowds were so large and eager that Jesus asked His disciples to keep a small boat ready so He would not be crushed. People with diseases pushed forward to touch Him. Whenever unclean spirits saw Him, they fell down and cried out, [[v11|You are the Son of God!]] Jesus sternly ordered them not to make Him known. He did not want His identity proclaimed by demons, nor did He want to be known simply as a miracle-worker before people understood His mission.

### The Appointment of the Twelve (3:13–19)

Jesus went up on a mountainside and called to Himself those He wanted, and they came. He appointed twelve, [[v14|that they might be with him, and that he might send them out to preach]], and to have authority to drive out demons. This is studied below.

Mark lists the Twelve. Simon was given the name Peter, which means "rock." James and John, the sons of Zebedee, were named [[v17|Boanerges, which means, Sons of Thunder]], perhaps because of their fiery personalities; in Luke 9:54 they offered to call down fire on a Samaritan village. The others were Andrew, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas, James the son of Alphaeus, Thaddaeus, Simon the Zealot, and Judas Iscariot, [[v19|who also betrayed him]]. The group included fishermen, a tax collector (Matthew), and a Zealot (Simon), whose political loyalties were opposed. Jesus formed them into one community.

The number twelve is significant. Israel had twelve tribes, descended from the twelve sons of Jacob. By choosing twelve apostles, Jesus was signaling the renewal of God's people. Mark's mention of Judas's betrayal at the very beginning reminds readers that even in this chosen circle, the shadow of the cross was present.

### Jesus' Family Thinks He Is Out of His Mind (3:20–21)

Jesus went home, and again the crowd gathered so that He and His disciples could not even eat. When His family heard about it, they went to take charge of Him, because they said, [[v21|He is insane]]. The word "friends" in the WEB here refers to those close to Him, most likely His family, as verse 31 shows. They probably thought He had become overwhelmed, unbalanced, or dangerously caught up in His mission. Their concern may have been sincere, but they did not understand who He was. John 7:5 tells us that at this time even His brothers did not believe in Him.

### The Scribes' Accusation and the Strong Man (3:22–30)

Meanwhile, scribes who had come down from Jerusalem, likely official representatives of the religious authorities, said, [[v22|He has Beelzebul]], and that He cast out demons by the prince of demons. They could not deny His power, so they attributed it to Satan.

Jesus called them over and answered with parables. How can Satan drive out Satan? A kingdom or household divided against itself cannot stand. If Satan were fighting against himself, his rule would be coming to an end. Then Jesus gave a short parable that explains His exorcisms: [[v27|But no one can enter into the house of the strong man to plunder unless he first binds the strong man; then he will plunder his house]]. Satan is the strong man who has held people captive. Jesus is the stronger one who has come to bind him and set his captives free. His victory over demons shows that He is more powerful than Satan. This echoes Isaiah 49:24–25, where God promises to take captives from the mighty.

Jesus then warned that all sins and blasphemies people commit can be forgiven, but whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven. Mark explains why Jesus said this: [[v30|because they said, “He has an unclean spirit.”]] The scribes were looking at the clear work of God's Spirit in freeing people from evil and calling it the work of Satan. (This warning is studied in depth at Matthew 12:31–32.)

### Jesus' True Family (3:31–35)

Now Jesus' mother and brothers arrived and stood outside, sending someone in to call Him. The crowd sitting around Jesus told Him that His mother, brothers, and sisters were outside looking for Him. Jesus asked, [[v33|Who are my mother and my brothers?]] Looking at those seated in a circle around Him, He said, [[v34|Behold, my mother and my brothers!]] and then explained who belongs to His family. This is studied below.

Notice the contrast in this scene. Jesus' family stood outside, trying to take Him away. His disciples sat inside, around Him, listening. Physical closeness to Jesus did not guarantee understanding; responding to Him did.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 3:5 — Anger and Grief at Hard Hearts

verse: Mark 3:5

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes Jesus' emotional response in the synagogue on the Sabbath. A man with a withered hand was present, and the Pharisees were watching to see whether Jesus would heal him so they could accuse Him of breaking the Sabbath. Jesus brought the man into the middle of the room and asked the watchers a direct question: [[v4|Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good or to do harm? To save a life or to kill?]] They refused to answer.

Mark then gives us a rare glimpse into Jesus' feelings. He [[v5|looked around at them with anger]]. Jesus made eye contact with each of them, and His anger was visible. But Mark immediately adds, [[v5|being grieved at the hardening of their hearts]]. His anger was mixed with sorrow. The word "hardening" describes something that has become stiff, insensitive, or calloused. Their hearts had become so focused on rules and on defeating Jesus that they had no compassion for a suffering man.

Matthew and Luke tell this story but do not mention Jesus' anger and grief. Mark, likely drawing on Peter's eyewitness memory, preserves this emotional detail.

Then Jesus said to the man, [[v5|Stretch out your hand]]. He did not touch him or do anything that could be considered "work." He simply spoke, and the man obeyed. As he stretched out his hand, it was fully restored. The man's obedience is part of the miracle; he did what he could not do until Jesus commanded it.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus experienced real human emotions, including anger. His anger was not sinful. It was a righteous response to cruelty disguised as religion. Paul writes, [[Ephesians 4:26|Be angry, and don’t sin]]. Jesus' anger was directed at injustice and hardness, not at personal offense, and it was joined with grief, a sorrow that these people were missing God's heart.

The verse also reveals the danger of a hardened heart. The Pharisees were religious, knowledgeable, and committed, yet they could watch a suffering man and care only about catching Jesus in a violation. Hardness of heart is a recurring warning in Scripture. Psalm 95:8 says, [[Psalm 95:8|Don’t harden your heart]]. In Mark, even the disciples will be warned about hardened hearts (Mark 6:52; 8:17). It is a danger for everyone, not just for Jesus' opponents.

Finally, the healing shows God's purpose for the Sabbath and for all His commands: to do good and to save life. Jesus' question exposed the real choice. Refusing to help when one is able is not neutral; it is a failure to do good.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine their own hearts for hardness. It is possible to know a great deal about God and to be active in religious life while becoming calloused toward the needs of others. Believers can ask God to soften their hearts and to make them sensitive to suffering, especially when rules or routines make it easy to look away.

It also shows that some anger is appropriate. Believers can feel righteous anger at cruelty, injustice, and the mistreatment of vulnerable people. But Jesus' example shows that such anger should be joined with grief, directed at wrongdoing rather than personal offense, and expressed in ways that bring healing rather than revenge.

Finally, Jesus' question, "Is it lawful to do good or to do harm?", challenges believers to act when they can help. Choosing not to help someone in need is not truly neutral. Believers can look for opportunities to do good, even when it is inconvenient or when others criticize them for it.

### Mark 3:14 — Chosen to Be With Him and Sent Out

verse: Mark 3:14

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes the appointment of the twelve apostles. Mark says that Jesus went up on a mountainside and [[v13|called to himself those whom he wanted]]. The initiative was entirely His. These men did not apply or compete for the position; Jesus chose them.

He [[v14|appointed twelve]]. The number deliberately recalls the twelve tribes of Israel. Jesus was forming the core of a renewed people of God.

Mark gives two purposes for their appointment. First, [[v14|that they might be with him]]. Before they did anything for Jesus, they were called simply to be with Him. They would travel with Him, eat with Him, listen to His teaching, watch how He treated people, see His miracles, and learn from His example. Discipleship in that culture meant living closely with one's teacher, learning both from words and from life.

Second, [[v14|that he might send them out to preach]]. The word "apostle" means "one who is sent." Jesus would send them as His messengers to proclaim the kingdom of God. Verse 15 adds that they would have authority to drive out demons. Their mission would continue His own work.

The order matters. Being with Jesus comes first; being sent comes second. Their effectiveness in preaching and ministry would grow out of their relationship with Him. Later, after Pentecost, the religious leaders noticed Peter and John's boldness and [[Acts 4:13|recognized that they had been with Jesus]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of discipleship: a relationship with Jesus that leads to mission. Jesus did not choose the Twelve primarily as employees or students of a program. He chose them to share His life. Their service would flow from their companionship with Him.

It also shows that God's call is based on His choice, not human merit. Jesus called "those whom he wanted." The Twelve were ordinary men, including fishermen, a tax collector, and a political zealot. Even Judas, who would betray Him, was among them. God's call rests on His grace and purposes, not on the qualifications of those He calls.

The two purposes, being with Jesus and being sent by Him, apply to all believers, not just the apostles. Jesus' final words in Matthew combine these same themes: He sends His followers to make disciples, and He promises, "I am with you always" (Matthew 28:19–20). The apostles had a unique, foundational role, but every believer is called into relationship with Christ and into His mission.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to prioritize being with Jesus. Service, ministry, and activity can easily crowd out time with Him. Yet fruitful ministry grows from close relationship. Believers can spend time in prayer, in Scripture, and in worship, simply being in His presence, before and alongside their service.

It also reminds believers that they are sent. Being with Jesus is not meant to end in private spirituality. He sends His followers into the world to share the good news and to continue His work of compassion and freedom. Every believer has a place in that mission, whether in their home, workplace, community, or beyond.

Finally, this verse offers encouragement to those who feel unqualified. Jesus chose ordinary people and shaped them by being with them. Believers can trust that the same Lord who called them will also equip them as they spend time with Him and step out in obedience.

### Mark 3:35 — Whoever Does God's Will Is My Family

verse: Mark 3:35

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes a scene that began earlier in the chapter. In verse 21, Jesus' family heard that He was so busy with the crowds that He could not even eat, and they set out to take charge of Him, saying He had lost His mind. Mark then told of the scribes accusing Jesus of working by Satan's power. Now Jesus' mother and brothers arrive. They stand outside the crowded house and send someone in to call Him.

The people sitting around Jesus told Him, [[v32|Behold, your mother, your brothers, and your sisters are outside looking for you]]. In that culture, family loyalty was extremely important. Many expected Jesus to stop what He was doing and go to His family.

Instead, Jesus asked, "Who are my mother and my brothers?" Then He looked around at those seated in a circle around Him, the people listening to His teaching, and said, "Behold, my mother and my brothers!" Then He explained: [[v35|For whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother]].

Jesus was not rejecting His mother and brothers. He cared for His mother even from the cross (John 19:26–27), and His brother James later became a leader in the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13; Galatians 1:19). Rather, Jesus was redefining family in the kingdom of God. The deepest family bond is formed not by blood but by doing God's will. Notice that He added "sister," including women among His disciples, which was significant in a culture where religious teachers did not usually have female students.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that God's kingdom creates a new family. Those who follow Jesus become brothers and sisters to Him and to one another. Throughout the New Testament, believers call each other "brothers" and "sisters," reflecting this new identity. Paul describes the church as [[Ephesians 2:19|the household of God]].

The verse also shows that belonging to Jesus' family is defined by response, not by status. His physical family, standing outside, had not yet understood or accepted Him. The crowd sitting at His feet, listening and responding, were closer to Him. Doing God's will is the mark of His family. This includes believing in Jesus, whom God sent, and following His teaching.

This teaching is especially meaningful for those who have lost natural family ties or whose families reject them because of their faith. In God's family, they find a home. At the same time, it does not cancel the responsibility to honor and care for one's natural family, which Jesus affirmed elsewhere (Mark 7:9–13).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to see the church as their family. Fellow believers are brothers and sisters, and relationships within the church can reflect the loyalty, care, and love of a family. Believers can welcome others, share their lives, support one another in need, and treat each other with the commitment family members show.

It also encourages those who feel alone or rejected. Whether because of family conflict, loss, or isolation, many people lack close family relationships. In Christ, they belong to a larger family. Churches can be places where such people find genuine belonging.

Finally, this verse calls believers to do God's will. Being in Jesus' family is not about outward association but about listening to Him and responding with obedience. Believers can sit at His feet like those in the crowd, learning from Him and seeking to do what pleases the Father.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 3 shows how people respond to Jesus in different ways, and each response invites reflection. The Pharisees watched Jesus with hard hearts, more interested in catching Him than in seeing a suffering man healed. Believers can ask God to keep their hearts soft and compassionate, and to help them choose to do good when they have the opportunity, even when religious habits or the criticism of others make it difficult.

The crowds came from every direction to see Jesus, but many came only for what He could do for them. The Twelve were called into a deeper relationship: to be with Him and to be sent by Him. Believers are invited into this same pattern of discipleship. Spending time with Jesus through prayer and Scripture is the foundation for serving Him in the world.

Jesus' family misunderstood Him, and the scribes slandered Him. Both responses show that closeness to religious tradition or even to Jesus Himself does not guarantee understanding. Believers can stay humble and teachable, refusing to dismiss God's work simply because it does not fit their expectations. The parable of the strong man also gives hope: Jesus has bound the power of evil and sets captives free. No bondage is beyond His power.

Finally, Jesus' declaration that whoever does God's will is His family gives believers a new identity and a new community. They belong to God's family, with brothers and sisters in Christ. This calls them to love one another, welcome the lonely, and live as members of a household centered on doing God's will.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for doing good and saving life, even when others were watching to accuse You. Soften any hardness in my heart, and give me Your compassion for those who are suffering. Help me to feel grief and righteous anger at what harms people, and to respond with healing rather than revenge.

Thank You for calling ordinary people to be with You and to be sent by You. Draw me close to You, so that my service flows from time spent in Your presence. Thank You that You have bound the strong man and that You set captives free. Keep me humble and open to Your work, even when it surprises me. Thank You for making me part of Your family. Help me to do the Father's will and to love Your people as my brothers and sisters. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
