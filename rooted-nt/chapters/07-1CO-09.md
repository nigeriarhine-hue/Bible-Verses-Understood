# 1 Corinthians 9
theme: Giving Up Rights for the Sake of the Gospel and Running to Win
main: Continuing his teaching on freedom and love, Paul offers his own example. As an apostle, he has every right to receive financial support from the churches, a right supported by common sense, the Law of Moses, and the Lord's own command. Yet he has chosen not to use this right, enduring hardship so as not to hinder the gospel. He feels compelled to preach and finds his reward in offering the gospel free of charge. Though free, he has made himself a servant to all, becoming all things to all people so that by all means he might save some. Finally, using the image of athletes, he urges believers to run with purpose and self-discipline for an imperishable prize.

## Chapter Background and Story

1 Corinthians 9 may seem at first like a change of subject. In chapter 8, Paul was discussing food offered to idols. In chapter 10, he will return to that topic. But chapter 9 is not a digression. Paul is giving his own life as an example of the principle he taught in chapter 8: believers should be willing to give up their rights for the sake of others.

The chapter also suggests that some Corinthians were questioning Paul's authority as an apostle. Perhaps they thought that because he worked as a tentmaker and did not accept their money, he was not a real apostle, or that he was less important than other leaders who received support. Paul answers by showing that he had every right to support, but chose not to use it for the sake of the gospel.

### Paul's Rights as an Apostle (9:1–14)

Paul begins with a series of questions: [[v1|Am I not free? Am I not an apostle? Haven’t I seen Jesus Christ, our Lord? Aren’t you my work in the Lord?]] Paul had seen the risen Christ on the road to Damascus (Acts 9), a key qualification for an apostle (Acts 1:21–22). And the Corinthian church itself was proof of his apostleship: [[v2|for you are the seal of my apostleship in the Lord]].

Paul then lists rights that apostles have. [[v4|Have we no right to eat and to drink?]] Apostles had the right to be provided with food and drink by the churches. [[v5|Have we no right to take along a wife who is a believer, even as the rest of the apostles, and the brothers of the Lord, and Cephas?]] Other apostles, Jesus' brothers, and Peter traveled with their wives, supported by the churches. This verse tells us that Peter was married (compare Mark 1:30). [[v6|Or have only Barnabas and I no right to not work?]] Paul and Barnabas worked to support themselves, but they had the right not to.

Paul supports these rights with examples from everyday life: [[v7|What soldier ever serves at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard, and doesn’t eat of its fruit? Or who feeds a flock, and doesn’t drink from the flock’s milk?]] Workers naturally benefit from their work.

He also appeals to Scripture: [[v9|For it is written in the law of Moses, “You shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain.”]] (Deuteronomy 25:4). An ox treading grain should be allowed to eat some of it. If God cares about oxen, how much more does He care about His workers? [[v10|Yes, it was written for our sake]]. [[v11|If we sowed to you spiritual things, is it a great thing if we reap your fleshly things?]]

He points to the temple: those who served in the temple received food from the offerings (Numbers 18:8–31). And most importantly: [[v14|Even so the Lord ordained that those who proclaim the Good News should live from the Good News]]. Jesus Himself taught this (Luke 10:7; Matthew 10:10). Paul makes clear that it is right for churches to support those who serve them.

Yet Paul did not use this right, as studied below.

### Paul's Reason for Refusing Support (9:15–18)

[[v15|But I have used none of these things, and I don’t write these things that it may be done so in my case; for I would rather die, than that anyone should make my boasting void]]. Paul was not writing to ask for money. He would rather die than lose his "boast," his joy in preaching without charge. His sense of calling is studied below.

[[v18|What then is my reward? That when I preach the Good News, I may present the Good News of Christ without charge, so as not to abuse my authority in the Good News]]. Paul's reward was the joy of offering the gospel freely, making clear that he was not in it for money. In Corinth, where traveling teachers often charged fees and many were suspected of greed, this mattered greatly.

### All Things to All People (9:19–23)

[[v19|For though I was free from all, I brought myself under bondage to all, that I might gain the more]]. Paul was free, not bound by anyone. Yet he voluntarily made himself a servant to everyone, in order to win more people to Christ.

He gives examples: [[v20|To the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might gain Jews; to those who are under the law, as under the law, that I might gain those who are under the law]]. Among Jews, Paul observed Jewish customs, such as when he circumcised Timothy (Acts 16:3) or took a vow (Acts 21:26). [[v21|to those who are without law, as without law (not being without law toward God, but under law toward Christ), that I might win those who are without law]]. Among Gentiles, Paul did not insist on Jewish customs. But he was never lawless; he was always under the law of Christ, the law of love. His summary statement is studied below.

### Running to Win (9:24–27)

Paul closes with an image from athletics, studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### 1 Corinthians 9:12 — That We May Cause No Hindrance to the Gospel

verse: 1 Corinthians 9:12

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had just made a strong case that apostles have the right to receive financial support from the churches they serve. He used examples from soldiers, farmers, and shepherds, and he cited the Law of Moses. The Corinthians themselves had supported other teachers.

[[v12|If others partake of this right over you, don’t we yet more?]] Other teachers had received support from the Corinthian church. If they had this right, surely Paul, the founder of the church, had it even more.

[[v12|Nevertheless we didn’t use this right]]. But Paul and his co-workers had chosen not to use this right. They supported themselves through their own work, such as tentmaking.

[[v12|but we bear all things, that we may cause no hindrance to the Good News of Christ]]. They endured hardship, working long hours and sometimes going without, so that nothing would get in the way of the gospel. In a city where many teachers charged fees and were suspected of greed, Paul did not want anyone to think he preached for money. He wanted the gospel to be clearly free.

The word "hindrance" pictures something that cuts into a road and blocks the way forward. Paul did not want anything, even his legitimate rights, to block people from coming to Christ.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that the gospel is more important than personal rights. Paul had a genuine right to support, but he valued the progress of the gospel more. Jesus said, [[Mark 10:45|For the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many]].

It also shows that believers should consider how their actions affect their witness. Even legitimate actions can become obstacles to the gospel in certain situations. Paul wisely considered how his choices would be perceived.

Finally, the verse connects to the discussion of food offered to idols. Just as Paul gave up his right to support, believers should be willing to give up their right to eat certain foods if it would harm others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to consider whether any of their rights or freedoms are hindering the gospel. Believers can ask whether their behavior, lifestyle, or choices might make it harder for others to hear and receive the good news.

It also encourages Christian workers to maintain integrity with money. Paul's example shows the importance of avoiding even the appearance of greed. Ministers and ministries can be transparent and careful with finances so that the gospel is not discredited.

Finally, the verse affirms that churches should support those who serve them. Paul's choice was his own; he clearly taught that those who preach the gospel should be supported. Churches can generously care for their pastors and missionaries.

### 1 Corinthians 9:16 — Woe to Me If I Don't Preach

verse: 1 Corinthians 9:16

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had explained that he chose not to accept payment for preaching so that he could offer the gospel free of charge. Now he explains why preaching itself gave him nothing to boast about.

[[v16|For if I preach the Good News, I have nothing to boast about, for necessity is laid on me]]. Paul could not take credit for preaching, because he had no choice. God had laid it on him as a necessity. On the road to Damascus, Jesus had commissioned him to preach to the Gentiles (Acts 26:16–18). Preaching was not a career choice; it was a divine calling.

[[v16|but woe is to me if I don’t preach the Good News]]. If Paul failed to preach, he would be disobeying God's call. "Woe" is a strong word of warning, used by the prophets to describe coming judgment. Paul felt the weight of his calling so deeply that he could not imagine not preaching.

This echoes the experience of Old Testament prophets. Jeremiah said that God's word was like [[Jeremiah 20:9|a burning fire shut up in my bones]], and he could not hold it in. Amos said, [[Amos 3:8|The Lord GOD has spoken. Who can but prophesy?]].

Paul's reward, then, was not in preaching itself, which was his duty, but in preaching freely, giving up his right to payment (9:17–18).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that the gospel is a stewardship entrusted to believers. Paul writes in the next verse, [[v17|But if not of my own will, I have a stewardship entrusted to me]]. Believers do not own the gospel; they are entrusted with it and responsible to share it.

It also shows the compelling nature of God's call. When God calls someone, the call is not optional. Paul felt compelled to preach. Peter and John said, [[Acts 4:20|for we can’t help telling the things which we saw and heard]].

Finally, the verse shows that faithful service is not a reason for pride. Paul could not boast about preaching, because it was what God required. Jesus taught that servants who do what they are commanded should say, [[Luke 17:10|We are unworthy servants. We have done our duty]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to take their calling seriously. Every believer has been entrusted with the gospel and called to share it in some way. Believers can ask God to give them a sense of urgency about sharing Christ with others.

It also reminds believers that service is not a reason for pride. When believers serve faithfully, they are simply doing what God has called them to do. Their service is a response to grace, not a way to earn credit.

Finally, the verse encourages those called to ministry. Pastors, missionaries, and others in ministry may face discouragement. Remembering God's call can sustain them through difficult times.

### 1 Corinthians 9:22 — All Things to All People

verse: 1 Corinthians 9:22

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had explained that although he was free, he made himself a servant to all people in order to win more of them to Christ. He gave examples: among Jews, he lived as a Jew; among Gentiles, he lived as a Gentile, while always remaining under the law of Christ.

[[v22|To the weak I became as weak, that I might gain the weak]]. The "weak" here probably refers to believers with sensitive consciences, like those discussed in chapter 8. Paul limited his freedom for their sake, becoming like them so that he could help them.

[[v22|I have become all things to all men, that I may by all means save some]]. This is Paul's summary. He adapted himself to different people and cultures, so that by every possible means, he might bring some to salvation.

This does not mean Paul compromised the gospel or his moral convictions. He never became an idolater to reach idolaters or sinned to reach sinners. He remained "under law toward Christ" (9:21). What he adapted were customs, practices, and approaches that did not affect the truth of the gospel. He was flexible in method but firm in message.

[[v23|Now I do this for the sake of the Good News, that I may be a joint partaker of it]]. Paul did everything for the gospel's sake, so that he might share in its blessings with others.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the heart of missionary love. Paul was willing to set aside his own preferences, comfort, and culture to reach others. This is what Christ did in the incarnation: He became human to reach humanity. John writes, [[John 1:14|The Word became flesh, and lived among us]].

It also shows the importance of understanding others. To become "all things to all people," Paul had to understand different cultures, customs, and ways of thinking. Effective witness requires listening, learning, and empathy.

Finally, the verse shows that the goal of flexibility is salvation. Paul adapted "that I may by all means save some." His flexibility was not about being popular or comfortable; it was about bringing people to Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to adapt their approach to reach others. Believers can learn about the cultures, interests, and concerns of people around them, and they can find ways to connect with them, while remaining faithful to the gospel.

It also encourages believers to set aside personal preferences for the sake of others. Believers might change how they dress, what they eat, or how they spend their time in order to build relationships and remove barriers to the gospel.

Finally, the verse warns against compromise. Believers should be flexible in methods but never in the message. They can adapt to others without abandoning biblical truth or moral convictions.

### 1 Corinthians 9:24–27 — Run to Win the Imperishable Crown

verse: 1 Corinthians 9:24-27

#### Simple Explanation

Paul closes this chapter with an image the Corinthians knew well. Corinth hosted the Isthmian Games every two years, one of the great athletic festivals of the ancient world, second only to the Olympic Games. Athletes trained intensely for these events.

[[v24|Don’t you know that those who run in a race all run, but one receives the prize? Run like that, so that you may win]]. In a race, many runners compete, but only one wins. Paul is not saying that only one believer will be saved. He is saying that believers should run with the same determination as athletes who want to win.

[[v25|Every man who strives in the games exercises self-control in all things]]. Athletes in the Isthmian Games had to train for ten months and follow strict diets and disciplines. They gave up many pleasures to prepare for competition.

[[v25|Now they do it to receive a corruptible crown, but we an incorruptible]]. The winners of the Isthmian Games received a crown made of pine or wilted celery, which would soon wither. Yet athletes disciplined themselves intensely for this temporary prize. Believers run for an "incorruptible" crown, eternal life and reward from God, which will never fade (2 Timothy 4:8; 1 Peter 5:4).

[[v26|I therefore run like that, not aimlessly. I fight like that, not beating the air]]. Paul ran with purpose, not wandering aimlessly. He fought like a boxer who lands his punches, not one who swings wildly at the air.

[[v27|but I beat my body and bring it into submission, lest by any means, after I have preached to others, I myself should be disqualified]]. Paul disciplined himself, keeping his body and its desires under control. He did not want to preach to others and then fail to live what he preached. "Disqualified" was a term used for athletes who broke the rules and were rejected from the competition. Paul took seriously the need for self-discipline and faithfulness.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the Christian life requires discipline. Salvation is a free gift, but living the Christian life involves effort, self-control, and perseverance. Paul writes, [[1 Timothy 4:7|exercise yourself toward godliness]].

They also show the value of eternal rewards. Athletes discipline themselves for temporary prizes. Believers have far greater motivation: an eternal crown. Hebrews urges believers to [[Hebrews 12:1|run with perseverance the race that is set before us]].

Finally, these verses show Paul's humility and seriousness. Even the great apostle did not assume that he could relax. He knew the danger of preaching to others while neglecting his own walk with God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to run their race with purpose. Believers can set spiritual goals, such as growing in prayer, Bible reading, service, and character, and pursue them with determination.

They also encourage self-discipline. Believers can identify areas where they need greater self-control, such as their time, eating, spending, media use, or speech, and ask God for help in disciplining themselves.

Finally, these verses remind believers to keep their eyes on the eternal prize. When the Christian life feels difficult, believers can remember that they are running for a crown that will never fade.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

1 Corinthians 9 shows that believers should be willing to give up their rights for the sake of the gospel. Paul had every right to receive financial support, but he chose not to use it so that nothing would hinder the gospel. Believers can consider whether any of their rights, freedoms, or choices might be obstacles to others coming to Christ.

The chapter also affirms that churches should support those who serve them. Paul's arguments from common sense, the Law, and the Lord's command show that it is right for churches to care for their pastors and missionaries. Paul's choice not to accept support was his own, made for the sake of the gospel in his particular situation.

1 Corinthians 9 calls believers to become all things to all people, adapting their approach to reach others while remaining faithful to Christ. Believers can learn about the people around them, build bridges across cultural differences, and set aside their own preferences, all for the purpose of bringing people to salvation.

Finally, the chapter calls believers to run their race with purpose and self-discipline. Like athletes training for a prize, believers can discipline themselves for the incorruptible crown of eternal life. They can pursue spiritual growth with determination and guard against preaching to others while neglecting their own walk with God.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for setting aside Your rights and becoming a servant to save me. Help me to follow Your example, giving up my own rights and preferences when they would hinder others from knowing You. Give me Paul's sense of urgency about sharing the gospel, and help me never to treat it as optional. Make me flexible in how I reach others, willing to become all things to all people, while remaining faithful to Your truth. Bless and provide for those who serve Your church and carry Your gospel. Help me to run my race with purpose, not aimlessly, and to discipline myself for the crown that will never fade. Keep me from preaching to others while neglecting my own walk with You. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
