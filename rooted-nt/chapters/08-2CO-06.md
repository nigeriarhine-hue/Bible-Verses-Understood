# 2 Corinthians 6
theme: Now Is the Day of Salvation
main: Paul urges the Corinthians not to receive God's grace in vain, because now is the acceptable time and the day of salvation. He commends his ministry not by impressive credentials but by endurance in suffering and by a life of purity, kindness, and sincere love, describing himself in a series of striking paradoxes. He opens his heart to the Corinthians and asks them to open theirs in return, and he calls them to avoid partnerships that would compromise their identity as the temple of the living God, who promises to be their Father.

## Chapter Background and Story

Chapter 6 flows directly from the end of chapter 5. Paul had just described himself as an ambassador of Christ, through whom God pleads, "be reconciled to God" (2 Corinthians 5:20). Now he turns that appeal toward the Corinthians themselves. They had believed the gospel, but their relationship with Paul was strained, and some of their behavior suggested that the grace they had received was not shaping their lives as it should.

The chapter has three movements. First, Paul makes an urgent appeal and defends his ministry by describing what it has cost him (6:1–10). Second, he speaks with remarkable tenderness, asking the Corinthians to open their hearts to him (6:11–13). Third, he warns them against being bound together with unbelievers in ways that compromise their faith (6:14–18). The appeal for openness then resumes in 7:2, which leads some scholars to think 6:14–7:1 may be a section Paul wrote with special emphasis, set in the middle of his plea. Whatever the case, the passage fits Paul's larger concern: the Corinthians were drawn toward the values of their surrounding culture and toward teachers who reflected those values, and Paul wanted them wholly devoted to Christ.

### An Urgent Appeal (6:1–2)

[[v1|Working together, we entreat also that you do not receive the grace of God in vain]]. "Working together" probably means working together with God, as Paul said in 1 Corinthians 3:9. To receive grace "in vain" means to receive it without allowing it to accomplish its purpose. The urgency of "now" in verse 2 is studied below.

### A Ministry Proven in Hardship (6:3–10)

[[v3|We give no occasion of stumbling in anything, that our service may not be blamed]]. Paul was careful not to let his conduct become an obstacle to anyone believing the gospel. His opponents commended themselves by their eloquence and credentials. Paul commends himself in a completely different way.

He gives a long list, arranged in groups. First come the hardships: [[v4|in great endurance, in afflictions, in hardships, in distresses]], [[v5|in beatings, in imprisonments, in riots, in labors, in watchings, in fastings]]. Each of these was literal. Acts records Paul being beaten and imprisoned in Philippi (Acts 16:22–24) and facing riots in Ephesus (Acts 19:23–41). "Watchings" refers to sleepless nights, and "fastings" may include times of hunger from poverty as well as voluntary fasting.

Then come the qualities of character: [[v6|in pureness, in knowledge, in perseverance, in kindness, in the Holy Spirit, in sincere love]], and the resources of ministry: [[v7|in the word of truth, in the power of God, by the armor of righteousness on the right hand and on the left]]. A soldier carried a weapon in the right hand and a shield in the left. Paul was equipped on every side, not by worldly weapons but by righteousness. The list ends with a series of paradoxes, studied below.

### Open Your Hearts (6:11–13)

After this intense description, Paul's tone softens. [[v11|Our mouth is open to you, Corinthians. Our heart is enlarged]]. He has spoken frankly because he loves them. Addressing them by name, "Corinthians," is rare in Paul's letters and shows strong emotion. [[v12|You are not restricted by us, but you are restricted by your own affections]]. The problem is not a lack of love on Paul's side. The Corinthians have narrowed their hearts toward him. [[v13|Now in return—I speak as to my children—you also open your hearts]]. Paul speaks as a father who longs for his children's affection. This plea continues in 2 Corinthians 7:2.

### Do Not Be Unequally Yoked (6:14–18)

Paul then warns against close partnerships with unbelievers that would compromise faithfulness to Christ. He asks a series of rhetorical questions, each expecting the answer "none." [[v15|What agreement does Christ have with Belial?]] "Belial" was a name used in Jewish writings of the time for Satan, meaning "worthlessness." The command in verse 14 and the promises in verses 16–18 are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### 2 Corinthians 6:2 — Now Is the Day of Salvation

verse: 2 Corinthians 6:2

#### Simple Explanation

Paul quotes Isaiah 49:8, where God speaks to His Servant, promising to hear and help Him in the time of His favor. In Isaiah, this promise was connected with God's plan to restore Israel and bring salvation to the nations. Paul sees that promise fulfilled in Christ.

Then Paul adds his own urgent commentary: "Behold, now is the acceptable time. Behold, now is the day of salvation." The time Isaiah looked forward to has arrived. Christ has died and risen; the message of reconciliation is being proclaimed. The door of God's favor stands open.

The word "now" is the heart of the verse. Paul is pressing the Corinthians not to delay or drift. God's grace is not something to be postponed or treated casually. They have received it, and they need to respond to it fully in the present.

This appeal applies both to people who have never responded to God and to believers who have grown careless. For the first, now is the time to be reconciled to God. For the second, now is the time to let grace do its full work.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

In Isaiah 49, the Servant is the one through whom God will be a light to the nations (Isaiah 49:6). Paul and Barnabas applied that same chapter to their mission to the Gentiles (Acts 13:47). By quoting it here, Paul places the Corinthians, mostly Gentiles, within the great story of God's promises. They are living in the long-awaited era of salvation.

The "day of salvation" is not only a future day but a present season that began with Christ's coming. Jesus announced this at the start of His ministry when He read from Isaiah and said, "Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:21). The age of fulfillment has begun. Yet Scripture also teaches that this season will not last forever; Hebrews 3:15 warns, [[Hebrews 3:15|Today if you will hear his voice, don’t harden your hearts]]. God's patience is generous, but every person's opportunity is real and limited.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

It is very easy to postpone spiritual matters. People intend to deal with faith later, after a busy season, after a crisis passes, after they feel more ready. This verse gently but firmly insists that the right time is now. God's welcome is available today.

For believers, this verse speaks to postponed obedience: the apology not yet made, the habit not yet surrendered, the calling not yet answered. Grace is not meant to sit unused. Today is a good day to respond to whatever God has been placing on the heart.

### 2 Corinthians 6:8–10 — Sorrowful Yet Always Rejoicing

verse: 2 Corinthians 6:8-10

#### Simple Explanation

Paul closes his description of his ministry with a series of paradoxes, pairs of statements that seem contradictory but are both true. They describe how the world saw him and how things really were in God's sight.

[[v8|as deceivers and yet true]]: his enemies called Paul a fraud, but he spoke the truth. [[v9|as unknown and yet well known]]: he was a nobody in the eyes of the powerful, yet known by God and by the churches. "As dying and behold—we live": he faced death again and again, yet God kept preserving him. "As punished and not killed": he suffered severe beatings but survived.

Verse 10 brings the list to a climax: [[v10|as sorrowful yet always rejoicing, as poor yet making many rich, as having nothing and yet possessing all things]]. Paul was genuinely sorrowful at times, yet underneath ran a steady joy. He was materially poor, yet through the gospel he brought spiritual riches to many. He owned little, yet in Christ he possessed everything that truly matters.

These paradoxes are not poetic exaggeration. They summarize Paul's actual experience, and they show how the gospel turns the world's values upside down.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These paradoxes mirror the life of Jesus Himself. He was regarded as a deceiver (Matthew 27:63), yet He was the truth. He died, yet He lives. He became poor so that others might become rich (2 Corinthians 8:9). Paul's ministry took the shape of the cross because he was following a crucified Lord.

The phrase "sorrowful yet always rejoicing" shows that Christian joy is not the absence of sorrow. Joy and grief can exist side by side. Paul wept over the churches (2 Corinthians 2:4), grieved over his people Israel (Romans 9:2), and yet rejoiced in the Lord always (Philippians 4:4). Joy rooted in God's faithfulness does not require denying pain.

"Having nothing and yet possessing all things" echoes 1 Corinthians 3:21–23, where Paul tells believers that all things are theirs because they belong to Christ. True wealth is found in belonging to God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many believers feel pressure to appear constantly happy, as if sadness were a failure of faith. These verses offer freedom. A Christian can grieve a loss, feel the weight of disappointment, or struggle through a hard season while still holding on to a deeper joy in God. Both can be honestly present at once.

These paradoxes also challenge the measures of success people often use. Being unknown, misunderstood, or financially modest does not mean a life is insignificant. A life given to God can make "many rich" in ways no bank account measures, through faithful love, prayer, encouragement, and quiet service.

### 2 Corinthians 6:14 — Do Not Be Unequally Yoked

verse: 2 Corinthians 6:14

#### Simple Explanation

A yoke was a wooden beam that joined two animals so they could pull a plow or cart together. If the animals were mismatched in size, strength, or temperament, they would pull against each other and the work would suffer. Deuteronomy 22:10 even forbade plowing with an ox and a donkey together. Paul uses this image to warn believers against being bound together with unbelievers in ways that pull them away from Christ.

Paul then asks two questions. What fellowship do righteousness and iniquity have? What fellowship does light have with darkness? The answer is that, at the deepest level, they move in opposite directions. A believer's life is oriented toward Christ; a partnership that requires going a different direction will create constant strain.

In context, Paul was most likely concerned about the Corinthians' continued involvement with idolatry and pagan temple life, as verse 16 suggests. Earlier he had warned them against eating in idol temples (1 Corinthians 10:14–22). He also may have had in mind their attraction to teachers whose values were worldly rather than Christ-centered.

Paul is not telling believers to avoid all contact with unbelievers. He made this clear in 1 Corinthians 5:9–10, where he said that would require leaving the world altogether. Christians are called to love, serve, work alongside, and befriend people who do not share their faith. The warning concerns binding commitments that would compromise faithfulness to Christ.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Christians have long applied this verse to marriage, since marriage is the closest of all human partnerships. Paul taught elsewhere that a widow is free to marry, but [[1 Corinthians 7:39|only in the Lord]]. Marrying someone who does not share faith in Christ often creates deep tension over the most important things in life: values, priorities, the raising of children, and the place of God in the home. For that reason, many churches counsel believers to marry fellow believers.

At the same time, this verse must be read alongside 1 Corinthians 7:12–16, where Paul tells believers already married to unbelievers not to leave their spouses if the spouses are willing to stay. This verse is not a command to end an existing marriage. A believer married to an unbeliever can trust that God is at work in that home, and the believing spouse's faithful love can be a powerful witness. Yet no passage of Scripture asks anyone to remain in danger; a spouse facing abuse should seek safety and help.

The principle also applies to business partnerships, binding agreements, and close alliances. The question is whether a commitment would require a believer to act against conscience or would steadily draw their heart away from God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

For those considering marriage, this verse invites honest reflection before commitment. Shared faith is not a guarantee of a happy marriage, but a deep difference in the most central loyalty of one's life is a serious matter. It is far wiser to weigh this before marriage than after.

For anyone entering a significant partnership, business venture, or close alliance, the same wisdom applies. Believers can work joyfully with people of all backgrounds, but they should be careful about commitments that would pressure them into dishonesty, compromise, or practices they know are wrong. Faithfulness to Christ needs to stay free.

### 2 Corinthians 6:16–18 — The Temple of the Living God

verse: 2 Corinthians 6:16-18

#### Simple Explanation

Paul gives the reason behind his warning: [[v16|For you are a temple of the living God]]. Corinth was full of temples to pagan gods. Paul reminds the believers that they themselves, together as the church, are God's temple. The living God dwells among them.

He then weaves together several Old Testament promises. The first comes from Leviticus 26:12 and Ezekiel 37:27, where God promises to live among His people and be their God. The second, in verse 17, echoes Isaiah 52:11, where God called His people to leave Babylon and touch nothing unclean. The third, in verse 18, recalls 2 Samuel 7:14, where God promised to be a Father to David's royal heir.

Notice how Paul adapts this last promise. God says, [[v18|You will be to me sons and daughters]]. The promise once spoken to the king is now extended to every believer, and Paul explicitly includes daughters as well as sons. Every member of God's people, male and female, is a beloved child of the Father.

The call to "come out" and "be separate" does not mean physically withdrawing from society. It means refusing to participate in what defiles: idolatry, immorality, and anything that competes with God for one's heart.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The idea of God dwelling with His people runs through the whole Bible. God walked with Adam and Eve in the garden, dwelt in the tabernacle and then the temple, and came to live among humanity in Jesus, who [[John 1:14|lived among us]]. Now, by the Spirit, God dwells in His people. The story will be completed in the new creation, when the dwelling of God will be with people forever (Revelation 21:3).

Paul's point is that holiness flows from this presence. Because God lives among His people, they are called to be distinct. But the motivation is not fear; it is relationship. God's call to separation ends with the most tender promise: "I will receive you. I will be to you a Father." Holiness is the way of life fitting for children who live in their Father's house.

These verses lead into 2 Corinthians 7:1, where Paul draws the conclusion: having these promises, believers should cleanse themselves from everything that defiles, [[2 Corinthians 7:1|perfecting holiness in the fear of God]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people think of holiness as a list of restrictions. These verses present it differently: holiness is the natural response to the privilege of God's presence. When believers remember that God dwells among them and calls them His sons and daughters, turning away from what defiles becomes an act of love rather than mere rule-keeping.

This passage also speaks a word of belonging. Those who have felt orphaned, rejected, or overlooked by earthly families can hear God say, "I will receive you. I will be to you a Father." In Christ, every believer has a home and a Father who welcomes them.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Second Corinthians 6 opens with urgency. God's grace is not meant to be received and then left idle. Now is the day of salvation. For those who have not yet responded to Christ, today is the day to come. For believers, today is the day to let grace keep reshaping their lives rather than putting off what God is asking.

Paul's description of his ministry offers a different picture of a faithful life than the one the world usually admires. He was not impressive by worldly measures. He was beaten, poor, misunderstood, and often exhausted. Yet his life was marked by purity, kindness, sincere love, and the power of God. His paradoxes remind believers that sorrow and joy can live together, that poverty can enrich others, and that a person who seems to have nothing can possess everything in Christ. This frees believers from measuring their worth by status, wealth, or reputation.

Paul's tender plea, "open your hearts," is a reminder that faith is lived in relationships. Misunderstandings, hurt feelings, and distance can creep in even among believers. Paul shows the way forward: speak honestly, love openly, and invite reconciliation.

Finally, the chapter calls believers to guard their loyalty to Christ. They are the temple of the living God. That identity should shape the commitments they make, especially marriage and other binding partnerships. This does not mean withdrawing from the world or treating unbelievers with suspicion; Christians are called to love their neighbors and live among them generously. It means refusing alliances that would pull them away from God, while resting in the beautiful promise that God receives them as His own sons and daughters.

## Chapter Prayer

Gracious Father, thank You that today is the day of Your favor and that Your grace has reached me. Do not let me receive it in vain; let it change my heart, my habits, and my choices. When I am misunderstood, overlooked, or weary, help me to trust that You see and that You are enough. Teach me to rejoice in You even when I am sorrowful, and to make others rich through love and faithfulness even when I have little. Open my heart toward the people around me, and heal the places where I have closed myself off. Give me wisdom in the commitments I make, so that my loyalty to You stays whole. Thank You that You dwell among Your people and that You call me Your child. Make my life a fitting home for Your presence. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
