# Acts 11
theme: Repentance unto Life for the Gentiles and a New Church in Antioch
main: When Peter returns to Jerusalem, some believers criticize him for eating with uncircumcised Gentiles. Peter tells them step by step how God led him to Cornelius and poured out the Holy Spirit on the Gentiles, and the church glorifies God, recognizing that He has granted the Gentiles repentance that leads to life. Meanwhile, scattered believers begin preaching to Greeks in Antioch, and a great number turn to the Lord. Barnabas encourages the new church, brings Saul to help teach, and the disciples are first called Christians. When a famine is foretold, the Antioch believers send relief to the believers in Judea.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 11 shows how the church responded to the conversion of Cornelius and how the gospel took root in a major Gentile city. The chapter has two parts. First, Peter explains to the Jerusalem church why he baptized Gentiles (11:1–18). Second, Luke describes the founding of the church in Antioch, which would become the base for Paul's missionary journeys (11:19–30).

### Peter Criticized (11:1–3)

News traveled quickly. [[v1|the apostles and the brothers who were in Judea heard that the Gentiles had also received the word of God]]. When Peter returned to Jerusalem, [[v2|those who were of the circumcision contended with him]]. This phrase refers to Jewish believers who believed that Gentiles needed to be circumcised and keep the Law of Moses to be part of God's people.

Notice what they criticized: [[v3|You went in to uncircumcised men and ate with them!]] Their concern was not primarily that Gentiles had believed, but that Peter had shared table fellowship with them. Eating together was a sign of acceptance and community. For many Jewish believers, this crossed a line they had been taught never to cross.

### Peter's Explanation (11:4–18)

Peter did not respond with anger or defensiveness. [[v4|But Peter began, and explained to them in order]]. He told them exactly what had happened: the vision of the sheet with unclean animals, the voice saying, "What God has cleansed, don't you call unclean," the arrival of the three men, the Spirit's instruction to go [[v12|without discriminating]], and the angel's message to Cornelius that Peter would bring [[v14|words by which you will be saved, you and all your house]]. Peter also mentioned that [[v12|These six brothers also accompanied me]]. He had witnesses.

Then Peter described the decisive moment: [[v15|As I began to speak, the Holy Spirit fell on them, even as on us at the beginning]]. He remembered Jesus' promise that His followers would be baptized with the Holy Spirit. Peter's conclusion and the church's response are studied below.

### The Church in Antioch (11:19–26)

Luke now picks up the story of those who had been scattered by the persecution after Stephen's death (8:1, 4). They traveled to Phoenicia (along the coast north of Israel), Cyprus (an island in the Mediterranean), and Antioch, [[v19|speaking the word to no one except to Jews only]].

Antioch was the capital of the Roman province of Syria and the third-largest city in the Roman Empire, after Rome and Alexandria, with a population of perhaps half a million. It was a cosmopolitan city of many cultures and religions, known for both its wealth and its moral corruption.

[[v20|But there were some of them, men of Cyprus and Cyrene, who, when they had come to Antioch, spoke to the Hellenists, preaching the Lord Jesus]]. Here "Hellenists" probably means Greek-speaking Gentiles. These unnamed believers took a bold step, sharing the gospel directly with non-Jews. [[v21|The hand of the Lord was with them, and a great number believed and turned to the Lord]].

The Jerusalem church sent Barnabas to Antioch. His ministry there is studied below. Barnabas then went to Tarsus to find Saul and brought him to Antioch. For a whole year, they taught many people. Then Luke adds a significant detail, studied below.

### Famine Relief (11:27–30)

Prophets came from Jerusalem to Antioch. One of them, Agabus, [[v28|indicated by the Spirit that there should be a great famine all over the world, which also happened in the days of Claudius]]. Claudius was the Roman emperor from A.D. 41 to 54. Ancient historians record several severe famines during his reign, including one in Judea around A.D. 46–48. The response of the Antioch church is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 11:17–18 — Who Was I to Withstand God?

verse: Acts 11:17-18

#### Simple Explanation

Peter had been criticized by Jewish believers in Jerusalem for entering the home of uncircumcised Gentiles and eating with them. He explained carefully everything that had happened: his vision, the Spirit's guidance, Cornelius's vision, and the Holy Spirit falling on the Gentiles as Peter spoke.

Then Peter drew his conclusion: [[v17|If then God gave to them the same gift as us when we believed in the Lord Jesus Christ, who was I, that I could withstand God?]] God had given the Gentiles "the same gift," the Holy Spirit, that He had given the Jewish believers at Pentecost. This showed that God had accepted them fully. If God had accepted them, how could Peter refuse to accept them? To oppose them would be to oppose God.

Notice that Peter emphasizes the gift was given "when we believed in the Lord Jesus Christ." The basis of acceptance for both Jews and Gentiles was faith in Jesus, not circumcision or keeping the Law.

The response of the critics was remarkable: [[v18|When they heard these things, they held their peace and glorified God]]. They stopped arguing and began praising God. Their minds were changed by the evidence of God's work.

They said, [[v18|Then God has also granted to the Gentiles repentance to life!]] "Repentance to life" means a turning to God that leads to eternal life. They recognized that repentance itself was God's gift, and that God had given it to the Gentiles just as He had given it to the Jews.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage shows that the church must follow where God leads. The Jerusalem believers had their own assumptions, but when they saw clear evidence of God's work, they submitted to it. The church does not decide whom God will accept; it recognizes and welcomes those whom God has already accepted.

It also reveals that salvation is a gift of God's grace from beginning to end. Even repentance, turning from sin to God, is something God "grants." Paul writes that God's kindness leads people to repentance (Romans 2:4). No one can boast in their own repentance; it is God's gift.

Finally, the passage shows a healthy way to handle disagreement in the church. Peter explained patiently, providing evidence and witnesses. The critics listened, changed their minds, and praised God. This pattern, honest discussion grounded in God's work and word, leads to unity.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to welcome those whom God has accepted. Sometimes churches struggle to accept people who are different, whether culturally, socially, or in their background. If God has given someone faith and His Spirit, the church is called to welcome them fully.

They also model how to handle conflict. When believers disagree, they can follow Peter's example: explaining carefully and humbly, and listening openly. They can also follow the Jerusalem believers' example: being willing to change their minds when they see God at work.

Finally, these verses encourage gratitude for the gift of repentance. Believers who have turned to God can thank Him for granting them repentance and life. They can also pray for others, asking God to grant them the same gift.

### Acts 11:23–24 — Barnabas Saw the Grace of God

verse: Acts 11:23-24

#### Simple Explanation

When the church in Jerusalem heard that many Gentiles in Antioch had believed, they sent Barnabas to investigate. This was a wise choice. Barnabas was from Cyprus, like some of those who had first preached in Antioch, and he was known for his generosity and encouragement (4:36–37; 9:27).

[[v23|who, when he had come, and had seen the grace of God, was glad]]. Barnabas could have come with suspicion, looking for problems. Instead, he saw evidence of God's grace in the lives of the new believers, and he rejoiced. He recognized that God was at work.

[[v23|He exhorted them all, that with purpose of heart they should remain near to the Lord]]. Barnabas encouraged the new believers to remain faithful to the Lord with determined hearts. New believers in a city like Antioch would face many temptations and pressures. Barnabas urged them to stay close to Jesus.

Luke then describes Barnabas's character: [[v24|For he was a good man, and full of the Holy Spirit and of faith]]. Barnabas was a person of genuine goodness, spiritual maturity, and trust in God. Few people in Scripture are simply described as "a good man."

[[v24|and many people were added to the Lord]]. Barnabas's ministry bore fruit. Through his encouragement, the church continued to grow.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the importance of recognizing God's grace in others. Barnabas did not focus on what was missing in the new believers or on their differences from Jewish believers. He saw what God was doing, and he was glad. Paul similarly gave thanks for the grace of God at work in the churches (1 Corinthians 1:4).

They also highlight the importance of perseverance. Barnabas urged the believers to remain true to the Lord. Faith is not only a one-time decision but a lifelong commitment. Jesus said, [[John 15:4|Remain in me, and I in you]].

Finally, Barnabas's character shows that effective ministry flows from who a person is. His goodness, faith, and fullness of the Spirit made him a channel of God's blessing. God uses people whose lives reflect His character.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to look for God's grace in others. It is easy to notice faults, especially in people who are different. Believers can train themselves to see and celebrate what God is doing in the lives of others.

They also call believers to encourage one another to remain faithful. New believers especially need support and encouragement. Believers can follow Barnabas's example, urging one another to stay close to the Lord through every season.

Finally, these verses invite believers to grow in character. Barnabas was a good man, full of the Spirit and faith. Believers can ask God to shape their character, knowing that genuine goodness and faith make a lasting impact.

### Acts 11:26 — First Called Christians

verse: Acts 11:26

#### Simple Explanation

The church in Antioch was growing rapidly. Barnabas realized he needed help, so he went to Tarsus, about a hundred miles away, to find Saul. Saul had been sent to Tarsus after his conversion when his life was threatened in Jerusalem (9:30). He had probably been ministering there for several years.

[[v26|When he had found him, he brought him to Antioch. For a whole year they were gathered together with the assembly, and taught many people]]. Barnabas and Saul worked together for a full year, teaching the new believers. Teaching was essential for a church made up of many new believers from pagan backgrounds who needed to understand the Scriptures and the way of Jesus.

[[v26|The disciples were first called Christians in Antioch]]. The name "Christian" means "follower of Christ" or "belonging to Christ." It may have been given by outsiders, possibly as a nickname. The church in Antioch included both Jews and Gentiles, so outsiders could not simply call them a Jewish sect. They needed a new name, and they chose one based on the person the believers constantly talked about: Christ.

The name appears only three times in the New Testament (here; Acts 26:28; 1 Peter 4:16). The believers usually called themselves "disciples," "brothers," "saints," or followers of "the Way." But the name "Christian" stuck and became the name used throughout history.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that the identity of believers is centered on Christ. They were known not by their ethnicity, culture, or social status, but by their connection to Christ. Paul writes, [[Colossians 3:11|where there can’t be Greek and Jew, circumcision and uncircumcision, barbarian, Scythian, bondservant, or free person; but Christ is all, and in all]].

It also shows the importance of teaching in the church. Barnabas and Saul spent a whole year teaching. Strong churches are built on solid teaching of God's word. Jesus commanded His followers to make disciples, [[Matthew 28:20|teaching them to observe all things that I commanded you]].

Finally, the verse shows that believers' lives can be visible to the world. Outsiders noticed that these people were devoted to Christ. Their lives revealed their allegiance.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to consider whether their lives reflect the name "Christian." The name means belonging to Christ. Believers can ask whether others would recognize them as followers of Jesus by their words, actions, and priorities.

It also encourages believers to value teaching. New believers need to be taught, and all believers continue to grow through learning God's word. Churches can prioritize solid biblical teaching, and believers can commit themselves to learning.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that their identity in Christ is greater than any other identity. Ethnicity, nationality, culture, and social status matter, but they are not the deepest truth about a believer. Belonging to Christ unites believers across all differences.

### Acts 11:29–30 — Sending Relief to the Brothers in Judea

verse: Acts 11:29-30

#### Simple Explanation

A prophet named Agabus came to Antioch from Jerusalem and predicted, by the Spirit, that a severe famine would spread over the Roman world. This famine came during the reign of Emperor Claudius. In Judea, where many believers were already poor, a famine would be devastating.

The church in Antioch responded immediately: [[v29|As any of the disciples had plenty, each determined to send relief to the brothers who lived in Judea]]. Each believer gave according to their ability. No one was forced; each person decided what they could give. This followed the principle Paul later taught: [[2 Corinthians 8:12|For if the readiness is there, it is acceptable according to what you have, not according to what you don’t have]].

Notice who they were helping: "the brothers who lived in Judea." The Antioch church was largely Gentile; the Judean church was Jewish. Yet they saw each other as family. The Gentile believers who had received the gospel from Jewish believers now helped them in their time of need.

[[v30|which they also did, sending it to the elders by the hands of Barnabas and Saul]]. They did not merely intend to help; they actually sent the gift. They entrusted it to Barnabas and Saul, trusted leaders, who delivered it to the elders of the Jerusalem church. This is the first mention of "elders" in the church, leaders who oversaw the congregation.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the unity of the church is expressed in practical love. Jews and Gentiles were one in Christ, and that unity was shown through generosity across ethnic and geographic lines. John writes, [[1 John 3:17|But whoever has the world’s goods and sees his brother in need, then closes his heart of compassion against him, how does God’s love remain in him?]]

They also reveal a pattern of mutual blessing. The Gentiles had received spiritual blessings from the Jewish believers; now they shared material blessings. Paul later wrote that if the Gentiles have shared in the Jews' spiritual blessings, [[Romans 15:27|they owe it to them also to serve them in material things]].

Finally, these verses show responsible handling of money. The gift was carried by trusted leaders and delivered to the elders. Paul later took great care to handle financial gifts with integrity (2 Corinthians 8:20–21).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to care for fellow believers in need, including those in other places and cultures. Many Christians around the world face poverty, famine, war, or persecution. Believers can give generously to support them.

They also show that giving should be according to ability. Not everyone can give the same amount. Each believer can decide what they are able to give, giving freely and cheerfully.

Finally, these verses highlight the importance of integrity in handling resources. Churches and ministries should be transparent and responsible with the gifts entrusted to them, ensuring that help reaches those who need it.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 11 shows a church willing to change its mind when God reveals His will. The Jerusalem believers were troubled when Peter ate with Gentiles, but when they heard how God had poured out His Spirit, they praised Him. Believers today can follow this example, remaining humble and open to God's leading, even when it challenges long-held assumptions.

The chapter also celebrates the spread of the gospel to new people and places. Unnamed believers from Cyprus and Cyrene shared Jesus with Gentiles in Antioch, and a great number believed. Every believer can share Christ with those around them, including people from different backgrounds, trusting that the Lord's hand is with them.

Acts 11 highlights the importance of encouragement and teaching. Barnabas saw the grace of God and encouraged the new believers to remain faithful. He and Saul taught the church for a year. Believers today need both encouragement to persevere and teaching to grow. Churches can provide both, and individual believers can be encouragers like Barnabas.

Finally, the chapter shows practical love across ethnic and geographic lines. The Antioch church, made up largely of Gentiles, sent relief to Jewish believers in Judea. Believers today can express their unity in Christ by giving generously to support fellow believers in need around the world.

Lord God, thank You for granting repentance that leads to life to people of every nation. Thank You for including me in Your family. Make me humble and willing to follow where You lead, even when it challenges my assumptions. Give me eyes like Barnabas to see Your grace at work in others, and a heart to encourage them to remain near to You. Let my life show that I belong to Christ, so that the name "Christian" fits me truly. Teach me through Your word and help me to grow. Make me generous, ready to share what I have with brothers and sisters in need, wherever they are. Unite Your church across every division by Your love. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
