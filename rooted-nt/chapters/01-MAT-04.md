# Matthew 4
theme: The Faithful Son in the Wilderness and the Call to Follow
main: Led by the Spirit into the wilderness, Jesus resists the devil's temptations by trusting and obeying God's Word, succeeding where Israel had failed. He then begins His public ministry in Galilee, announcing the kingdom of heaven, calling fishermen to follow Him, and showing the kingdom's power by teaching and healing.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 4 follows directly from Jesus' baptism. The Father had just declared from heaven, [[Matthew 3:17|This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased]], and the Spirit had come upon Jesus. Now that same Spirit leads Him into the wilderness, where His identity as God's Son will be tested. This order matters: Jesus faces temptation not to earn His Father's approval, but as the beloved Son who must show what kind of Son He is.

The chapter divides into two main parts. The first is the temptation in the wilderness (vv. 1–11). The second is the beginning of Jesus' ministry in Galilee (vv. 12–25), including His move to Capernaum, His first preaching, the calling of His first disciples, and a summary of His teaching and healing.

### The Temptation in the Wilderness (4:1–11)

Matthew says that Jesus [[v1|was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil]]. The Greek word for "tempted" can also mean "tested." From God's perspective this was a test that would reveal Jesus' faithfulness; from the devil's perspective it was an attempt to make Him fall. The wilderness was the barren region of Judea, a place associated in Israel's memory with the forty years their ancestors wandered after leaving Egypt.

The parallels with Israel's history are deliberate. Israel passed through the waters of the Red Sea and then spent forty years in the wilderness, where they were tested and repeatedly failed: they complained about food, tested God, and worshiped a golden calf. Jesus passes through the waters of baptism and then spends forty days in the wilderness, where He is tested and remains faithful. All three of His replies to the devil come from Deuteronomy 6–8, the chapters in which Moses reflected on Israel's wilderness years. Matthew is showing that Jesus is the true, faithful Son of God, succeeding where Israel had failed. There is also an echo of Moses himself, who fasted forty days and nights on Mount Sinai (Exodus 34:28).

After fasting forty days and nights, Jesus was hungry. The tempter came and said, [[v3|If you are the Son of God, command that these stones become bread]]. The word "if" does not express doubt so much as challenge: "Since you are God's Son, use your power for yourself." The temptation was to satisfy a real need by using His divine authority independently of the Father's will. Jesus answered from Deuteronomy 8:3, which says that God let Israel hunger in the wilderness and fed them with manna to teach them that people live not by bread alone but by every word from God's mouth.

Next the devil took Jesus to [[v5|the pinnacle of the temple]], probably a high corner of the temple complex overlooking a steep drop into the Kidron Valley. This time the devil quoted Scripture himself, Psalm 91:11–12, which promises that God's angels will guard His people. He urged Jesus to throw Himself down and let God rescue Him. Such a spectacular rescue might also have impressed crowds at the temple. Jesus replied with Deuteronomy 6:16: [[v7|You shall not test the Lord, your God]]. That verse refers to the time at Massah when the Israelites demanded that God prove His presence by giving them water (Exodus 17:1–7). Putting oneself in danger to force God to act is not faith but presumption.

Finally, the devil took Jesus to a very high mountain and showed Him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. He offered them all if Jesus would bow down and worship him. This was a temptation to gain the kingdom without the cross, to reach the goal of ruling the nations by taking a shortcut that bypassed obedience and suffering. Jesus responded firmly: [[v10|Get behind me, Satan!]] and quoted Deuteronomy 6:13, that God alone is to be worshiped and served. Then the devil left Him, and angels came and served Him.

### Jesus Begins His Ministry in Galilee (4:12–17)

Some time later, Jesus heard that John the Baptist had been arrested. (Matthew will explain the reason in chapter 14: John had condemned Herod Antipas for marrying his brother's wife.) Jesus withdrew to Galilee, the northern region where He had grown up. He left Nazareth and made His home in Capernaum, a fishing town on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee.

Matthew sees this move as fulfilling Isaiah 9:1–2. The tribal territories of Zebulun and Naphtali, in the north of Israel, had been the first to fall when Assyria invaded in the eighth century B.C., and the region had a mixed population, which is why Isaiah calls it [[v15|Galilee of the Gentiles]]. Isaiah promised that this region, which had first experienced darkness, would be the first to see a great light. That passage continues with the promise of a child born to reign on David's throne (Isaiah 9:6–7). Matthew says the light has now dawned in Jesus.

From that time, Jesus began to preach the same message John had preached: [[v17|Repent! For the Kingdom of Heaven is at hand]]. The phrase "from that time" marks a turning point in Matthew's Gospel, the beginning of Jesus' public ministry.

### The Calling of the First Disciples (4:18–22)

Walking along the shore of the Sea of Galilee, Jesus saw two brothers, Simon (later called Peter) and Andrew, casting a net into the water. He said to them, [[v19|Come after me, and I will make you fishers for men]]. They immediately left their nets and followed Him. A little farther on, He saw James and John, the sons of Zebedee, in a boat with their father, mending their nets. He called them, and they immediately left the boat and their father and followed Him.

John's Gospel tells us that at least some of these men had already met Jesus earlier, when they were associated with John the Baptist (John 1:35–42). Their response by the lake was therefore not entirely without background. Still, their decision was costly. Fishing on the Sea of Galilee was a significant business; Mark notes that Zebedee had hired servants (Mark 1:20). Leaving the boat and their father meant leaving livelihood, family business, and security to attach themselves to Jesus.

It was unusual for a rabbi to call his disciples. Normally students sought out a teacher. Jesus took the initiative and called these men to follow Him personally, not simply to learn a body of teaching.

### Teaching, Preaching, and Healing (4:23–25)

Matthew closes the chapter with a summary of Jesus' early ministry. He went throughout Galilee [[v23|teaching in their synagogues, preaching the Good News of the Kingdom, and healing every disease and every sickness among the people]]. Synagogues were local gathering places for prayer and the reading and teaching of Scripture, and visiting teachers were often invited to speak. Jesus' teaching explained the meaning of God's Word; His preaching announced the arrival of the kingdom; and His healings demonstrated what the kingdom looks like, with the effects of sin and evil being reversed.

News about Jesus spread throughout Syria, the larger Roman province that included Galilee. People brought those suffering from all kinds of conditions: diseases, pain, demon possession, seizures, and paralysis. He healed them. Great crowds followed Him from Galilee, from the Decapolis (a league of ten largely Gentile cities east of the Jordan), from Jerusalem and Judea, and from beyond the Jordan. This summary sets the stage for the Sermon on the Mount, which begins in the next chapter as Jesus sees the crowds and goes up the mountainside to teach.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 4:4 — Living by Every Word From God

verse: Matthew 4:4

#### Simple Explanation

This is Jesus' answer to the first temptation. He had fasted for forty days in the wilderness and was hungry. The devil came and suggested that, as the Son of God, Jesus should turn the stones around Him into bread. The wilderness of Judea was full of rounded limestone rocks that might even resemble small loaves. The suggestion seemed reasonable. Jesus had the power, and He had a genuine need.

But Jesus saw what the temptation really was. The Spirit had led Him into the wilderness, and the Father had not told Him to end His fast by making bread. To use His power to provide for Himself would be to act independently of His Father, putting His physical needs above His trust in God. It would also set a pattern of using His power for His own comfort rather than for His mission.

Jesus answered by quoting Deuteronomy 8:3. In that chapter Moses reminded Israel that God had led them through the wilderness for forty years to humble them and test them. God let them hunger and then fed them with manna, a food they had never known, to teach them that [[Deuteronomy 8:3|man does not live by bread only, but man lives by every word that proceeds out of the LORD’s mouth]]. Israel had often failed this test, complaining and doubting God's care. Jesus, facing the same kind of test, chose to trust His Father.

Jesus was not saying that bread is unimportant. He was saying that life depends ultimately on God and on obedience to His Word. He would rather remain hungry in obedience than be filled through disobedience.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows Jesus as the faithful Son who succeeds where Israel failed. Throughout the temptation narrative, Jesus relives Israel's wilderness experience and remains faithful at every point. This is not only an example for believers but part of His saving work: He is the obedient one whose faithfulness counts for those who belong to Him. Hebrews 4:15 says that He was [[Hebrews 4:15|in all points tempted like we are, yet without sin]].

The verse also shows the importance of Scripture in spiritual life. Jesus did not argue with the devil or rely on His own reasoning. He responded with God's Word, which He clearly knew deeply and understood in its context. Paul later calls the Word of God [[Ephesians 6:17|the sword of the Spirit]]. Knowing and trusting Scripture equips believers to recognize and resist temptation.

The temptation also teaches something about the nature of sin. Many temptations are not to do something obviously evil but to meet legitimate needs in illegitimate ways. Hunger was a real need. The question was whether Jesus would meet it by trusting His Father or by acting on His own.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to recognize that their deepest life comes from God, not only from physical provision. In a world that measures well-being mostly by material comfort, Jesus reminds His followers that relationship with God and obedience to His Word are essential for true life. Making time to read, understand, and live by Scripture is not an optional extra; it is nourishment.

It also helps believers recognize temptations to meet legitimate needs in wrong ways. The desire for security, affection, recognition, rest, or provision is not wrong. Temptation comes when we pursue these things through dishonesty, compromise, or disobedience. Jesus shows that it is better to wait on God, even through hardship, than to grab at what we need in a way that dishonors Him.

Finally, Jesus' example shows the value of knowing Scripture well. When temptation comes, it is too late to begin learning God's Word. Regular reading, reflection, and memorizing key passages prepare believers to recognize lies and respond with truth.

### Matthew 4:10 — Worship God Alone

verse: Matthew 4:10

#### Simple Explanation

This is Jesus' response to the third and final temptation. The devil had taken Him to a very high mountain and shown Him all the kingdoms of the world in their splendor. He promised to give them all to Jesus if Jesus would fall down and worship him. This temptation was the most direct of the three. It offered Jesus the authority over the nations that, according to Psalm 2:8, God had promised to give His Son, but it offered it on the devil's terms: without suffering, without the cross, and at the price of worshiping someone other than God.

Jesus replied, [[v10|Get behind me, Satan!]] This was a command to leave. "Satan" is a Hebrew word meaning "adversary" or "accuser." Then Jesus quoted Deuteronomy 6:13, where Moses commanded Israel to fear the LORD and serve Him alone, warning them not to follow other gods once they entered the land. Matthew's wording, "worship" and "serve him only," emphasizes that God alone is worthy of devotion.

After this the devil left Jesus, and angels came and served Him. The test was over, and Jesus had remained faithful at every point. Later in Matthew, Jesus will use similar words to Peter when Peter tries to talk Him out of going to the cross (Matthew 16:23). The temptation to avoid suffering on the way to glory would return, but Jesus would reject it again.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This temptation reveals what is at stake in worship. The devil's ultimate goal was to receive the worship that belongs to God alone. Idolatry, giving ultimate devotion to anything other than God, lies at the heart of all sin. Israel's great failure in the wilderness was making a golden calf and worshiping it (Exodus 32). Jesus, the faithful Son, refuses any rival to God.

The temptation also reveals the difference between the devil's way to power and God's way. The devil offered glory without suffering. God's way led through the cross. After His resurrection, Jesus would say to His disciples, [[Matthew 28:18|All authority has been given to me in heaven and on earth]]. He received the kingdom the devil had offered, but He received it from the Father, through obedience and sacrifice. The kingdoms of this world will become His, not because He compromised, but because He was faithful.

This verse shows that there are no shortcuts in God's kingdom that bypass obedience. The end does not justify the means. Even a good goal pursued by sinful methods is a form of serving another master.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to examine what they truly worship. Few people today are tempted to bow before the devil literally, but many are tempted to give ultimate devotion to success, money, power, approval, comfort, or relationships. Whatever we are willing to compromise our integrity for has become a rival to God. Recognizing these rivals honestly is the first step to turning away from them.

It also warns against shortcuts. Many temptations promise good results by questionable means: advancing a career through dishonesty, gaining influence through manipulation, or achieving a goal by cutting moral corners. Jesus' example teaches believers to pursue good ends only by good means, trusting God with the results.

Finally, Jesus' firm response, "Get behind me, Satan!", shows that some temptations should not be negotiated with. Believers can decide in advance that certain things are not up for discussion, and respond to temptation with clear refusal rather than prolonged consideration.

### Matthew 4:19 — "Come After Me"

verse: Matthew 4:19

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to Simon Peter and his brother Andrew as He walks along the shore of the Sea of Galilee. They are casting a net into the water, doing the ordinary work of their family trade. Fishing was hard, physical labor, often done at night, and it was an important industry around the lake. These men were not religious scholars or members of the elite. They were working men in a busy lakeside economy.

Jesus' call has two parts. The first is a command: "Come after me." This is the language of discipleship. In Jewish culture, a disciple literally followed his teacher, walking behind him, learning from his words and his way of life, and imitating him. But Jesus' call is unusual. Rabbis did not normally go looking for disciples; students chose their teachers. Jesus takes the initiative, and He calls them not to study the law under Him but to attach themselves to Him personally.

The second part is a promise: [[v19|I will make you fishers for men]]. Jesus uses the language of their own work to describe their new calling. Instead of gathering fish, they will gather people into God's kingdom. The phrase "I will make you" shows that Jesus Himself will train and shape them. They do not yet know how to do this work, but He will teach them.

Their response is immediate: [[v20|They immediately left their nets and followed him]]. The same happens with James and John, who leave the boat and their father. Matthew presents these responses as models of wholehearted discipleship.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that following Jesus begins with His call, not with our qualifications. He chose ordinary fishermen, not the most educated or influential people. Paul later reminds the Corinthians that God chose what the world considers foolish and weak (1 Corinthians 1:26–29). Jesus calls people as they are and promises to make them into what He intends.

The image of fishing for people may echo Jeremiah 16:16, where God says He will send fishermen to gather His people (though in that passage the context is judgment). In Jesus' ministry the image becomes one of gathering people into God's saving kingdom. Discipleship is not only about personal growth; it includes being sent to bring others to Jesus.

The call also shows the priority of Jesus over every other loyalty. Leaving nets, boats, and even a father represented a radical reordering of life. This does not mean that every disciple must abandon their job or family. Many followers of Jesus in the Gospels stayed in their homes and trades. But it does mean that Jesus' claim on a person's life comes first, and every other commitment must be held under His lordship.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Jesus' call still comes to people in the middle of ordinary life. He did not wait for these fishermen to become religious professionals. Believers can expect Him to meet them in their work, homes, and daily routines, and to call them to follow Him there.

Following Jesus means more than believing certain facts about Him; it means learning from Him, imitating Him, and obeying Him day by day. Practically, this involves spending time with His words in Scripture, praying, joining with other believers, and allowing Him to shape priorities, decisions, and relationships.

The promise "I will make you" is also an encouragement. Many believers feel unqualified to share their faith or to serve. Jesus did not call the fishermen because they were already skilled at fishing for people; He promised to make them so. Believers can step forward in faith, trusting that He will equip them as they follow. And when following Him requires leaving something behind, whether a habit, an ambition, or a source of security, the example of these disciples encourages a prompt and wholehearted response.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 4 shows how Jesus faced temptation and how He began calling others to follow Him. Both parts of the chapter speak to everyday life. In the wilderness, Jesus faced temptations that targeted His physical needs, His trust in God, and His mission. Believers face similar pressures: to meet needs in wrong ways, to test God rather than trust Him, and to pursue good goals through compromise. Jesus' consistent response was to rely on God's Word and to refuse anything that would draw Him away from His Father. Knowing Scripture, recognizing temptation for what it is, and holding firm convictions are practical ways believers can follow His example.

The chapter also shows that testing often comes after spiritual high points. Jesus went into the wilderness right after hearing His Father's voice at His baptism. Believers sometimes experience their hardest struggles after times of blessing or growth. Recognizing this pattern can help them stay alert and avoid becoming overconfident.

The calling of the fishermen reminds readers that Jesus' invitation is personal and life-changing. He did not merely invite people to agree with Him; He called them to follow Him. That call reshaped their priorities, their work, and their relationships. Believers today can ask what following Jesus requires of them in their current circumstances, and respond with the same readiness the first disciples showed.

Finally, the summary of Jesus' ministry, teaching, preaching, and healing, shows what kind of kingdom He brings. It is a kingdom that addresses both the soul and the body, bringing truth, forgiveness, and restoration. Those who follow Jesus are called to share in this kind of ministry: speaking truthfully about God, showing compassion to those who suffer, and bringing others to the One who can heal and save.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for remaining faithful in the wilderness when You were hungry, alone, and tempted. Thank You for trusting Your Father's Word, refusing to test Him, and worshiping Him alone. Teach me to live by every word that comes from God, and help me to know Scripture so well that I recognize temptation and answer it with truth.

Show me where I am tempted to meet good needs in wrong ways or to seek good goals through compromise, and give me strength to refuse the shortcuts that dishonor You. Thank You for calling ordinary people to follow You. Help me to hear Your call in the middle of my everyday life and to respond without delay. Make me into the person You want me to be, and use me to bring others to You. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
