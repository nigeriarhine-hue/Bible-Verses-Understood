# Matthew 26
theme: The Willing Sacrifice on the Night of Betrayal
main: As the Passover approaches, Jesus is anointed for burial, betrayed for thirty pieces of silver, and shares a final meal in which He explains His death as the blood of the new covenant poured out for the forgiveness of sins. In Gethsemane He submits His will to the Father; at His arrest He refuses violence and rescue; before the council He openly declares His identity; and in the courtyard Peter denies Him three times. Throughout, Matthew shows that Jesus goes to His death knowingly and willingly, in fulfillment of Scripture.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 26 begins the account of Jesus' passion, the events of His suffering and death. The first verse marks the transition: [[v1|When Jesus had finished all these words]]. This phrase closes each of Jesus' five major teaching sections in Matthew (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1), and here it says "all these words," signaling that His teaching ministry is now complete. What follows is the climax of the Gospel.

Matthew's account closely parallels Mark 14, but Matthew adds details that highlight two themes: Jesus' full awareness and control of what is happening, and the fulfillment of Scripture. Jesus is not a victim caught by surprise. He predicts each step, and He chooses to walk the path the Father has given Him.

### The Plot and the Anointing (26:1–16)

Jesus told His disciples, [[v2|You know that after two days the Passover is coming, and the Son of Man will be delivered up to be crucified]]. Passover remembered God's rescue of Israel from Egypt, when the blood of a lamb protected each household from judgment (Exodus 12). Jesus connected His death directly with this festival.

At the same time, the chief priests and elders met in the palace of the high priest, Caiaphas. Caiaphas served as high priest from about A.D. 18 to 36, an unusually long tenure that suggests he worked closely with the Roman authorities. In 1990, archaeologists discovered an ornate ossuary (bone box) inscribed "Joseph son of Caiaphas," which many believe belonged to him. The leaders plotted to arrest Jesus by deception and kill Him, but not during the festival, fearing a riot among the crowds.

In Bethany, at the home of Simon the leper, a woman poured a jar of very expensive perfume on Jesus' head. The disciples were indignant at the "waste," since it could have been sold and the money given to the poor. Jesus defended her, saying she had done a beautiful thing and had prepared His body for burial, and that her deed would be remembered wherever the gospel was preached. This scene is studied below.

Then Judas Iscariot, one of the Twelve, went to the chief priests and asked, [[v15|What are you willing to give me if I deliver him to you?]] They weighed out thirty pieces of silver. Matthew alone mentions the amount. In Exodus 21:32, thirty shekels was the compensation paid for a slave gored by an ox. In Zechariah 11:12–13, it was the insulting wage paid to God's rejected shepherd, which was thrown to the potter in the temple. Matthew will return to this prophecy in chapter 27. The price shows how little the religious leaders valued Jesus.

### The Last Supper (26:17–30)

On the first day of the Feast of Unleavened Bread, Jesus sent His disciples to prepare the Passover meal at a certain man's house in Jerusalem, with the message, [[v18|My time is at hand]]. Jesus knew His appointed hour had come.

That evening, as they reclined at the table (the customary posture for festive meals), Jesus announced that one of them would betray Him. The disciples were deeply grieved and asked one by one, [[v22|It isn’t me, is it, Lord?]] Jesus said that the one who dipped his hand into the dish with Him would betray Him, and He pronounced a solemn woe on the betrayer. Matthew alone records Judas asking, [[v25|It isn’t me, is it, Rabbi?]] Notice that the other disciples called Jesus "Lord," while Judas called Him "Rabbi," a respectful title but one that never appears on the lips of Jesus' faithful followers in Matthew. Jesus answered, [[v25|You said it]], a way of saying, "Yes, it is as you say."

During the meal, Jesus took bread, gave thanks, broke it, and gave it to the disciples, saying, [[v26|Take, eat; this is my body]]. Then He took a cup, gave thanks, and gave it to them, saying, [[v27|All of you drink it]], and explaining that it was His blood of the new covenant poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins. This is studied below. He added that He would not drink wine again until He drank it new with them in His Father's kingdom.

After singing a hymn, likely from the Hallel Psalms (Psalms 113–118) traditionally sung at Passover, they went out to the Mount of Olives.

### Peter's Denial Predicted (26:31–35)

On the way, Jesus told them that all of them would fall away that night, quoting Zechariah 13:7: [[v31|I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered]]. But He promised that after He was raised, He would go ahead of them to Galilee. Peter insisted that even if everyone else fell away, he never would. Jesus told him that before the rooster crowed that night, he would deny Him three times. Peter replied that even if he had to die with Jesus, he would never deny Him, and all the disciples said the same.

### Gethsemane (26:36–46)

They came to Gethsemane, an olive grove whose name means "oil press." Jesus took Peter, James, and John with Him and began to be sorrowful and deeply distressed. He told them, [[v38|My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death. Stay here and watch with me]]. Matthew alone includes the words "with me," emphasizing Jesus' desire for companionship in His agony.

He went a little farther, fell on His face, and prayed, [[v39|My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass away from me; nevertheless, not what I desire, but what you desire]]. Returning, He found the disciples sleeping and asked Peter whether they could not watch with Him for one hour. He urged them to watch and pray so they would not enter into temptation, because the spirit is willing but the flesh is weak. (Mark 14 treats this warning in depth; in Matthew the emphasis falls on Jesus' own progression in prayer, studied below.)

He went away a second time and prayed, [[v42|My Father, if this cup can’t pass away from me unless I drink it, your desire be done]]. Again He found them asleep. He prayed a third time, saying the same words. Then He returned and told them that the hour had come, the Son of Man was being betrayed, and the betrayer was near.

### The Arrest (26:47–56)

Judas arrived with a large crowd armed with swords and clubs, sent by the chief priests and elders. He had arranged a signal: the one he kissed was the man to arrest. He came to Jesus, said, [[v49|Greetings, Rabbi!]], and kissed Him. Matthew alone records Jesus' reply: [[v50|Friend, why are you here?]] Even in the moment of betrayal, Jesus addressed Judas as "friend," though the word could also carry a tone of reproach, as in Matthew 20:13 and 22:12.

One of Jesus' companions (John 18:10 identifies him as Peter) drew a sword and cut off the ear of the high priest's servant. Jesus told him to put the sword away, because those who take the sword will die by the sword. He added that He could ask His Father, who would immediately send more than twelve legions of angels. A Roman legion had about six thousand soldiers, so twelve legions would be over seventy thousand angels. But then, He asked, how would the Scriptures be fulfilled? This scene is studied below.

Jesus then asked the crowd why they came with weapons as though He were a robber, when He had taught in the temple every day. All this happened to fulfill the writings of the prophets. Then all the disciples deserted Him and fled.

### The Trial Before Caiaphas (26:57–68)

Jesus was taken to Caiaphas, where the scribes and elders had gathered. Peter followed at a distance into the courtyard to see what would happen. The council looked for false testimony to justify a death sentence but could not find agreeing witnesses, as the law required (Deuteronomy 19:15). Finally two came forward and claimed Jesus had said He could destroy the temple and rebuild it in three days, a distortion of something He had said about His body (John 2:19–21).

Jesus remained silent, fulfilling Isaiah 53:7. Then Caiaphas said, [[v63|I adjure you by the living God that you tell us whether you are the Christ, the Son of God]]. To "adjure" someone was to put them under a solemn oath. Under oath, Jesus answered, [[v64|You have said so]], meaning yes, and added that they would see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven, combining Psalm 110:1 and Daniel 7:13. Jesus was claiming to be the divine King who would judge them.

The high priest tore his clothes and declared that Jesus had spoken blasphemy. The council agreed that He deserved death. They spat in His face, struck Him, and mocked Him, demanding that He prophesy who hit Him.

### Peter's Denials (26:69–75)

Meanwhile, in the courtyard, a servant girl said that Peter had been with Jesus the Galilean. He denied it before everyone. At the gateway, another girl said the same, and Peter denied it with an oath: [[v72|I don’t know the man]]. A little later, bystanders said his Galilean accent gave him away. He began to curse and swear, "I don't know the man!" Immediately the rooster crowed. Peter remembered Jesus' words, went outside, and [[v75|wept bitterly]].

The contrast is deliberate. Inside, under oath, Jesus told the truth about Himself, knowing it would cost Him His life. Outside, Peter swore falsely to save himself. Yet Matthew's Gospel does not end with Peter's failure; the risen Jesus will gather His scattered disciples in Galilee (Matthew 28:16–20).

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 26:13 — A Gift Remembered Wherever the Gospel Goes

verse: Matthew 26:13

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words in Bethany, two days before Passover, at the home of Simon the leper. A woman (identified in John 12:3 as Mary, the sister of Martha and Lazarus) had come to Him with an alabaster jar of very expensive perfume and poured it on His head while He reclined at the table. Anointing a guest's head with a little oil was a gesture of hospitality, but this woman used a whole jar of costly perfume, an extravagant act of devotion.

The disciples were indignant. They asked, [[v8|Why this waste?]] and pointed out that the perfume could have been sold for a large sum and given to the poor. Their objection sounded reasonable and even righteous.

Jesus defended her. He said she had done [[v10|a good work for me]]. The poor would always be with them, giving them many opportunities to help, but He would not always be with them. Then He explained the deeper meaning of her act: [[v12|in pouring this ointment on my body, she did it to prepare me for burial]]. In Jewish custom, bodies were anointed with spices and perfumes before burial. Jesus had just told His disciples that He would be crucified in two days. Whether the woman fully understood or not, her gift became a preparation for His death.

Then Jesus made a remarkable promise in this verse: [[v13|wherever this Good News is preached in the whole world, what this woman has done will also be spoken of as a memorial of her]]. Jesus expected the gospel to be preached throughout the whole world, and He said her act would be part of that story. The promise has been fulfilled; her deed is recorded in three Gospels and remembered by Christians everywhere.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the value Jesus places on wholehearted devotion. The disciples measured the woman's act by its cost and practical usefulness. Jesus measured it by its love. Her gift was not wasteful, because nothing given to Jesus out of love is wasted.

The scene also shows that the gospel is centered on Jesus' death. Her anointing pointed to His burial, and Jesus connected her act with the preaching of the gospel. The good news that would go to the whole world is the news of His death and resurrection.

Matthew places this story between the leaders' plot and Judas's betrayal. The contrast is sharp: the leaders scheme, Judas sells Jesus for thirty pieces of silver, and this woman pours out something precious for Him. Every person must decide whether Jesus is worth everything or worth a small price.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to express their love for Jesus wholeheartedly. Worship, time, resources, and service given to Him are never wasted, even if others do not understand. Believers can ask what it would look like to give Christ their best rather than their leftovers.

It also warns against a critical spirit toward others' devotion. The disciples criticized the woman with arguments that sounded practical and spiritual. Believers can be slow to judge how others express their love for Christ, recognizing that God sees the heart.

Finally, this verse holds devotion to Christ and care for the poor together. Jesus did not dismiss care for the poor; He assumed His followers would continue to help them. Love for Jesus is the root, and generosity toward others is one of its fruits. Both belong in the life of a disciple.

### Matthew 26:28 — Blood Poured Out for the Forgiveness of Sins

verse: Matthew 26:28

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words during the Last Supper, the Passover meal He shared with His twelve disciples on the night before His death. The Passover meal included bread, wine, and the Passover lamb, and the leader explained the meaning of the elements in light of Israel's deliverance from Egypt. Jesus had just taken bread, given thanks, broken it, and said, "Take, eat; this is my body." Now He took a cup of wine, gave thanks, and told all of them to drink from it.

He explained: [[v28|for this is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the remission of sins]]. "Blood of the covenant" recalls Exodus 24:8, where Moses sprinkled sacrificial blood on the people to seal the covenant at Mount Sinai. "New covenant" recalls Jeremiah 31:31–34, where God promised a new covenant in which He would forgive His people's sins and write His law on their hearts. "Poured out for many" echoes Isaiah 53:12, where the Servant poured out His life to death and bore the sins of many.

Matthew alone includes the phrase [[v28|for the remission of sins]]. "Remission" means forgiveness, the cancellation of a debt. This phrase connects the cup directly with the angel's announcement at the beginning of Matthew's Gospel that Jesus would [[Matthew 1:21|save his people from their sins]]. The purpose of Jesus' death is forgiveness.

The disciples would have known that Jesus was speaking about His death, since He had predicted it repeatedly. But they could not yet understand how His death would accomplish forgiveness. That understanding came after the resurrection and the gift of the Spirit.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse explains the meaning of Jesus' death in His own words. His blood, His life given in death, establishes the new covenant and brings forgiveness of sins. In the Old Testament sacrificial system, blood represented life given in place of the sinner (Leviticus 17:11). Hebrews explains that [[Hebrews 9:22|Apart from shedding of blood there is no remission]], and that Christ's sacrifice accomplished what animal sacrifices could only picture.

The command, "All of you drink it," is significant. Every disciple, including those who would soon deny and desert Him, was included. Forgiveness is offered to all who come to Christ, regardless of their failures.

Christians have understood the Lord's Supper in different ways, from belief in Christ's real presence in the bread and wine, to a spiritual presence received by faith, to a memorial of His death. All Christian traditions agree that the meal proclaims Jesus' death for the forgiveness of sins and points forward to the day when He will drink it new in His Father's kingdom.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to trust that their sins are truly forgiven through Christ's death. Guilt and shame can linger even after confession, but Jesus said His blood was poured out for the forgiveness of sins. Believers can rest in what He has accomplished rather than trying to earn forgiveness through their own efforts.

It also calls believers to take the Lord's Supper seriously. Each time they share the bread and cup, they remember His sacrifice, receive His grace, examine their hearts, and look forward to His return. Coming to the table with gratitude and honest repentance deepens faith.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that they belong to a covenant community. The cup was shared among all the disciples. Those forgiven by Christ are joined to one another, and they are called to extend forgiveness to each other as they have been forgiven.

### Matthew 26:39 — "My Father, … Not What I Desire"

verse: Matthew 26:39

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records Jesus' first prayer in Gethsemane, an olive grove on the Mount of Olives where He went after the Last Supper. He had taken Peter, James, and John with Him and told them that His soul was overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death. He asked them to stay and keep watch with Him. Then He went a little farther and [[v39|fell on his face]], the posture of complete humility and desperate prayer.

He addressed God as [[v39|My Father]]. Matthew emphasizes the personal relationship: Jesus prays as the Son speaking to His Father. He asked, [[v39|if it is possible, let this cup pass away from me]]. The "cup" represents the suffering He was about to endure, including the cup of God's judgment against sin, an image used by the prophets (Isaiah 51:17). Jesus genuinely desired that, if there were any other way, He might be spared this suffering.

But He immediately added, [[v39|nevertheless, not what I desire, but what you desire]]. He placed His own desire under the Father's will.

Matthew's account highlights a progression in Jesus' prayers. In His second prayer, the wording changes: [[v42|My Father, if this cup can’t pass away from me unless I drink it, your desire be done]]. The first prayer asks whether the cup might pass; the second accepts that it will not, and embraces the Father's will. This echoes the words Jesus taught His disciples in the Lord's Prayer: [[Matthew 6:10|Let your will be done]]. He prayed a third time with the same words. When He rose from prayer, He went to meet His betrayer with calm resolve.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This prayer reveals the depth of Jesus' humanity and the strength of His obedience. He felt the full horror of what was coming. He did not face the cross with indifference or stoic detachment. Yet He chose obedience. Hebrews 5:8 says that He [[Hebrews 5:8|learned obedience by the things which he suffered]].

The progression in His prayers shows how honest prayer can lead to peace in God's will. Jesus did not pretend to want the cup. He expressed His desire honestly. But as He continued in prayer, His request moved toward acceptance. Prayer did not change the Father's plan, but it prepared Jesus to walk the path ahead.

It is important to note what Jesus' submission does and does not mean. He submitted to a specific purpose: giving His life for the salvation of the world. His submission does not teach that believers must passively accept every suffering or injustice as God's will. Those who arrested and condemned Jesus were acting wickedly, and Scripture consistently calls for resisting evil and protecting the vulnerable. Jesus' obedience was a willing choice to fulfill His saving mission.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse teaches believers to pray honestly. They can bring their fears, desires, and requests to God openly, even repeatedly, as Jesus did. God welcomes honest prayer from His children.

It also teaches believers to surrender their will to God's. After expressing their desires, they can say, "Not what I desire, but what You desire." This surrender is not resignation but trust that the Father's will is good, even when it is hard. In practice, it may mean accepting a closed door, persevering in a difficult calling, or choosing obedience when it is costly.

Finally, this verse shows that prayer can bring strength to face what lies ahead. Jesus emerged from Gethsemane ready to face His arrest. Believers facing difficult decisions or trials can spend time in prayer, asking not only for deliverance but for strength to do God's will, and wisdom to recognize when God's will includes seeking help, protection, or change.

### Matthew 26:52 — Put Away the Sword

verse: Matthew 26:52

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes at the moment of Jesus' arrest in Gethsemane. Judas had arrived with a large armed crowd sent by the chief priests and elders. He identified Jesus with a kiss, and the crowd seized Him. In that moment, one of Jesus' companions drew a sword and struck the servant of the high priest, cutting off his ear. John's Gospel tells us that the swordsman was Peter and the servant's name was Malchus (John 18:10). Luke adds that Jesus touched the man's ear and healed him (Luke 22:51).

Jesus immediately said, [[v52|Put your sword back into its place, for all those who take the sword will die by the sword]]. Jesus rejected violence as a way of defending Him or advancing His kingdom. Violence leads to more violence.

Then Jesus explained that He did not need human defense: [[v53|Or do you think that I couldn’t ask my Father, and he would even now send me more than twelve legions of angels?]] A Roman legion had about six thousand soldiers, so twelve legions would be a vast heavenly army. Jesus was not helpless. He could have been rescued in an instant. But He asked, [[v54|How then would the Scriptures be fulfilled that it must be so?]] He chose not to call for help because His death was the fulfillment of God's plan revealed in Scripture.

Peter's action showed sincere loyalty but misunderstood Jesus' mission. Peter was ready to fight for Jesus, but he was not ready to watch Jesus willingly go to the cross.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus' kingdom does not advance through violence. Jesus later told Pilate, [[John 18:36|My Kingdom is not of this world. If my Kingdom were of this world, then my servants would fight]]. His kingdom comes through truth, love, and self-sacrifice. This connects with His teaching in the Sermon on the Mount about not retaliating and loving enemies (Matthew 5:38–44).

It also shows that Jesus' death was voluntary. He was not overpowered. He had access to unlimited power and chose not to use it. He laid down His life willingly, as He said in John 10:18. This makes His sacrifice an act of love and obedience, not merely a tragedy.

Christians have applied this verse in different ways to questions of war, self-defense, and the role of government. Some believe it supports a commitment to nonviolence in all circumstances. Others believe it forbids using violence to advance Christ's kingdom or for personal revenge, while allowing governments to use force to restrain evil (Romans 13:4) and allowing the protection of the innocent. In its immediate context, Jesus was rejecting armed resistance to His own arrest, because He came to give His life.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to reject violence and force as ways of advancing their faith or defending Christ's honor. History contains many sad examples of people using violence in Jesus' name. His words, "Put your sword back," stand against all such efforts.

It also calls believers to break cycles of retaliation in their own lives. Whether in families, communities, or online conflict, responding to hostility with hostility usually escalates harm. Following Jesus means seeking peace, responding to wrongs with justice and restraint rather than revenge.

Finally, this verse invites believers to trust God's power and plan. Peter thought he needed to defend Jesus with a sword. Jesus knew that His Father's power was available and that His Father's plan was good. Believers can trust God in situations that seem out of control, remembering that God's purposes are often accomplished through ways that look like weakness.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 26 shows a range of responses to Jesus as He approaches the cross. A woman pours out a costly gift in love. Judas sells Him for thirty pieces of silver. The disciples promise loyalty and then sleep, fight, flee, and deny. The religious leaders plot, bribe, and condemn. In the middle of all this, Jesus moves forward with clear purpose and willing obedience. Readers are invited to see themselves in these responses and to ask how they are responding to Jesus.

The chapter calls believers to wholehearted devotion. The woman at Bethany gave something precious without calculating its cost. Believers can ask whether they give Jesus their best, whether in worship, time, resources, or service, and whether they are quick to criticize others' devotion.

The Last Supper reminds believers that Jesus' death was for the forgiveness of sins. The new covenant rests on His blood, not on their faithfulness. This gives assurance and humility. Believers who have failed can return to Christ, confident that His blood was poured out for people like them, including disciples who would deny Him that very night.

Gethsemane teaches believers how to face hard things: with honest prayer, persistent seeking of God's will, and surrender to the Father. It also teaches watchfulness, since the disciples' failure to pray left them unprepared for the test. Jesus' response at His arrest teaches the rejection of violence and retaliation, and trust in God's power and plan.

Finally, Peter's denial and bitter weeping show both the danger of self-confidence and the hope of restoration. Believers can recognize their weakness, depend on God's strength, and, when they fail, turn back to Jesus with genuine repentance. The One who went willingly to the cross did so for disciples who had failed Him, and His grace is greater than their failures.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for walking willingly toward the cross, knowing everything that would happen to You. Give me a heart like the woman at Bethany, who poured out her best for You without counting the cost. Keep me from valuing You cheaply, as Judas did, and from criticizing the devotion of others.

Thank You for the cup of the new covenant, Your blood poured out for the forgiveness of my sins. Help me to rest in Your forgiveness and to share it with others. When I face my own Gethsemane, teach me to pray honestly and to say, "Your will be done." Keep me watchful and prayerful, aware of my weakness. Help me to put away every sword of retaliation and to trust Your power and plan. When I fail You, as Peter did, let my sorrow lead me back to You, the Savior who gave Himself for me. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
