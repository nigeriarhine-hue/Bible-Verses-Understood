# Mark 11
theme: The King Enters, the Temple Is Judged, and Faith Is Called For
main: Jesus enters Jerusalem on a colt to shouts of "Hosanna," then looks over the temple and returns the next day to drive out the merchants, declaring that God's house should be a house of prayer for all nations. Mark wraps this action inside the story of a leafy but fruitless fig tree that Jesus curses and that withers, showing that God's judgment rests on empty religion. Jesus calls His disciples to faith in God and to forgiveness in prayer, and He refuses to answer leaders who will not admit the truth about John the Baptist.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 11 begins the final week of Jesus' earthly life. Mark 11–16 covers only about eight days, yet it fills more than a third of Mark's Gospel. Jesus has traveled from Galilee to Jericho and now climbs the steep road to Jerusalem for Passover. The city was filled with pilgrims, and Messianic hopes were high. Jesus had predicted three times that He would be rejected, killed, and raised in Jerusalem (Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34). Now He arrives.

Mark arranges this chapter carefully. He tells about Jesus' entry, then His first look at the temple, then the cursing of the fig tree, then the cleansing of the temple, then the withered fig tree. This "sandwich" structure, in which the fig tree story surrounds the temple scene, shows that the two events interpret each other. (Matthew's account of these events is studied at Matthew 21; this chapter focuses on details distinctive to Mark.)

### The Entry Into Jerusalem (11:1–11)

As they approached Jerusalem, near the villages of Bethphage and Bethany on the Mount of Olives, Jesus sent two disciples ahead to a village to find [[v2|a young donkey tied, on which no one has sat]]. Animals that had never been used were considered suitable for sacred purposes (Numbers 19:2; 1 Samuel 6:7). If anyone asked, they were to say, [[v3|The Lord needs him]], and that He would send it back. Everything happened exactly as Jesus said, showing that He was in control of the events of His final week.

They brought the colt, threw their cloaks on it, and Jesus sat on it. This act fulfilled Zechariah 9:9, which describes Israel's king coming humbly, riding on a donkey's colt. Many people spread their cloaks on the road, and others spread leafy branches cut from the fields. Those in front and behind shouted, [[v9|Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!]], words from Psalm 118:25–26, a psalm sung by pilgrims at Passover. Mark adds a shout found only in his Gospel: [[v10|Blessed is the kingdom of our father David that is coming in the name of the Lord!]] The crowd was hoping for the restoration of David's kingdom, probably imagining political liberation from Rome.

Mark's account of the entry ends quietly. Jesus entered the temple, [[v11|looked around at everything]], and since it was already late, went back to Bethany with the Twelve. Mark alone records this inspection. Jesus was like a king surveying His house before acting. What He saw would lead to His action the next day.

### The Fig Tree Cursed (11:12–14)

The next morning, as they were leaving Bethany, Jesus was hungry. Seeing a fig tree in leaf at a distance, He went to see if it had any fruit. He found nothing but leaves, [[v13|for it was not the season for figs]]. Jesus said to it, [[v14|May no one ever eat fruit from you again!]], and His disciples heard it. This puzzling episode is studied below.

### Cleansing the Temple (11:15–19)

In Jerusalem, Jesus entered the temple courts and began driving out those who were buying and selling. He overturned the tables of the money changers and the seats of those selling doves. The money changers exchanged foreign coins for the Tyrian silver coins required for the temple tax, and doves were the sacrifice of the poor (Leviticus 12:8). Mark adds a detail found only in his account: Jesus [[v16|would not allow anyone to carry a container through the temple]]. People apparently used the outer court as a shortcut, carrying goods through the sacred space. Jesus stopped it all.

He then taught, quoting Isaiah 56:7: [[v17|My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations]]. Mark alone includes "for all the nations." The commercial activity was taking place in the Court of the Gentiles, the only area where non-Jews could come to worship. By turning it into a marketplace, the temple authorities were blocking the very people God wanted to welcome. Then Jesus quoted Jeremiah 7:11: [[v17|But you have made it a den of robbers!]] In Jeremiah's day, God had warned that the temple would be destroyed because people practiced injustice and then used the temple as a hiding place, the way robbers retreat to a den. (This action is studied in depth at Matthew 21:13.)

The chief priests and scribes heard this and looked for a way to kill Jesus. But they feared Him, because the whole crowd was amazed at His teaching. When evening came, Jesus and His disciples left the city.

### The Withered Fig Tree and the Lesson on Faith (11:20–26)

The next morning, as they passed by, they saw the fig tree withered from the roots. Peter remembered and said, [[v21|Rabbi, look! The fig tree which you cursed has withered away]]. Jesus responded with teaching about faith and prayer, studied below. He then added an important condition for prayer: forgiveness, also studied below. (Verse 26 is not found in many early manuscripts.)

### The Question of Authority (11:27–33)

Back in the temple courts, the chief priests, scribes, and elders, representing the Sanhedrin, the ruling council, came to Jesus and demanded, [[v28|By what authority do you do these things? Or who gave you this authority to do these things?]] They were the official authorities over the temple. Jesus had acted without their permission.

Jesus said He would answer if they answered one question: was John's baptism from heaven or from men? They discussed it among themselves. If they said "from heaven," Jesus would ask why they had not believed John. If they said "from men," they feared the people, who all considered John a prophet. So they answered, [[v33|We don’t know]]. Jesus replied that He would not tell them by what authority He acted.

Jesus' question was not an evasion. John had testified that Jesus was the Coming One. If the leaders had acknowledged John's authority, they would have known the source of Jesus' authority too. Their refusal to answer honestly exposed their unwillingness to accept the truth. They were more concerned with protecting their position and avoiding public embarrassment than with discovering what God was doing.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 11:13–14 — The Fruitless Fig Tree

verse: Mark 11:13-14

#### Simple Explanation

These verses describe one of the most puzzling events in Jesus' ministry. On the morning after His entry into Jerusalem, as Jesus left Bethany, He was hungry. He saw a fig tree in the distance that was in leaf, and He went to see whether it had any fruit. When He reached it, He found nothing but leaves. Mark adds, [[v13|for it was not the season for figs]]. Then Jesus said to the tree, [[v14|May no one ever eat fruit from you again!]] The next morning, the disciples saw the tree withered from the roots (v. 20).

Many readers wonder why Jesus would curse a tree for not having fruit when it was not the season. The answer lies in understanding both fig trees and Mark's purpose.

Fig trees in that region usually produced their leaves in spring, around Passover. Along with the leaves, many fig trees produced small early knobs of fruit, called "paggim," which were edible, though not as good as the main crop that came later in summer. A tree full of leaves but without any of these early knobs was a sign that it would not produce fruit that year. The tree's leaves advertised fruit, but it had none. Its appearance was misleading.

More importantly, Mark presents this event as an acted parable, a symbolic action like those of the Old Testament prophets, who sometimes performed actions to illustrate God's message. Mark places the cursing of the fig tree right before the cleansing of the temple and the discovery of the withered tree right after it. The fig tree represents the temple and the religious system centered on it. Like the tree, the temple was impressive and busy, full of activity and appearance, but it was not producing the fruit God desired: prayer, justice, and welcome for the nations. Jesus' curse on the tree pictured God's judgment on fruitless religion. The temple was destroyed by the Romans about forty years later, in A.D. 70.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage connects with the Old Testament, where the fig tree and vine often represent Israel. The prophets used fig trees to describe God's disappointment when He looked for fruit among His people and found none. Jeremiah wrote, [[Jeremiah 8:13|No grapes will be on the vine, no figs on the fig tree, and the leaf will fade]], describing God's judgment. Micah lamented that there was no early fig to eat (Micah 7:1). Jesus' action stands in this prophetic tradition.

The passage warns that outward religious activity without genuine fruit is dangerous. The temple had magnificent buildings, constant sacrifices, and crowds of worshipers, yet it had become a place of commerce and exploitation rather than prayer. God looks for the fruit of faithfulness, justice, mercy, and true worship, not merely leaves of religious appearance.

The fig tree also shows that Jesus has authority to pronounce judgment. The same Jesus who healed and welcomed sinners also confronted religious hypocrisy with God's judgment. His love and His holiness belong together.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage invites believers to examine whether their lives bear real fruit or only leaves. It is possible to be active in church, use religious language, and appear devout while lacking genuine love for God and others. Believers can ask whether their faith produces fruit such as prayer, love, honesty, generosity, and humility, or whether it is mostly outward appearance.

It also challenges churches to evaluate their life together. Are they houses of prayer, places where all people, including outsiders, are welcome to meet God? Or have they become focused on activity, buildings, or programs while neglecting what matters most to God?

Finally, this passage encourages believers to take God's judgment seriously while trusting His grace. Jesus' warning is not meant to drive people to despair but to call them to repentance and genuine faith. Those who abide in Christ will bear fruit (John 15:5), not by their own effort, but by His life in them.

### Mark 11:22–24 — Have Faith in God

verse: Mark 11:22-24

#### Simple Explanation

These verses contain Jesus' response when Peter pointed out that the fig tree Jesus had cursed had withered from the roots. The disciples were amazed that the tree had died so quickly. Jesus turned their attention from the miracle to its source: [[v22|Have faith in God]].

The phrase is simple but central. Jesus did not say "have faith in faith" or "have faith in your words." He said "have faith in God." The power that withered the fig tree came from God, and faith is trust directed toward Him.

Then Jesus gave a vivid illustration: whoever says to [[v23|this mountain]], "Be taken up and thrown into the sea," and does not doubt but believes, will have it done. Jesus and His disciples were near Jerusalem, and "this mountain" may refer to the Mount of Olives or the temple mount itself. The sea might be the Dead Sea, visible from the Mount of Olives. "Moving mountains" was a common Jewish expression for doing something seemingly impossible. If Jesus was pointing to the temple mount, His words may have connected to the judgment He had just pronounced on the temple.

Jesus continued: [[v24|all things whatever you pray and ask for, believe that you have received them, and you shall have them]]. This is a bold promise about prayer offered in faith.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses teach that prayer rooted in faith in God is powerful. Believers are invited to pray boldly, trusting that God can do what seems impossible. Faith is not wishful thinking but confident trust in God's power and character.

However, these verses must be understood in the context of the whole Bible's teaching on prayer. They do not mean that believers can obtain anything they want simply by believing hard enough. Jesus Himself prayed in Gethsemane, "Not what I desire, but what you desire" (Mark 14:36), and the Father did not remove the cup. Paul prayed three times for his thorn to be removed, and God said no (2 Corinthians 12:7–9). John teaches that God hears prayers that are [[1 John 5:14|according to his will]]. Faith in God includes trusting His wisdom when His answer differs from our request.

These verses also must not be used to blame people for unanswered prayers by suggesting that they did not have enough faith. Many faithful believers have prayed for healing or deliverance and not received it in this life. Scripture honors such people (Hebrews 11:35–39). The point of Jesus' teaching is to encourage bold, trusting prayer, not to create a formula that guarantees results.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to pray boldly. They can bring to God situations that seem impossible: broken relationships, deep sin, serious illness, injustice, or spiritual darkness. God is able to do far more than they imagine. Faith means trusting His power rather than being limited by what seems possible.

They also call believers to direct their faith toward God, not toward their own feelings or words. The focus is not on how strongly one believes, but on whom one believes in. Believers can pray with confidence because God is good, powerful, and faithful.

Finally, these verses invite believers to trust God's wisdom alongside His power. When answers to prayer differ from what they hoped, they can continue to have faith in God, knowing that His purposes are good even when they do not understand them. They can also comfort others who are suffering without suggesting that their faith was lacking.

### Mark 11:25 — Forgive When You Pray

verse: Mark 11:25

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words right after encouraging His disciples to have faith in God and to pray boldly. He has promised that whatever they ask in prayer, believing, they will receive. Now He adds an important condition that shapes how they pray: [[v25|Whenever you stand praying, forgive, if you have anything against anyone]].

"Stand praying" reflects the common Jewish posture for prayer, standing with hands raised. Jesus says that whenever His followers pray, they must deal with any resentment they hold against others. "Anything against anyone" covers every grievance and every person.

The purpose is given: [[v25|so that your Father, who is in heaven, may also forgive you your transgressions]]. "Transgressions" are wrongdoings, stepping over the line God has set. Those who pray to God for forgiveness and help must themselves be willing to forgive.

This teaching connects with the cleansing of the temple and the fig tree. The temple was meant to be a house of prayer, but it had become corrupt. Now Jesus teaches His disciples what true prayer involves: faith in God and a forgiving heart. Prayer is not merely about getting what we ask for; it is about being in right relationship with God and others.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that forgiveness is essential to a healthy relationship with God. Jesus consistently connects receiving God's forgiveness with forgiving others. In the Lord's Prayer, He taught His followers to pray, "Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors" (Matthew 6:12). In the parable of the unforgiving servant, He showed that those who have been forgiven much must forgive others (Matthew 18:21–35).

This does not mean that people earn God's forgiveness by forgiving others. God's forgiveness is a gift of grace. But a heart that has truly received God's forgiveness will be changed by it. Refusing to forgive others while asking God for forgiveness reveals a heart that has not fully understood or received God's mercy.

The placement of this verse right after the promise about powerful prayer is significant. Bold faith and bitter resentment cannot coexist comfortably. Unforgiveness hinders prayer because it damages our relationship with God. Peter later writes that husbands who mistreat their wives may find their prayers hindered (1 Peter 3:7), showing that relationships with others affect relationships with God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine their hearts whenever they pray. Before bringing requests to God, they can ask whether they are holding resentment against anyone. If so, they can bring that resentment to God and choose to forgive, releasing the debt and the desire for revenge.

It also helps believers understand forgiveness correctly. Forgiving someone does not mean pretending the wrong did not happen, excusing it, or necessarily restoring trust immediately. In cases of abuse or ongoing harm, forgiveness can coexist with protective boundaries and with seeking justice through appropriate channels. Forgiveness means giving up the right to personal revenge and leaving judgment to God.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to make forgiveness a regular part of their spiritual lives. Forgiveness is often not a one-time act but a process, especially for deep wounds. Each time believers pray, they can renew their decision to forgive, asking God for the strength to let go and for healing in their hearts.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 11 shows Jesus arriving in Jerusalem as King and Judge. The crowds welcomed Him with hopes of a political kingdom, but Jesus came humbly on a colt and then turned His attention to the temple. Believers can welcome Jesus as King while allowing Him to define what His kingdom means. He is not a king who exists to fulfill their agendas but one who calls them to genuine faith and fruitful lives.

The fig tree and the temple warn against religion that is all leaves and no fruit. Believers can examine whether their faith produces genuine fruit: prayer, love, justice, and welcome for others. Churches can ask whether they are truly houses of prayer for all nations, places where outsiders are welcomed to meet God, or whether other activities have crowded out what matters most.

Jesus' teaching on faith calls believers to trust God boldly in prayer. Nothing is too difficult for Him. Yet faith also trusts His wisdom when His answers differ from their requests. Believers can pray with confidence, avoid turning faith into a formula, and comfort others without blaming them for unanswered prayers.

The call to forgive when praying reminds believers that their relationship with God cannot be separated from their relationships with others. Resentment hinders prayer. Believers can regularly examine their hearts and release grudges to God, while using wisdom about boundaries and justice. Finally, the leaders' refusal to answer honestly about John shows the danger of protecting one's position instead of admitting truth. Believers can choose honesty and humility, even when the truth is costly.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You came to Jerusalem as the humble King, riding on a colt. I welcome You as my King, and I ask You to reign in my life on Your terms, not mine. Search my life as You searched the temple. Where I have leaves without fruit, appearance without reality, forgive me and make me fruitful.

Make my life and Your church a house of prayer for all nations, open to everyone who seeks You. Teach me to have faith in God, to pray boldly, and to trust Your wisdom when Your answers are different from my requests. Show me anyone I am holding resentment against, and give me grace to forgive as You have forgiven me. Keep me honest and humble, willing to accept the truth even when it costs me. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
