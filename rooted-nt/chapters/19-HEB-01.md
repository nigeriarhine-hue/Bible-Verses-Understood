# Hebrews 1
theme: God Has Spoken by His Son
main: In the past God spoke to His people through the prophets in many ways, but in these last days He has spoken by His Son, whom He appointed heir of all things and through whom He made the universe. The Son is the radiance of God's glory and the exact imprint of His nature, upholding all things by His powerful word. After making purification for sins, He sat down at the right hand of the Majesty on high, having become far superior to the angels. Using a chain of Old Testament quotations, the author shows that the Son holds a name and position no angel has ever had: He is called God's Son, worshiped by angels, addressed as God whose throne lasts forever, the eternal Creator who never changes, and the one invited to sit at God's right hand. Angels, by contrast, are ministering spirits sent to serve those who will inherit salvation.

## Chapter Background and Story

Hebrews opens without any greeting, name, or thanksgiving. It plunges straight into its great theme: Jesus Christ is God's final and supreme word to humanity. The first four verses, a single long sentence in Greek, form one of the most majestic descriptions of Christ in the Bible.

The first readers were believers, probably Jewish Christians, who were tempted to drift away from Christ, perhaps back to the familiar practices of the temple and the Law. The author wants them to see that turning away from Jesus would mean turning away from the very fullness of what God had been promising all along. Everything God had said through the prophets, the Law, the priesthood, and the sacrifices pointed forward to Christ.

Why does the author spend so much time comparing Jesus with angels? In Jewish tradition, angels were believed to have played a role in giving the Law at Mount Sinai (Acts 7:53; Galatians 3:19; Hebrews 2:2). Angels were highly honored, and some may have been tempted to see Jesus as a kind of angel or to give angels too much importance. The author shows from Scripture that the Son is far above all angels. If the message delivered through angels was serious, how much more the message delivered by the Son.

### God's Final Word (1:1–4)

The opening verses about God speaking through His Son, and the Son's glory, are studied below. [[v4|having become as much better than the angels as the more excellent name he has inherited is better than theirs]]. The word "better" appears here for the first time; it will become a key word throughout Hebrews. The "name" the Son has inherited is the title "Son" itself, as the following verses show, a title and a position no angel holds.

### Greater Than the Angels (1:5–14)

The author now quotes seven Old Testament passages, mostly from the Psalms, to show the Son's superiority.

[[v5|For to which of the angels did he say at any time, “You are my Son. Today I have become your father?” and again, “I will be to him a Father, and he will be to me a Son?”]] The first quotation is from Psalm 2:7, a royal psalm about God's anointed King. The second is from 2 Samuel 7:14, God's promise to David about his royal descendant. Both point ultimately to the Messiah. God never spoke to any angel this way. The phrase "Today I have become your father" is applied in the New Testament to Jesus' resurrection and enthronement (Acts 13:33), when He was publicly declared to be God's Son with power (Romans 1:4). It does not mean that the Son began to exist at that moment; verse 2 has already said He was active in creation.

[[v6|When he again brings in the firstborn into the world he says, “Let all the angels of God worship him.”]] This quotation comes from the Greek version of Deuteronomy 32:43 (see also Psalm 97:7). "Firstborn" refers to the Son's rank and inheritance, as in Colossians 1:15. Angels do not receive worship; they give it to the Son. Since Scripture teaches that only God is to be worshiped (Exodus 20:3–5; Revelation 22:8–9), this verse points to the Son's divinity.

[[v7|Of the angels he says, “He makes his angels winds, and his servants a flame of fire.”]] Quoting Psalm 104:4, the author shows that angels are servants, powerful and glorious like wind and fire, but created and changeable. The contrast with the Son is studied below.

[[v10|And, “You, Lord, in the beginning, laid the foundation of the earth. The heavens are the works of your hands.]] [[v11|They will perish, but you continue. They all will grow old like a garment does.]] [[v12|You will roll them up like a mantle, and they will be changed; but you are the same. Your years won’t fail.”]] Psalm 102:25–27 originally addresses the LORD as Creator. The author applies it to the Son. He is the eternal Creator. The universe will wear out like old clothing and be replaced, but the Son will remain the same forever. Hebrews 13:8 says, [[Hebrews 13:8|Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever]].

[[v13|But which of the angels has he told at any time, “Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies the footstool of your feet?”]] The final quotation is from Psalm 110:1, the Old Testament verse most often quoted in the New Testament. God invites the Messiah to sit at His right hand, the place of supreme honor and authority, until every enemy is defeated. No angel has ever been given that invitation. The role of angels is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Hebrews 1:1–2 — God Has Spoken to Us by His Son

verse: Hebrews 1:1-2

#### Simple Explanation

The letter begins with God speaking. "God, having in the past spoken to the fathers through the prophets at many times and in various ways." Throughout the Old Testament, God revealed Himself to Israel's ancestors. He spoke "at many times," over many centuries, and "in various ways": through dreams, visions, laws, history, poetry, prophecy, and direct encounters. Each revelation was true, but each was partial.

"Has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son." Now God has spoken in a new and final way. "The end of these days" refers to the last era of history, which began with Christ's coming. God's final word is not a message delivered by a messenger but a person: His Son.

The author then describes the Son. "Whom he appointed heir of all things." Everything belongs to the Son. He is the rightful owner and ruler of all creation.

"Through whom also he made the worlds." The Son was the agent of creation. The word for "worlds" can also mean "ages," including all of time and history. The Son is not part of creation but its Maker (John 1:3; Colossians 1:16).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses establish the central claim of Hebrews: Jesus is God's supreme and final revelation. The prophets spoke truly, but they spoke in fragments. In Jesus, God has spoken fully. John's Gospel expresses the same truth: [[John 1:18|No one has seen God at any time. The only born Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, has declared him]]. Jesus Himself said, [[John 14:9|He who has seen me has seen the Father]].

This does not mean the Old Testament is unimportant. Hebrews quotes it constantly and treats it as God's own word. But the Old Testament finds its fulfillment and its fullest meaning in Christ. Every promise, picture, and prophecy points to Him.

The description of the Son as both "heir of all things" and the one [[v2|through whom also he made the worlds]] shows that Christ stands at both the beginning and the end of history. All things came through Him and all things are headed toward Him.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

If God has spoken by His Son, then the most important thing anyone can do is listen to Him. Believers can make knowing Jesus, through the Gospels and the whole of Scripture, the center of their spiritual lives.

This truth also brings confidence. People often wonder what God is like and whether He has spoken. Hebrews answers clearly: God has spoken, and His clearest word is Jesus. Those who want to know God can look to Him.

Finally, believers can read the Old Testament with new eyes, seeing how the many partial revelations of the past prepare for and point to Christ.

### Hebrews 1:3 — The Radiance of God's Glory

verse: Hebrews 1:3

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes who the Son is and what He has done.

"His Son is the radiance of his glory." Just as sunlight streams from the sun, the Son shines forth God's glory. The light and the sun cannot be separated; to see the light is to see the sun. In Jesus, the glory of God shines into the world.

"The very image of his substance." The word for "image" was used for the impression a seal makes in wax or the stamp on a coin. The Son exactly represents God's being. Everything God is, the Son reveals perfectly.

"And upholding all things by the word of his power." The Son not only created the universe; He sustains it moment by moment. His powerful word holds everything in existence and carries it toward its goal.

"Who, when he had by himself purified us of our sins." Here the description turns from cosmic glory to the cross. The Creator and Sustainer of the universe made purification for sins. He did it "by himself," through His own sacrifice.

"Sat down on the right hand of the Majesty on high." After completing His work, the Son sat down in the place of supreme honor beside God the Father. Sitting down signals that His work of purification is finished. Old Testament priests never sat down, because their work was never done (Hebrews 10:11–12).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is one of the clearest statements in Scripture of the Son's divine nature. He is not merely like God or sent by God; He is the radiance of God's glory and the exact representation of His being. The early church drew on verses like this when it confessed that the Son is "God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God," as the Nicene Creed puts it.

The verse also holds together the Son's majesty and His humility. The one who upholds the universe is the same one who purified sins, which in the language of Hebrews means through His death as a sacrifice. The glory of God is seen most clearly at the cross, where the Son gave Himself for sinners.

The phrase "sat down" anticipates one of the major themes of Hebrews: the finished, once-for-all nature of Christ's sacrifice. Nothing more needs to be added. Believers do not need to repeat sacrifices or earn their cleansing. Christ has done it, and He has sat down.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers who want to know what God is like can look at Jesus. His compassion, holiness, truth, and love are the exact representation of God's character.

This verse also brings security. The one who holds the universe together holds believers' lives together too. When life feels chaotic, they can remember that all things are upheld by His powerful word.

Finally, the truth that Christ has completed the work of purification brings peace. Believers do not need to carry the guilt of sins that Christ has already cleansed. They can rest in His finished work.

### Hebrews 1:8–9 — Your Throne, O God, Is Forever

verse: Hebrews 1:8-9

#### Simple Explanation

In contrast to the angels, who are servants (v7), the author quotes Psalm 45:6–7 about the Son.

"But of the Son he says, 'Your throne, O God, is forever and ever.'" The Son is addressed as "God." His throne is eternal. Psalm 45 was originally a royal wedding song for Israel's king, but its language goes beyond any human king, and the author of Hebrews sees its fulfillment in Christ, the eternal King.

"The scepter of uprightness is the scepter of your Kingdom." A scepter symbolized a king's authority. The Son's rule is marked by justice and righteousness.

Verse 9: "You have loved righteousness and hated iniquity." The Son's character is perfectly righteous. He loves what is right and hates what is evil.

"Therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness above your fellows." Kings were anointed with oil as a sign of their appointment. The Son is anointed with "the oil of gladness," a sign of joy and favor, above all others.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage is remarkable because it calls the Son "God" while also speaking of "God, your God." This reflects the mystery of the Trinity: the Son is fully God, and yet He is distinct from the Father. The New Testament holds these truths together in passages such as John 1:1 ("the Word was with God, and the Word was God") and Titus 2:13 ("our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ").

The Son's eternal throne also fulfills God's promise to David that his descendant's throne would be established forever (2 Samuel 7:13, 16). Jesus is the son of David and the Son of God, the eternal King who rules in righteousness.

The connection between righteousness and joy is also significant. The Son loves righteousness, and He is anointed with gladness. Holiness and joy are not opposites. True joy is found in loving what is good.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to worship Jesus as God and to trust His rule. In a world where earthly rulers come and go, often ruling unjustly, Christ's throne is eternal and His scepter is righteous. Believers can find hope in His just and unending reign.

They also invite believers to share the Son's love for righteousness and hatred of evil. Following Christ means loving what He loves and rejecting what He rejects.

Finally, the "oil of gladness" reminds believers that life with Christ is meant to be joyful. Holiness is not a grim burden but the path to the deepest joy.

### Hebrews 1:14 — Ministering Spirits

verse: Hebrews 1:14

#### Simple Explanation

The chapter ends with a question that sums up the role of angels. "Aren’t they all serving spirits, sent out to do service for the sake of those who will inherit salvation?"

Angels are "serving spirits." They are not rulers or objects of worship. They are servants of God.

They are "sent out to do service." God sends them on missions.

"For the sake of those who will inherit salvation." Remarkably, angels serve believers. Those who belong to Christ are heirs of salvation, and God's mighty angels are sent to help them.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse puts angels in their proper place. They are glorious and powerful beings, but they are servants, not the Son. And astonishingly, they serve God's people. Scripture gives many examples: angels protected Elisha (2 Kings 6:15–17), shut the lions' mouths for Daniel (Daniel 6:22), strengthened Jesus in Gethsemane (Luke 22:43), freed Peter from prison (Acts 12:7–11), and encouraged Paul in the storm (Acts 27:23–24). Psalm 91:11 says that God will put His angels in charge of His people to guard them.

This truth has two applications. First, believers should not worship angels or seek to communicate with them as though they were mediators. Only the Son is worthy of worship, and He is the one mediator between God and humanity (1 Timothy 2:5). Second, believers can take comfort that God cares for them in ways they often cannot see. His servants are at work on their behalf.

The phrase "those who will inherit salvation" also points forward. Salvation has a present and a future dimension. Believers already have it, and they will inherit it fully when Christ returns.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can trust that God cares for them even when they cannot see His hand. His protection and provision often come in ways they never notice.

This verse also guards against unhealthy fascination with angels. In some popular spirituality, angels receive attention and devotion that belong only to God. Hebrews keeps the focus where it belongs: on the Son.

Finally, the description of believers as "those who will inherit salvation" reminds them of their great future. They are heirs. Their inheritance is secure, and God Himself, with all His angels, is committed to bringing them safely to it.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Hebrews 1 lifts believers' eyes to the greatness of Jesus Christ. God once spoke through the prophets in many partial ways, but now He has spoken fully and finally through His Son. The Son is the heir of all things, the Creator of the universe, the radiance of God's glory, and the exact representation of His being. He holds all things together by His powerful word. And He has made purification for sins and sat down at the right hand of God, His work complete.

This vision of Christ calls for worship. Scripture shows that the Son is far greater than the angels. He alone is called God's Son; the angels worship Him; He is addressed as God whose throne is forever; He is the unchanging Creator; and He alone is invited to sit at God's right hand. Angels, glorious as they are, are servants sent to help God's people.

For believers who are weary or tempted to drift, this chapter offers a powerful reason to hold fast. There is no greater word, no greater revelation, no greater Savior than Jesus. Turning away from Him would mean turning away from God's final word. Believers can listen to Him, trust Him, and rest in His finished work.

The chapter also brings comfort. The one who upholds the universe upholds the lives of His people. His throne is eternal and His rule is righteous. And God's angels are at work on behalf of those who will inherit salvation. Whatever believers face, they belong to the Son, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever.
