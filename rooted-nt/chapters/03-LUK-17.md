# Luke 17
theme: Forgiveness, Humble Service, Gratitude, and Readiness for the Kingdom
main: Jesus warns against causing others to stumble, calls His disciples to rebuke and forgive repeatedly, and teaches that faith grows as humble servants do their duty without seeking praise. Of ten men He heals of leprosy, only one, a Samaritan, returns to thank Him. When asked when God's kingdom will come, Jesus says it is already present among them, and then warns that the Son of Man's coming will be sudden, like the flood in Noah's day.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 17 continues Jesus' journey to Jerusalem. Luke reminds us of this in verse 11: [[v11|As he was on his way to Jerusalem]]. The chapter begins with short teachings for the disciples about relationships within the community (vv. 1–10), then records the healing of ten lepers (vv. 11–19), and ends with teaching about the coming of God's kingdom and the return of the Son of Man (vv. 20–37). Its themes include forgiveness, faith, humility, gratitude, and readiness.

### Stumbling Blocks and Forgiveness (17:1–4)

Jesus told His disciples that [[v1|It is impossible that no occasions of stumbling should come]]. In a fallen world, temptations and causes of sin are inevitable. But [[v1|woe to him through whom they come!]] It would be better for such a person to be thrown into the sea with a millstone around his neck than to cause one of these [[v2|little ones]] to stumble. "Little ones" refers to vulnerable believers, especially those who are young in faith. (See the discussion at Matthew 18:6 and Mark 9:42.)

Then Jesus said, [[v3|Be careful]], and gave instructions about how to respond when a fellow believer sins. This is studied below.

### Increase Our Faith (17:5–10)

The apostles responded, [[v5|Increase our faith]]. Perhaps they felt overwhelmed by Jesus' demanding teaching on forgiveness. Jesus answered, [[v6|If you had faith like a grain of mustard seed, you would tell this sycamore tree, ‘Be uprooted and be planted in the sea,’ and it would obey you]]. The mulberry or sycamine tree had deep, extensive roots and was very hard to uproot. Jesus' point was that the size of faith is less important than its object. Even small faith in a great God can accomplish what seems impossible, including the "impossible" task of forgiving repeatedly. (A similar saying is studied at Matthew 17:20.)

Jesus then told a short parable about a servant. When a servant comes in from plowing or tending sheep, his master does not tell him to sit down and eat; he tells him to prepare supper and serve him first. The master does not thank the servant for doing what he was told. Jesus concluded, [[v10|Even so you also, when you have done all the things that are commanded you, say, ‘We are unworthy servants. We have done our duty.’]]

This parable is not about how God treats His people; elsewhere Jesus describes the master serving his faithful servants (Luke 12:37). It is about the attitude disciples should have. They should not think that obeying God earns them special credit or puts God in their debt. Obedience is simply what servants owe their Lord. "Unworthy" here means something like "ordinary" or "not deserving of special reward." This teaching guards against pride. Even after forgiving seven times a day, the disciples should not think they have done something remarkable; they have simply done their duty.

### The Ten Lepers (17:11–19)

As Jesus traveled along the border between Samaria and Galilee, He entered a village. Ten men with leprosy met Him, standing at a distance as the law required (Leviticus 13:45–46). In the Bible, "leprosy" referred to various skin diseases that made a person ritually unclean and excluded them from the community. These ten had formed their own group, bound together by shared suffering. Their group included both Jews and at least one Samaritan; their common disease had broken down ethnic barriers.

They called out, [[v13|Jesus, Master, have mercy on us!]] Jesus told them, [[v14|Go and show yourselves to the priests]]. According to the law, a person healed of a skin disease had to be examined by a priest before being declared clean and allowed back into community life (Leviticus 14). Jesus sent them before they were healed, testing their faith. [[v14|As they went, they were cleansed]].

One of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back, praising God with a loud voice. He fell on his face at Jesus' feet and thanked Him. Luke adds, [[v16|and he was a Samaritan]]. This is studied below. Jesus asked where the other nine were, and whether no one except this foreigner returned to give glory to God. Then He said to the Samaritan, [[v19|Get up, and go your way. Your faith has healed you]]. The word for "healed" can also mean "saved." All ten were cleansed of leprosy, but the Samaritan who returned received something more: a relationship with Jesus and a declaration of salvation.

### The Coming of the Kingdom (17:20–37)

The Pharisees asked Jesus when God's kingdom would come. Many Jews expected the kingdom to arrive with dramatic signs, such as the overthrow of Rome and the restoration of Israel's independence. Jesus answered, [[v20|God’s Kingdom doesn’t come with observation]], meaning not with signs that can be tracked and calculated. People will not say, "Here it is!" or "There it is!" [[v21|for behold, God’s Kingdom is within you]]. This is studied below.

Then Jesus turned to His disciples and spoke about the future. A time would come when they would long to see one of the days of the Son of Man but would not see it. People would claim, [[v23|Look, here!]] or [[v23|Look, there!]] They should not follow such claims. When the Son of Man comes, it will be like lightning flashing across the sky, sudden and visible to all. [[v25|But first, he must suffer many things and be rejected by this generation]]. The cross had to come before the glory.

Jesus compared His coming to the days of Noah and Lot. People were going about ordinary life, eating, drinking, marrying, buying, selling, planting, building, until judgment came suddenly: the flood in Noah's day, and fire and sulfur on Sodom in Lot's day. They were not wicked because they ate and built; their problem was that they lived as though God did not matter and ignored His warnings. So it will be when the Son of Man is revealed.

On that day, a person on the housetop should not go down to get his belongings, and a person in the field should not turn back. [[v32|Remember Lot’s wife!]] As she fled Sodom, she looked back with longing and became a pillar of salt (Genesis 19:26). Jesus warned against clinging to the things of this world. [[v33|Whoever seeks to save his life loses it, but whoever loses his life preserves it]].

Jesus said that on that night, two people will be in one bed; one will be taken and the other left. Two women will be grinding grain together; one will be taken and the other left. (Verse 36, about two in a field, is missing from the oldest manuscripts.) The disciples asked, [[v37|Where, Lord?]] Jesus answered with a proverb: [[v37|Where the body is, there the vultures will also be gathered together]]. Just as vultures inevitably gather where there is a carcass, judgment will come wherever it is deserved. The coming of the Son of Man will be unmistakable. (See the discussion of similar teaching at Matthew 24.)

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 17:3–4 — Rebuke and Forgive

verse: Luke 17:3-4

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to His disciples right after warning them about causing others to stumble. He had said that it would be better for someone to be thrown into the sea with a millstone around his neck than to cause a vulnerable believer to sin. Now He turns to the other side of community life: how to respond when someone sins against you.

[[v3|Be careful]]. This means "watch yourselves" or "pay attention to yourselves." It may refer back to the warning about causing others to stumble, or forward to the teaching that follows. Either way, Jesus calls His followers to watchfulness in their relationships.

[[v3|If your brother sins against you, rebuke him]]. "Brother" refers to a fellow disciple. To "rebuke" means to point out the wrong honestly. Jesus does not tell His followers to ignore sin or pretend it did not happen. Love sometimes requires honest confrontation. (Matthew 18:15 gives fuller instructions about going privately to the person.)

[[v3|If he repents, forgive him]]. When the person acknowledges the wrong and turns from it, the response must be forgiveness. Forgiveness means releasing the debt and giving up the right to hold the offense against them.

[[v4|If he sins against you seven times in the day, and seven times returns, saying, ‘I repent,’ you shall forgive him]]. "Seven times in a day" is a striking picture of repeated offenses, almost absurdly frequent. The number seven suggests completeness. Jesus means that there is no limit to forgiveness when someone sincerely repents. In Matthew 18:22, Jesus tells Peter to forgive not seven times but seventy times seven.

The apostles' immediate response was, [[v5|Increase our faith]]. They recognized that this kind of forgiveness required more faith than they had.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses hold together truth and grace. Jesus does not call His followers to ignore sin, nor to hold grudges. He calls them to honest confrontation and generous forgiveness. Both are expressions of love. Rebuke without forgiveness becomes harsh; forgiveness without honesty can enable ongoing harm.

They also reflect God's own forgiveness. God forgives His people repeatedly when they repent. Believers who have received such forgiveness are called to extend it to others. Paul writes, [[Colossians 3:13|even as Christ forgave you, so you also do]].

It is important to understand that forgiveness does not always mean immediate restoration of trust, especially when someone has caused serious harm. Forgiveness releases the desire for revenge and the debt owed; rebuilding trust may take time and evidence of real change. In cases of abuse, forgiveness can coexist with protective boundaries and with seeking justice through appropriate authorities.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to address wrongs honestly rather than holding silent resentment or gossiping about others. When someone has sinned against them, they can speak to that person directly and gently, with the goal of restoration.

They also call believers to forgive generously when others repent. It can be difficult to forgive someone who keeps failing in the same way. Jesus asks His followers to keep forgiving, trusting God for the strength to do so.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to ask God for more faith, as the apostles did. Forgiveness is often beyond human strength. Believers can pray for God's help to forgive and to love those who hurt them, while also using wisdom about trust and boundaries.

### Luke 17:10 — [[Luke 17:10|We Are Unworthy Servants]]

verse: Luke 17:10

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes a short parable Jesus told after the apostles asked Him to increase their faith. He had just taught them to forgive repeatedly, even seven times in a day, and He had told them that even faith as small as a mustard seed could uproot a tree.

Then He told a parable about a servant. Imagine having a servant who has been out plowing or tending sheep all day. When he comes in, the master would not say, [[v7|Come immediately and sit down at the table]]. Instead, he would say, [[v8|Prepare my supper, clothe yourself properly, and serve me while I eat and drink. Afterward you shall eat and drink]]. The master would not thank the servant for doing what he was told, because that was simply the servant's job.

Jesus applied this: [[v10|Even so you also, when you have done all the things that are commanded you, say, ‘We are unworthy servants. We have done our duty.’]]

"Unworthy servants" does not mean worthless people. It means servants who have no special claim on their master, who have not earned extra reward. They have simply done what they were supposed to do.

The parable addresses the disciples' attitude. After hearing Jesus' demanding teaching on forgiveness, the disciples might have thought that if they managed to forgive seven times a day, they would deserve special credit. Jesus warned against that kind of thinking. Obedience to God is not a way of putting God in their debt; it is simply the right response of servants to their Lord.

In the ancient world, servants (or slaves) were expected to obey without receiving thanks. Jesus used this familiar arrangement to make a point about humility, not to approve of slavery or to describe God's character as harsh. Elsewhere, Jesus says that the faithful servant will be welcomed by the master, who will even serve him (Luke 12:37).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches humility in service. Believers can be tempted to think that their good works earn them special favor with God, or to keep score of their obedience. Jesus corrects this attitude. Salvation and all of God's blessings are gifts of grace, not wages earned. Paul writes, [[Romans 4:4|Now to him who works, the reward is not counted as grace, but as something owed]]. God owes no one anything.

It also guards against spiritual pride. When believers serve faithfully, give generously, or forgive repeatedly, they may be tempted to feel superior to others. Jesus' words remind them that they have only done what they should.

At the same time, this verse should be read alongside Jesus' other teachings. God does reward His faithful servants generously (Luke 19:17), not because they have earned it, but because He is gracious. The attitude of humble servanthood frees believers from pride and from the burden of trying to earn God's favor.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to serve God without seeking praise or recognition. Whether they serve in church, care for family, or help others, they can do so humbly, recognizing that they are simply doing what God asks.

It also frees believers from keeping score. They do not need to track their good deeds or compare them with others. Their relationship with God rests on His grace, not on their performance.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to find joy in obedience itself. Serving God is a privilege, not a burden to be rewarded. Believers can say with humility and gladness, "We have done our duty," and trust God's generous heart.

### Luke 17:15–16 — The One Who Returned to Give Thanks

verse: Luke 17:15-16

#### Simple Explanation

These verses describe the response of one man among ten who were healed of leprosy. As Jesus traveled along the border between Samaria and Galilee on His way to Jerusalem, ten men with leprosy met Him. They stood at a distance, as required by law, and called out, "Jesus, Master, have mercy on us!" Jesus told them to go and show themselves to the priests, who could declare them clean and allow them to return to their families and communities. As they went, they were healed.

Then [[v15|One of them, when he saw that he was healed, turned back, glorifying God with a loud voice]]. He did not continue to the priests immediately. He turned around and came back to Jesus. He had probably called out to Jesus from a distance before; now he praised God loudly, no longer ashamed or isolated.

[[v16|He fell on his face at Jesus’ feet, giving him thanks]]. Falling at someone's feet showed deep humility and worship. He thanked Jesus personally for his healing. Notice that he glorified God and thanked Jesus, suggesting that he recognized God's power working through Jesus.

Then Luke adds the surprise: [[v16|and he was a Samaritan]]. Samaritans were despised by Jews as religious heretics and ethnic outsiders. Yet this Samaritan was the only one who returned to give thanks. The other nine, presumably Jews, received healing but did not return.

Jesus asked, [[v17|Weren’t the ten cleansed? But where are the nine?]] and [[v18|Were there none found who returned to give glory to God, except this foreigner?]] Then He told the Samaritan, [[v19|Get up, and go your way. Your faith has healed you]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal that gratitude is a mark of genuine faith. All ten were healed physically, but only one returned to thank Jesus. The word Jesus used to the Samaritan, "Your faith has healed you," can also mean "saved you." The Samaritan received more than physical healing; he received a personal encounter with Jesus and a declaration of salvation.

They also show that God's grace reaches outsiders. Luke repeatedly highlights Samaritans responding to God: the Good Samaritan in Jesus' parable (Luke 10:30–37) and now this healed man. In Acts, Samaritans would receive the gospel with joy (Acts 8:4–8). God's kingdom crosses ethnic and religious boundaries.

Finally, the story warns against taking God's blessings for granted. The nine may have been grateful in their hearts, or they may have been eager to return to normal life. Either way, they did not come back to honor the one who healed them. It is possible to receive God's gifts without truly turning to God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to practice gratitude. Every good thing in life comes from God: health, provision, relationships, salvation, and countless daily mercies. Believers can make a habit of returning to God in thanksgiving, both in private prayer and in public worship.

They also challenge believers not to be like the nine, who received blessings but moved on without acknowledging God. When prayers are answered or difficulties are resolved, believers can pause to thank God before rushing back to normal life.

Finally, these verses remind believers that people they consider outsiders may show greater faith and gratitude than those who seem to be insiders. Believers can welcome and learn from people of every background who respond to God with sincere hearts.

### Luke 17:21 — God's Kingdom Is in Your Midst

verse: Luke 17:21

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is part of Jesus' answer to a question from the Pharisees. They asked Him when God's kingdom would come. Many Jews of that time expected the kingdom to arrive with dramatic signs: the defeat of Israel's enemies, the restoration of David's throne, and visible cosmic events. They may have expected Jesus to give them a timeline or a list of signs to watch for.

Jesus answered, [[v20|God’s Kingdom doesn’t come with observation]]. The word "observation" refers to watching carefully, the way someone watches for signs to calculate a date. The kingdom would not arrive in a way that could be tracked and predicted by signs.

[[v21|neither will they say, ‘Look, here!’ or, ‘Look, there!’]] People would not be able to point to one location and say, "The kingdom is over there."

[[v21|for behold, God’s Kingdom is within you]]. The Greek phrase translated "within you" can also mean "among you" or "in your midst." Many scholars prefer "among you" in this context, because Jesus was speaking to Pharisees who opposed Him; it seems unlikely that He meant the kingdom was in their hearts. He likely meant that the kingdom was already present among them in His own person and ministry. The King had come, and His healings, exorcisms, and teaching showed that God's reign was breaking into the world. Others take "within you" to emphasize the inward, spiritual nature of the kingdom: it begins in people's hearts as they submit to God's rule. Both truths are taught in Scripture.

The Pharisees were looking for a future political event, while the kingdom was already standing in front of them in Jesus. Yet in the following verses, Jesus also spoke to His disciples about a future coming of the Son of Man, which would be sudden and visible to all. The kingdom has both a present reality and a future fulfillment.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that God's kingdom is present wherever Jesus is. It is not merely a future hope but a present reality. Wherever people submit to Jesus as King, wherever the sick are healed and sinners forgiven, the kingdom is at work. Earlier Jesus said, [[Luke 11:20|But if I by God’s finger cast out demons, then God’s Kingdom has come to you]].

It also warns against being so focused on future signs that one misses God's present work. The Pharisees wanted dramatic signs but failed to recognize the King standing among them. People today can become absorbed in speculation about the end times while ignoring Jesus' call to follow Him now.

Finally, the verse, together with the following teaching, shows the "already and not yet" nature of the kingdom. It has already come in Jesus, but it will be fully revealed when He returns. Believers live between these two realities, experiencing the kingdom now while waiting for its completion.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to recognize God's kingdom in the present. They do not need to wait for some future event to experience God's reign. They can submit to Jesus as King today, allowing His rule to shape their thoughts, words, and actions.

It also encourages believers to look for signs of God's kingdom around them: lives transformed, relationships restored, the poor cared for, and the gospel spreading. These are evidence that the kingdom is at work.

Finally, this verse warns against being consumed by speculation about the future. While Jesus will return, and believers should be ready, their primary focus should be on following Him faithfully now. The kingdom is "in your midst," calling for a response today.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 17 covers several aspects of discipleship. It begins with relationships within the community of faith. Believers must be careful not to cause others, especially vulnerable believers, to stumble. When others sin against them, they should speak honestly and forgive generously whenever there is repentance, even repeatedly. This kind of forgiveness requires faith, and believers can ask God to increase it.

The parable of the servant teaches humility. Believers should serve God faithfully without expecting special praise or thinking that their obedience earns them favor. Everything they have is a gift of grace. This humility frees them from pride and from the burden of trying to earn God's love.

The story of the ten lepers calls believers to gratitude. All ten were healed, but only one returned to give thanks, and he was a Samaritan. Believers can make a habit of returning to God in thanksgiving and can welcome people of every background who respond to God with sincere faith.

Finally, Jesus' teaching about the kingdom reminds believers that God's reign is already present in Jesus, and that it will be fully revealed when He returns. His return will be sudden and unmistakable, like the flood in Noah's day. Believers can live faithfully now, not clinging to the things of this world like Lot's wife, but ready at all times to meet the Son of Man.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, keep me from causing anyone to stumble, especially those who are young or vulnerable in faith. Give me the courage to speak honestly when someone wrongs me, and the grace to forgive them whenever they repent, even again and again. Increase my faith, because I cannot forgive like this by my own strength.

Teach me to serve You with humility, without seeking praise or thinking that I have earned Your favor. Make me like the Samaritan who returned to thank You, and keep me from taking Your blessings for granted. Thank You that Your kingdom is present wherever You reign. Help me to live under Your rule today, and keep me ready for the day of Your return, not clinging to the things of this world. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
