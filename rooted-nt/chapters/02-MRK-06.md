# Mark 6
theme: Rejected at Home, Sent Out, and Still Slow to Understand
main: Jesus is dismissed by His hometown, then sends the Twelve out to preach and heal. Mark interrupts their mission with the story of John the Baptist's execution by Herod, a man who enjoyed listening to John yet killed him to protect his pride. When the apostles return, Jesus invites them to rest, feeds five thousand people out of compassion, and walks on the sea to His struggling disciples, who still fail to grasp who He is.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 6 shows the widening reach of Jesus' ministry and the growing danger surrounding it. In the previous chapter, Jesus had freed a man from a legion of demons, healed a woman of a twelve-year illness, and raised a girl from death. One might expect everyone to welcome Him. Instead, Mark 6 opens with rejection in His own hometown, and the chapter includes the brutal death of John the Baptist, foreshadowing what will happen to Jesus. At the same time, Jesus extends His mission through the Twelve and reveals His identity through two extraordinary miracles.

### Rejected at Nazareth (6:1–6)

Jesus returned to His hometown of Nazareth, a small village in the hills of Galilee, with His disciples. On the Sabbath He taught in the synagogue, and many were astonished. They asked where He got this wisdom and power. But their amazement turned into offense: [[v3|Isn’t this the carpenter, the son of Mary and brother of James, Joses, Judah, and Simon? Aren’t his sisters here with us?]]

The word "carpenter" can refer to a builder who worked with wood or stone. In a village like Nazareth, such a craftsman would build and repair houses, furniture, and farm tools. The townspeople knew Jesus' family and trade. Calling Him "the son of Mary," rather than the son of Joseph, was unusual in that culture and may have been an insult implying questions about His birth. (Christians have understood Jesus' "brothers and sisters" in different ways, as children of Mary and Joseph, as children of Joseph from an earlier marriage, or as close relatives; see the note at Matthew 13:55.) Their familiarity with Jesus' ordinary background made it hard for them to accept Him as anyone special.

Jesus replied, [[v4|A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country, and among his own relatives, and in his own house]]. Then Mark makes a striking statement: [[v5|He could do no mighty work there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them]]. This does not mean Jesus' power was limited by human unbelief in an absolute sense; He still healed some. But Jesus' miracles were not shows to compel belief. Where people refused to trust Him, He did not force His works upon them. Mark adds that Jesus [[v6|marveled because of their unbelief]]. Elsewhere Jesus marveled at great faith (Matthew 8:10); here He marveled at unbelief.

### Sending Out the Twelve (6:7–13)

Jesus then called the Twelve and sent them out two by two, giving them authority over unclean spirits. Sending them in pairs provided companionship, accountability, and the two witnesses required by Jewish law to confirm testimony (Deuteronomy 19:15).

He told them to take nothing for the journey except a staff: no bread, no bag, no money in their belts. They could wear sandals but not take an extra tunic. (Mark's list differs slightly from Matthew's and Luke's, which forbid a staff or sandals; the Gospel writers' varying details all emphasize simplicity and dependence on God.) They were to stay in whatever house welcomed them until they left that town. If a place refused to welcome or listen to them, they were to shake the dust off their feet as a testimony against it.

The Twelve went out and preached that people should repent. They drove out many demons and anointed many sick people with oil and healed them. Olive oil was used as medicine in the ancient world, and anointing also symbolized God's blessing and healing presence. James 5:14 later instructs church elders to anoint the sick with oil and pray for them.

### The Death of John the Baptist (6:14–29)

News of Jesus' ministry reached King Herod. This was Herod Antipas, son of Herod the Great, who ruled Galilee and Perea. He held the title "tetrarch," but Mark calls him "king," perhaps reflecting popular usage or a touch of irony. People had different opinions about Jesus: some said He was John the Baptist raised from the dead, others said He was Elijah, others a prophet. But Herod said, [[v16|This is John, whom I beheaded. He has risen from the dead]]. His guilty conscience haunted him.

Mark then tells the story of John's death in a flashback. Herod had arrested John because John told him, [[v18|It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife]]. Herod had married Herodias, who had been married to his half-brother. The law of Moses forbade this (Leviticus 18:16). Herodias held a grudge against John and wanted to kill him, but she could not, because Herod feared John, knowing he was a righteous and holy man. Herod protected him and liked to listen to him, even though John's words greatly perplexed him. This is studied below.

Finally Herodias found her opportunity. At Herod's birthday banquet for his officials, military commanders, and leading men of Galilee, Herodias's daughter danced and pleased Herod and his guests. Herod promised with an oath to give her anything she asked, up to half his kingdom, an exaggerated boast, since his kingdom was not his to divide. She asked her mother what to request, and Herodias said, [[v24|The head of John the Baptizer]]. The girl hurried back and asked for John's head on a platter, immediately.

Herod was deeply distressed, but because of his oaths and his guests, he did not want to refuse her. He sent an executioner, who beheaded John in prison and brought his head on a platter. John's disciples came and took his body and laid it in a tomb.

Mark places this story between the sending of the Twelve and their return. The placement is significant: those who preach God's message may face the same fate as John. It also foreshadows Jesus' own death at the hands of a ruler who knew He was innocent but gave in to pressure.

### The Apostles Return and the Feeding of the Five Thousand (6:30–44)

The apostles returned and reported to Jesus everything they had done and taught. Because so many people were coming and going that they did not even have time to eat, Jesus said, [[v31|Come away into a deserted place, and rest awhile]]. This is studied below.

They went by boat to a solitary place, but many people saw them leaving, ran ahead on foot from all the towns, and arrived first. When Jesus landed and saw the large crowd, [[v34|he had compassion on them because they were like sheep without a shepherd]]. He began teaching them many things. The phrase "sheep without a shepherd" recalls Numbers 27:17 and Ezekiel 34, where God promised to shepherd His people Himself. The contrast with Herod's feast is striking: Herod, the ruler, threw a lavish party that ended in murder; Jesus, the true shepherd-king, taught and fed the people in a deserted place.

Late in the day, the disciples urged Jesus to send the people away to buy food. Jesus answered, [[v37|You give them something to eat]]. They asked if they should spend two hundred denarii, about eight months' wages, on bread. Jesus told them to see how many loaves they had. They found five loaves and two fish.

Jesus had the people sit down in groups on the green grass. Mark's detail about green grass suggests springtime, around Passover (John 6:4), and recalls Psalm 23: [[Psalm 23:2|He makes me lie down in green pastures]]. The people sat in groups of hundreds and fifties, recalling how Moses organized Israel in the wilderness (Exodus 18:21). Jesus took the loaves and fish, looked up to heaven, blessed them, broke the loaves, and gave them to His disciples to distribute. Everyone ate and was satisfied, and the disciples gathered twelve baskets full of leftovers. About five thousand men had eaten.

### Walking on the Sea (6:45–52)

Immediately Jesus made His disciples get into the boat and go ahead of Him to Bethsaida while He dismissed the crowd. Then He went up on a mountainside to pray. This is the second time Mark records Jesus withdrawing to pray (see Mark 1:35). John 6:15 tells us that the crowd wanted to make Jesus king by force, which may explain the urgency of His actions.

During the night, the disciples were straining at the oars because the wind was against them. Jesus saw them, and about the fourth watch of the night (between 3 and 6 a.m.), He came toward them, walking on the sea. Mark adds a puzzling detail: [[v48|and he would have passed by them]]. This phrase echoes Old Testament passages where God "passed by" Moses and Elijah to reveal His glory (Exodus 33:19–22; 1 Kings 19:11). Jesus was revealing Himself in a way that only God had done before.

When the disciples saw Him, they thought He was a ghost and cried out in terror. Immediately He spoke to them: [[v50|Cheer up! It is I! Don’t be afraid]]. "It is I" is literally "I am," recalling God's name revealed to Moses (Exodus 3:14). He climbed into the boat, and the wind died down. The disciples were completely amazed, [[v52|for they hadn’t understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened]]. This is studied below.

### Healings at Gennesaret (6:53–56)

When they crossed over, they landed at Gennesaret. People recognized Jesus immediately and ran throughout the region bringing the sick on mats. Wherever He went, in villages, towns, or countryside, they placed the sick in the marketplaces and begged to touch even the fringe of His cloak, and all who touched it were healed. The chapter ends with a picture of overflowing compassion, in contrast to the unbelief of Nazareth at its beginning.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 6:20 — Enjoying the Truth Without Obeying It

verse: Mark 6:20

#### Simple Explanation

This verse explains Herod's complicated relationship with John the Baptist. Herod had arrested John and put him in prison because John publicly condemned Herod's marriage to Herodias, the former wife of his half-brother. Herodias was furious and wanted John dead. But Herod would not allow it, at least not yet.

Mark gives the reason: [[v20|for Herod feared John, knowing that he was a righteous and holy man, and kept him safe]]. Herod recognized that John was a genuine man of God. He was afraid of him, perhaps because of John's popularity with the people (Matthew 14:5), but also because he sensed John's holiness. He protected John from Herodias's schemes.

Then Mark adds something surprising: [[v20|When he heard him, he did many things, and he heard him gladly]]. Many manuscripts read, "he was greatly perplexed." Herod would summon John from prison to hear him speak. John's words disturbed and confused him, yet Herod enjoyed listening. He was fascinated by John's message.

But listening was not enough. Herod never repented of his sin or released John. When his pride was at stake at the birthday banquet, in front of important guests, he chose to keep his rash oath rather than protect the man he knew was righteous. He was [[v26|exceedingly sorry]], but he still gave the order.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that it is possible to be attracted to the truth, even moved by it, without ever submitting to it. Herod respected John, listened to him, and was troubled by his words. But he did not change. His fascination with John's message did not lead to repentance. In the end, his pride and fear of others' opinions were stronger than his respect for God's messenger.

Herod's story illustrates the parable of the sower: some hear the word but allow other things to choke it (Mark 4:18–19). It also illustrates the warning of James 1:22 about hearing without doing. Listening to God's word can be pleasant and even moving, but if it never leads to obedience, the heart grows harder over time.

Herod's choice also shows the danger of small compromises. He had married unlawfully, imprisoned a prophet, and made a rash oath while trying to impress his guests. Each step made the next one easier. By the time the crisis came, he was trapped by his own choices and his concern for appearances.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine whether they enjoy hearing God's word without obeying it. It is possible to enjoy sermons, read Christian books, and be moved by Scripture while refusing to change in areas God has clearly addressed. Believers can ask whether there is a truth God has been pressing on them that they have avoided acting on.

It also warns against letting pride and fear of others' opinions override conscience. Herod gave the order to kill John because he did not want to lose face before his guests. Believers face similar pressures in smaller ways: to go along with what others expect, to avoid embarrassment, or to keep a careless promise even when it requires doing wrong. Integrity sometimes means admitting a mistake publicly rather than compounding it.

Finally, Herod's story encourages believers to respond to God's word while their hearts are still tender. Being troubled by God's truth is an opportunity for repentance. Delaying that response can lead to deeper hardness.

### Mark 6:31 — Come Away and Rest Awhile

verse: Mark 6:31

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes when the twelve apostles returned from their mission. Jesus had sent them out two by two with authority to preach, cast out demons, and heal the sick. They had done exactly that and now came back to tell Jesus [[v30|all things, whatever they had done, and whatever they had taught]]. They were likely excited, exhausted, and full of stories. At the same time, they had probably just heard the devastating news of John the Baptist's execution.

Mark explains the situation: [[v31|For there were many coming and going, and they had no leisure so much as to eat]]. The demands of ministry were so constant that they could not even eat a meal in peace.

Jesus responded with a caring invitation: [[v31|Come away into a deserted place, and rest awhile]]. The words "come away" and "by yourselves" (as the following verse says they went "by themselves") emphasize separation from the crowds. "A deserted place" means a quiet, uninhabited area. "Rest awhile" acknowledges that rest is needed, but also that it is temporary; there would be more work later.

Ironically, their rest was interrupted. The crowds saw where they were going and arrived first. Jesus responded with compassion and taught and fed them. But His invitation still reveals His concern for His disciples' need for rest.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus cares about the well-being of those who serve Him. He did not treat His disciples as tools to be used until they broke. He recognized their physical and emotional need for rest, food, and time away from constant demands. Rest is part of God's design for human life, built into creation through the Sabbath.

The invitation also echoes Jesus' broader call to rest found in Matthew 11:28: [[Matthew 11:28|Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest]]. True rest is found in His presence. He invited the disciples to come away with Him, not merely to stop working.

The interruption that followed also teaches something. Ministry and life are often unpredictable. Jesus held both rest and responsiveness together. He planned for rest and also adapted compassionately when needs arose. Later that night, He took time to pray alone (v. 46). The rhythm of engagement and withdrawal appears throughout His ministry.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse gives believers permission, even encouragement, to rest. Many people, including those who serve in churches and ministries, feel guilty when they rest, as if it shows a lack of commitment. Jesus' words show that rest is not laziness; it is part of faithful living. Believers can build regular times of rest and renewal into their lives.

It also invites believers to rest with Jesus. True rest involves more than stopping activity; it includes spending time with Him, enjoying His presence, and being refreshed by Him. Quiet times of prayer, Scripture, worship, and reflection can restore the soul.

Finally, those who lead or supervise others can follow Jesus' example by caring for the well-being of those who serve. Encouraging rest, noticing exhaustion, and creating space for renewal are ways of showing Christ's care.

### Mark 6:52 — Hearts That Did Not Understand the Loaves

verse: Mark 6:52

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is Mark's comment on the disciples' reaction after Jesus walked on the water. Earlier that day, they had seen Jesus feed five thousand men with five loaves and two fish, and they had gathered twelve baskets of leftovers. Then Jesus sent them across the lake while He prayed. During the night, they struggled against the wind. Jesus came to them walking on the sea, and they cried out in fear, thinking He was a ghost. He spoke to them, "It is I; don't be afraid," and climbed into the boat. The wind stopped.

Mark says they were [[v51|very amazed among themselves, and marveled]]. Then he explains why they were so shocked: [[v52|for they hadn’t understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened]].

What should they have understood about the loaves? The feeding of the five thousand revealed that Jesus had divine power to provide, like God feeding Israel with manna in the wilderness. If they had understood that miracle, they would not have been astonished that He could walk on water and calm the wind. Both miracles pointed to the same truth: Jesus has the authority of God over creation.

"Their hearts were hardened" is a surprising statement about Jesus' own disciples. The same phrase was used about the Pharisees in Mark 3:5. Here it does not mean the disciples were hostile to Jesus, but that they were spiritually dull, slow to grasp the meaning of what they had seen. Mark is honest about the disciples' struggles throughout his Gospel. Later Jesus will ask them directly, [[Mark 8:17|Is your heart still hardened?]]

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that seeing miracles does not automatically produce understanding or faith. The disciples had witnessed extraordinary events, yet they still did not grasp who Jesus was. Spiritual understanding requires more than experiences; it requires hearts open to God's revelation.

It also shows Mark's honesty about the disciples. They were not heroes of faith at this point. They were slow, fearful, and confused. This encourages readers who struggle to understand or trust. If Jesus' closest followers needed time to grow, so do believers today. Jesus patiently continued teaching them.

Finally, the verse connects remembering and trusting. If the disciples had reflected on what Jesus did with the loaves, they would have been less afraid in the storm. Remembering God's past faithfulness strengthens trust in present trials.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to reflect on what God has already shown them. Many believers have experienced God's provision, protection, and faithfulness in the past. Yet when new difficulties arise, they react as if God had never helped them before. Taking time to remember and give thanks for past answers to prayer can strengthen faith for present challenges.

It also encourages believers to ask God for soft, understanding hearts. Spiritual dullness can affect even committed followers. Believers can pray for insight to understand Scripture and to recognize God's work in their lives.

Finally, this verse offers grace to those who feel slow to learn. Jesus did not abandon His confused disciples. He kept teaching, correcting, and revealing Himself to them. Believers can trust that He will patiently continue His work in them, too.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 6 presents many contrasting responses to Jesus and His message. The people of Nazareth let familiarity keep them from faith. Herod enjoyed John's preaching but would not obey it. The crowds pursued Jesus eagerly. The disciples served faithfully yet were slow to understand. Each response invites reflection: are we open to Jesus even when He comes in ordinary ways, and do we respond to God's word with obedience rather than mere interest?

The sending of the Twelve shows that Jesus shares His mission with His followers. They went out in pairs, depending on God's provision and preaching repentance. Believers today are also sent to share the good news and show compassion, often in partnership with others and with trust in God's care.

John the Baptist's death warns about the cost of faithfulness and the danger of compromise. John paid with his life for speaking truth to power. Herod, by contrast, sacrificed his conscience to protect his image. Believers can ask God for John's courage and guard against Herod's pattern of small compromises leading to great sin.

Jesus' invitation to rest shows His care for those who serve. Faithful discipleship includes rhythms of work and rest, engagement and withdrawal, service and prayer. Finally, the feeding of the five thousand and the walk on water reveal Jesus as the compassionate shepherd and the Lord of creation. Believers can trust Him to provide and to come to them in their struggles, and they can remember His past faithfulness when new storms arise.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, forgive me for the times I have let familiarity or assumptions keep me from trusting You. Thank You for sending Your followers out to share Your message and for giving them what they need. Give me the courage of John the Baptist to speak the truth, and guard me from Herod's habit of enjoying Your word without obeying it.

Thank You for caring about my need for rest. Teach me to come away with You and be renewed. Thank You for Your compassion on the crowds, for feeding them, and for coming to Your struggling disciples across the waves. When I am afraid, help me to remember what You have already done for me, and soften my heart so that I understand who You are. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
