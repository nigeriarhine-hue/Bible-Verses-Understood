# Luke 3
theme: Prepare the Way: Repentance That Changes Everyday Life
main: In a precisely dated moment of history, God's word comes to John the Baptist, who calls people to a repentance that shows itself in generosity, honesty, and justice. John points beyond himself to the One who will baptize with the Holy Spirit and fire, and he is imprisoned for confronting Herod. Jesus is baptized while praying, the Spirit descends, and the Father declares Him His beloved Son. Luke then traces Jesus' ancestry all the way back to Adam, showing that He belongs to all humanity.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 3 moves forward about eighteen years from the end of Luke 2, where the twelve-year-old Jesus was found in the temple and then returned to Nazareth to grow in wisdom and stature. Now John the Baptist begins his public ministry, and Jesus is baptized and begins His work. Luke's account parallels Matthew 3 and Mark 1, but he adds several distinctive features: a detailed historical setting, John's practical instructions to different groups, the mention of Jesus praying at His baptism, and a genealogy that traces Jesus' family back to Adam. (Matthew 3:2, 3:8, and 3:17 are studied in depth in Matthew's commentary.)

### The Historical Setting (3:1–2)

Luke begins with a remarkable list of rulers, the kind of dating a careful historian would use. It was [[v1|in the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar]], the Roman emperor who succeeded Augustus. Depending on how the years were counted, this was around A.D. 27–29. Pontius Pilate was governor of Judea (A.D. 26–36). Herod Antipas was tetrarch of Galilee; his brother Philip ruled the regions northeast of Galilee; and Lysanias ruled Abilene, northwest of Damascus. The high priests were Annas and Caiaphas. Annas had been high priest from A.D. 6 to 15 and was removed by the Romans, but he remained very influential; his son-in-law Caiaphas held the office during Jesus' ministry (John 18:13).

This list of powerful men, emperors, governors, tetrarchs, and high priests, sets the stage for something surprising: [[v2|the word of God came to John, the son of Zacharias, in the wilderness]]. God's word did not come to the palace in Rome or the temple in Jerusalem, but to a man in the desert. This phrase echoes the way the Old Testament introduces prophets: "the word of the LORD came to" Jeremiah, Hosea, and others. After centuries without a prophet, God was speaking again.

### John's Preaching (3:3–9)

John went throughout the region around the Jordan River, [[v3|preaching the baptism of repentance for remission of sins]]. Baptism in water was a sign of turning away from sin and turning to God. Luke quotes Isaiah 40:3–5 at greater length than Matthew or Mark. Isaiah described a voice crying in the wilderness to prepare the way for the Lord: valleys would be filled, mountains leveled, crooked roads made straight, and rough ways smooth. In the ancient world, roads were prepared this way for the arrival of a king. Luke continues the quotation to include its climax: [[v6|All flesh will see God’s salvation]]. This verse is studied below.

John spoke bluntly to the crowds coming for baptism: [[v7|You offspring of vipers, who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?]] Like snakes fleeing a fire, many were coming to escape judgment without real change. He told them to produce fruit that showed their repentance and not to rely on being descendants of Abraham. God could raise up children for Abraham from stones. The ax was already at the root of the trees, ready to cut down any that did not bear good fruit.

### What Should We Do? (3:10–14)

Luke alone records the practical questions people asked John and his specific answers. The crowds asked, [[v10|What then must we do?]] John answered that anyone with two tunics should share with someone who had none, and anyone with food should do the same. Tax collectors asked what they should do. John told them, [[v13|Collect no more than that which is appointed to you]]. Soldiers asked the same question, and John told them, [[v14|Extort from no one by violence, neither accuse anyone wrongfully. Be content with your wages]]. This section is studied below.

### John Points to the Messiah (3:15–20)

The people were filled with expectation and wondered whether John might be the Messiah. John answered that he baptized with water, but someone more powerful was coming, whose sandal straps John was not worthy to untie. That one [[v16|will baptize you in the Holy Spirit and fire]]. The Holy Spirit would be poured out on God's people, as the prophets had promised (Joel 2:28). Fire could signify both purification and judgment. John described the coming one with a winnowing fork in His hand, separating wheat from chaff, gathering the wheat into His barn and burning the chaff with unquenchable fire.

With many other words, John [[v18|preached good news to the people]]. Even his warnings of judgment were part of good news, because they called people to turn to God before it was too late.

Then Luke briefly tells how John's ministry ended. John rebuked Herod Antipas for marrying Herodias, his brother's wife, and for all the other evil things he had done. Herod added to his sins by locking John up in prison. Luke mentions this here, before Jesus' baptism, to close John's story and turn the focus fully to Jesus. (Mark 6:14–29 tells the full story of John's death.)

### The Baptism of Jesus (3:21–22)

When all the people were being baptized, Jesus was baptized too. Luke adds a detail found only in his Gospel: Jesus [[v21|was praying]]. Luke emphasizes Jesus' prayer life throughout his Gospel, showing Him praying at key moments (5:16; 6:12; 9:18, 28–29; 22:41–44). As Jesus prayed, heaven was opened, and the Holy Spirit descended on Him [[v22|in a bodily form like a dove]]. Luke's phrase "in bodily form" emphasizes that this was a visible event. A voice came from heaven: [[v22|You are my beloved Son. In you I am well pleased]].

The Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are all present in this scene. The Spirit's coming on Jesus anointed Him for His mission, as He will announce in the synagogue at Nazareth: [[Luke 4:18|The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to preach good news to the poor]].

### The Genealogy of Jesus (3:23–38)

Luke notes that Jesus was [[v23|about thirty years old]] when He began His ministry. Thirty was the age when priests began their service (Numbers 4:3) and when David became king (2 Samuel 5:4). Luke describes Jesus as [[v23|the son (as was supposed) of Joseph]], acknowledging that Joseph was His legal father, though not His biological father, since Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit (Luke 1:35).

Luke's genealogy differs from Matthew's in several ways. Matthew begins with Abraham and moves forward to Jesus, emphasizing that Jesus is the son of Abraham and the son of David, the fulfillment of Israel's hope (Matthew 1:1–17). Luke begins with Jesus and moves backward, past David and Abraham, all the way to Adam, [[v38|the son of God]]. Between David and Joseph, the two lists name different ancestors: Matthew traces the line through Solomon, Luke through David's son Nathan. Scholars have offered several explanations. One common view is that Luke records Mary's genealogy (with Joseph named as the son-in-law of Heli), while Matthew records Joseph's. Another is that Matthew gives the royal legal line and Luke the biological line of Joseph, with differences explained by customs such as levirate marriage, in which a man could be the legal son of one man and the biological son of another. Ancient genealogies served different purposes, and both Gospels agree that Jesus is a descendant of David.

Luke's genealogy is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 3:6 — All Flesh Will See God's Salvation

verse: Luke 3:6

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes the passage from Isaiah that Luke quotes to describe John the Baptist's ministry. Matthew and Mark quote only the first part of Isaiah 40:3, about the voice crying in the wilderness to prepare the way of the Lord. Luke continues the quotation through Isaiah 40:5, ending with this line: [[v6|All flesh will see God’s salvation]].

In Isaiah's original context, around the time of the Babylonian exile, these words announced that God would come to rescue His people and bring them home. Isaiah 40 begins, "Comfort, comfort my people." The image of a highway in the wilderness, with valleys raised and mountains lowered, pictured God returning to His people in power. The prophecy ended with the promise that all people would see God's glory and salvation.

"All flesh" means all humanity, every person, not just Israel. Luke's decision to include this phrase is deliberate. One of his major themes is that the good news of Jesus is for everyone: Jews and Gentiles, rich and poor, men and women, righteous and sinners. Simeon had already declared that Jesus would be [[Luke 2:32|a light for revelation to the nations]]. Now Luke shows that John's ministry was preparing the way for a salvation that would reach the entire world.

"God's salvation" refers to God's rescue of His people from sin, death, and everything that separates them from Him. In Luke's Gospel, this salvation is embodied in Jesus Himself. When Simeon held the infant Jesus, he said, [[Luke 2:30|for my eyes have seen your salvation]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the universal reach of the gospel. God's plan was never limited to one nation. From His promise to Abraham that all the families of the earth would be blessed through him (Genesis 12:3), God intended to bring salvation to all peoples. Luke's two volumes, the Gospel and Acts, trace how this salvation moved from a small group of Jewish followers in Jerusalem to Gentiles throughout the Roman world (Acts 1:8; 28:28).

The verse also shows the purpose of repentance. John called people to prepare the way by repenting, leveling the mountains of pride and filling the valleys of despair, straightening crooked paths of sin. Repentance clears the way for people to see and receive God's salvation.

Finally, the verse reflects the hope that one day God's salvation will be visible to all. Christians look forward to the return of Christ, when every knee will bow and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord (Philippians 2:10–11).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to share the good news with all people. If God's salvation is for all flesh, believers should not limit their love and witness to people who are like them. They can reach out across cultural, social, economic, and ethnic barriers, recognizing that every person needs and can receive God's salvation.

It also invites believers to examine whether anything in their lives is blocking the way. Pride, bitterness, dishonesty, or other sins can act like mountains and crooked roads, hindering their relationship with God and their witness to others. Repentance clears the way.

Finally, this verse offers hope. No person is beyond the reach of God's salvation. Believers can pray for and share Christ with those who seem far from God, trusting that God desires all people to see His salvation.

### Luke 3:10–11 — "What Then Must We Do?"

verse: Luke 3:10-11

#### Simple Explanation

These verses begin a section found only in Luke's Gospel. John the Baptist had been preaching powerfully, calling people to repent and warning them that they must produce fruit showing their repentance was genuine. He told them not to rely on being descendants of Abraham, and he warned that every tree not producing good fruit would be cut down and thrown into the fire.

The crowds responded with a sincere question: [[v10|What then must we do?]] They wanted to know what repentance looked like in practice. John's answer was simple and concrete: [[v11|He who has two coats, let him give to him who has none. He who has food, let him do likewise]].

The "coat" was a tunic, the basic garment worn next to the skin. Many poor people had only one. Having two tunics meant having more than one needed. John told those with extra to share with those without. The same applied to food. Repentance meant generosity toward the needy.

Then specific groups asked the same question. Tax collectors asked what they should do. They worked for the ruling authorities and were known for overcharging and keeping the extra for themselves. John did not tell them to quit their jobs; he told them, [[v13|Collect no more than that which is appointed to you]]. Repentance for them meant honesty and fairness in their work.

Soldiers also asked. These may have been Jewish soldiers serving Herod Antipas or soldiers attached to Roman officials. Soldiers were poorly paid and sometimes used their power to extort money from civilians by threats or false accusations. John told them, [[v14|Extort from no one by violence, neither accuse anyone wrongfully. Be content with your wages]]. Repentance meant refusing to abuse power.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that repentance is not merely a religious feeling or ritual. It touches the most practical areas of life: possessions, money, work, and the use of power. True repentance changes how people treat others. This connects with the prophets, who repeatedly taught that God desires justice and mercy more than religious rituals (Isaiah 1:16–17; Amos 5:24; Micah 6:8).

It is significant that John did not tell tax collectors or soldiers to abandon their professions. He told them to do their work with integrity. Faith is meant to be lived out in ordinary jobs and everyday responsibilities. Believers can honor God in whatever legitimate work they do by being honest, fair, and generous.

The common thread in John's answers is love for neighbor. Sharing clothing and food, collecting only what is fair, and refusing to exploit others are all ways of treating others as one would want to be treated. These are the fruits that show that a heart has truly turned to God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to ask John's question for themselves: "What then must we do?" Repentance is not complete until it reaches daily life. Believers can consider how their faith should change the way they use money and possessions, how they do their jobs, and how they use whatever power or influence they have.

Practically, this might mean sharing extra clothing, food, or resources with those in need; being honest in business, taxes, and financial dealings; treating employees, customers, and coworkers fairly; refusing to use positions of authority to intimidate or take advantage of others; and practicing contentment rather than constant striving for more.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to serve God where they are. They do not need to leave their jobs to follow God faithfully. Whether they are teachers, business owners, factory workers, nurses, soldiers, or parents, they can bring integrity, generosity, and compassion into their daily work.

### Luke 3:38 — The Son of Adam, the Son of God

verse: Luke 3:38

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes the genealogy of Jesus in Luke's Gospel. Luke's list begins in verse 23 with Jesus, [[v23|being the son (as was supposed) of Joseph]], and then traces His ancestors backward through seventy-seven generations. It passes through King David, Abraham, and Noah, finally reaching [[v38|the son of Enos, the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God]].

Adam was the first human being, created directly by God (Genesis 2:7). Luke calls Adam "the son of God," not in the same sense that Jesus is the Son of God, but because Adam owed his existence directly to God, without a human father.

The placement of this genealogy is significant. It comes immediately after Jesus' baptism, where the voice from heaven declared, "You are my beloved Son." And it comes immediately before the temptation in the wilderness, where the devil says, [[Luke 4:3|If you are the Son of God]]. Luke surrounds the genealogy with declarations about Jesus' sonship.

Matthew's genealogy begins with Abraham, emphasizing that Jesus is the Messiah of Israel. Luke goes further back, to Adam, emphasizing that Jesus belongs to all humanity. He is not only the son of Abraham and David, but the son of Adam, connected to every human being. This fits Luke's emphasis on the universal reach of the gospel.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse presents Jesus as the representative of all humanity. Paul develops this idea, describing Adam as the head of the old humanity, through whom sin and death entered the world, and Christ as the head of a new humanity, through whom life and righteousness come (Romans 5:12–21; 1 Corinthians 15:22, 45). Where Adam, the first "son of God," failed when tempted in the garden, Jesus, the true Son of God, remains faithful when tempted in the wilderness (Luke 4:1–13).

The genealogy also affirms that Jesus was fully human. He had real ancestors and belonged to a real family history. He was not a phantom or a heavenly being disguised as a man. He shared the human story from its very beginning.

Finally, by tracing Jesus back to Adam, Luke shows that the gospel addresses the human condition as a whole. Every person descends from Adam and shares in the brokenness that entered the world through sin. Jesus came to restore what was lost, offering salvation to all of Adam's descendants.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse reminds believers that Jesus belongs to every person and every people. No ethnic group, nation, or social class has a special claim on Him. Believers can approach people of every background with the confidence that Jesus came for them too.

It also offers comfort to those who feel their family history disqualifies them. Jesus' family tree, traced back to Adam, includes every kind of person. God works through human history, with all its failures, to accomplish His purposes.

Finally, this verse invites believers to see Jesus as the one who succeeds where humanity has failed. Every person, like Adam, has sinned. Jesus, the faithful Son, offers a new beginning. Believers can find their identity not in their connection to Adam's failure, but in their union with Christ, the true Son of God.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 3 shows that God's word often comes in unexpected places. The rulers of the world held the power and the titles, but God spoke to a prophet in the wilderness. Believers can remain open to hearing God's voice in humble and unexpected ways, and they can trust that God's purposes move forward regardless of who holds earthly power.

John's preaching calls for genuine repentance. Like the crowds, believers can ask, "What then must we do?" The answer touches everyday life: sharing with those in need, being honest in work and money, refusing to abuse power, and being content. Repentance is not only a feeling of sorrow but a change in how people live and treat others.

John's humility is also an example. Though people wondered if he was the Messiah, he pointed beyond himself to Jesus. Believers can serve faithfully while directing attention to Christ rather than themselves. John's courage in confronting Herod reminds them that faithfulness sometimes means speaking truth to those in power, even at great cost.

Jesus' baptism, marked by prayer and the Father's declaration of love, shows the importance of prayer and the security of knowing one's identity in God. Finally, Luke's genealogy, reaching back to Adam, reminds believers that Jesus came for all humanity. The salvation John announced is for "all flesh," and believers are called to share that good news with everyone.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord God, thank You for speaking Your word in the wilderness and for sending John to prepare the way for Jesus. Search my heart and show me the mountains of pride, the valleys of despair, and the crooked paths of sin that need to be made straight. Help me to repent in ways that change how I live.

Show me what I must do. Give me a generous heart to share with those in need, integrity in my work and in my dealings with money, and humility in how I use any power or influence I have. Teach me to be content. Thank You that Jesus, Your beloved Son, came for all people, and that His salvation reaches every son and daughter of Adam. Help me to point others to Him, as John did, and to share the good news that all flesh may see Your salvation. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
