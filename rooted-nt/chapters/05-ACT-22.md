# Acts 22
theme: Paul's Testimony: From Zealous Persecutor to Witness for the Nations
main: Standing on the barracks stairs, Paul speaks to the hostile crowd in their own language. He describes his zeal for the Law and his persecution of the Way, his encounter with the risen Jesus on the Damascus road, and Ananias's call to be baptized and wash away his sins. When he tells how Jesus sent him to the Gentiles, the crowd erupts in fury. The Roman commander orders him flogged for questioning, but Paul asks whether it is lawful to scourge an uncondemned Roman citizen, and the commander, alarmed, brings him before the Jewish council instead.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 22 contains the second of three accounts of Paul's conversion in Acts (see also 9:1–19 and 26:9–18). Each telling is shaped for its audience. Here Paul speaks to a crowd of Jews in Jerusalem who had just tried to kill him because they believed he had betrayed the Law and defiled the temple. Paul's speech emphasizes his Jewish credentials, his zeal for God, and the role of Ananias, a respected, law-observant Jew. He wants his listeners to understand that his faith in Jesus did not come from rejecting the God of Israel but from an encounter with Him.

The conversion itself and Ananias's welcome are studied at Acts 9:4–5, 9:15–16, and 9:17. The studies below focus on what this telling adds.

### "I Am Indeed a Jew" (22:1–5)

Paul began, [[v1|Brothers and fathers, listen to the defense which I now make to you]]. These were the same words Stephen had used to begin his speech (7:2). When the crowd heard Paul speaking in their own language, [[v2|they were even more quiet]]. His background and his past as a persecutor are studied below.

### The Damascus Road (22:6–11)

Paul described the light from heaven at about noon, the voice saying, [[v7|Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?]], and his question, [[v8|Who are you, Lord?]] The answer: [[v8|I am Jesus of Nazareth, whom you persecute]]. Only here does Paul include the words "of Nazareth," connecting the heavenly Lord with the man from Galilee whom Jerusalem had rejected.

Paul added a question not recorded in Acts 9: [[v10|What shall I do, Lord?]] His first response to Jesus was a readiness to obey. The Lord told him to go into Damascus, where he would be told what to do. Blinded by [[v11|the glory of that light]], Paul was led by the hand into the city.

### Ananias and Paul's Commission (22:12–16)

Paul described Ananias as [[v12|a devout man according to the law, well reported of by all the Jews who lived in Damascus]]. To this audience, Ananias's reputation as a law-observant Jew mattered. Ananias said, [[v13|Brother Saul, receive your sight!]] and Paul could see.

Ananias then gave Paul his commission: [[v14|The God of our fathers has appointed you to know his will, and to see the Righteous One, and to hear a voice from his mouth]]. Notice that Ananias speaks of "the God of our fathers," the God of Israel. The title "the Righteous One" was a messianic title (Acts 3:14; 7:52). [[v15|For you will be a witness for him to all men of what you have seen and heard]]. Then came a call to respond, studied below.

### The Vision in the Temple (22:17–21)

Paul then described a vision he had in the temple after returning to Jerusalem, studied below. This account was especially significant because he was speaking in the temple area and had been accused of defiling it. Paul showed that he had prayed there and received a vision from God there.

### The Crowd's Fury and Paul's Citizenship (22:22–30)

[[v22|They listened to him until he said that; then they lifted up their voice and said, “Rid the earth of this fellow, for he isn’t fit to live!”]] The crowd had listened quietly until Paul mentioned being sent to the Gentiles. The idea that God would send a Jewish messenger to the Gentiles, offering them equal standing in God's people, was intolerable to them. They shouted, [[v23|threw off their cloaks, and threw dust into the air]], signs of extreme rage and horror.

The Roman commander, who may not have understood Paul's Aramaic speech, could not tell why the crowd was so angry. He ordered Paul to be brought into the barracks and [[v24|examined by scourging]]. Roman scourging was brutal, done with leather whips tipped with pieces of bone or metal; it was sometimes fatal. Paul's question is studied below.

The commander asked whether Paul was really a Roman citizen. He said, [[v28|I bought my citizenship for a great price]]. Under Emperor Claudius, citizenship could sometimes be obtained through bribes. The commander's name, Claudius Lysias (23:26), suggests he took the emperor's name when he became a citizen. Paul replied, [[v28|But I was born a Roman]]. His father or an earlier ancestor must have received citizenship, perhaps for service to Rome. Being born a citizen carried more prestige than buying it.

The soldiers who were about to interrogate Paul withdrew immediately, and the commander was afraid because he had bound a Roman citizen. The next day, wanting to know exactly why Paul was being accused, he released him and ordered the chief priests and the whole council to assemble. He brought Paul before them.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 22:3–4 — Zealous for God, Yet Persecuting the Way

verse: Acts 22:3-4

#### Simple Explanation

Paul stood on the steps of the Roman barracks in Jerusalem, bruised from being beaten by the crowd and bound with chains. Below him was the crowd that had just tried to kill him. They believed he was a traitor who taught against the Law and had defiled the temple.

Paul began by establishing his background: [[v3|I am indeed a Jew, born in Tarsus of Cilicia, but brought up in this city at the feet of Gamaliel]]. Though born outside Israel, Paul was raised and educated in Jerusalem. He studied under Gamaliel, one of the most respected rabbis of the time (5:34). To sit "at the feet" of a teacher meant to be his student.

[[v3|instructed according to the strict tradition of the law of our fathers]]. Paul received rigorous training in the Law. He later described himself as [[Philippians 3:5|a Hebrew of Hebrews; concerning the law, a Pharisee]].

[[v3|being zealous for God, even as you all are today]]. Paul acknowledged his listeners' zeal. He did not insult them. He identified with them: he had once been just like them, passionate for God and for the Law.

Then he described how his zeal had expressed itself: [[v4|I persecuted this Way to the death, binding and delivering into prisons both men and women]]. Paul's zeal had led him to persecute followers of Jesus, even to the point of death. He arrested both men and women. The high priest and the council could confirm this.

Paul was saying, in effect, "I understand your anger. I once felt the same way. I did what you are doing now. Let me tell you what changed me."

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that zeal for God is not enough. Paul was sincerely zealous, yet he was fighting against God. He later wrote of his fellow Jews, [[Romans 10:2|For I testify about them that they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge]]. Sincere religious passion can be misdirected when it is not guided by the truth about Jesus.

They also show the power of humble, empathetic witness. Paul did not attack his audience. He acknowledged their zeal and confessed his own past. By identifying with them, he hoped to open their hearts to his message. Paul wrote, [[1 Corinthians 9:22|I have become all things to all men, that I may by all means save some]].

Finally, these verses show the depth of God's grace. Paul had persecuted the church to death. Yet God saved him and made him an apostle. Paul later called himself the chief of sinners (1 Timothy 1:15–16), and said that God showed him mercy so that he could be an example of Christ's patience to all who would believe.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers to examine whether their zeal is guided by truth. Passion for God is good, but it must be shaped by Scripture and by the character of Jesus. Zeal that leads to hatred, violence, or harshness is not true zeal for God.

They also encourage believers to share their faith with empathy. When speaking with those who oppose the gospel, believers can acknowledge what is good in them and share honestly about their own journey. Humility and understanding can open doors that arguments cannot.

Finally, these verses give hope to those who feel their past is too dark. Paul had persecuted believers to death, yet God forgave and transformed him. No one's past is beyond God's grace.

### Acts 22:16 — Arise, Be Baptized, and Wash Away Your Sins

verse: Acts 22:16

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was telling the crowd in Jerusalem about his conversion. He described how Jesus appeared to him on the road to Damascus, how he was blinded, and how a devout man named Ananias came to him. Ananias restored Paul's sight and told him that God had appointed him to know His will, to see the Righteous One, and to be His witness to all people.

Then Ananias said, [[v16|Now why do you wait?]] There was urgency in Ananias's words. Paul had met the risen Lord and had received a commission. It was time to respond.

[[v16|Arise, be baptized, and wash away your sins, calling on the name of the Lord]]. Ananias gave Paul four instructions. First, "arise," get up and act. Second, "be baptized," publicly identify with Jesus through baptism. Third, "wash away your sins," receive forgiveness and cleansing. Fourth, "calling on the name of the Lord," turning to Jesus in faith and prayer.

The phrase "calling on the name of the Lord" echoes Joel 2:32, quoted by Peter at Pentecost: [[Acts 2:21|whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved]]. In the Old Testament, "the Lord" referred to the God of Israel. Here, Paul is told to call on the name of Jesus. This shows that the early church worshiped Jesus as Lord.

The image of washing recalls the Old Testament washings for purification. Through Christ, believers are made clean from their sins.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the close connection between baptism, forgiveness, and faith. Christians have understood this connection in different ways. Some traditions see baptism as a means through which God grants forgiveness and cleansing. Others understand baptism as the outward sign that expresses the inner reality of cleansing received through faith, with the phrase "calling on the name of the Lord" showing that it is faith in Christ that saves. Paul himself later wrote of believers being washed, sanctified, and justified [[1 Corinthians 6:11|in the name of the Lord Jesus, and in the Spirit of our God]]. All Christians agree that forgiveness comes through Christ and that baptism is an important act of obedience and identification with Him.

It also shows the urgency of responding to Christ. Ananias asked, "Why do you wait?" When people come to know Jesus, they are called to respond without delay.

Finally, the verse shows the completeness of God's cleansing. Paul had done terrible things, persecuting the church to death. Yet he could wash away his sins by calling on the Lord. God's forgiveness is complete. Isaiah wrote, [[Isaiah 1:18|Though your sins are as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites anyone who has heard the gospel to respond. If someone knows the truth about Jesus but has not yet trusted Him, Ananias's question applies: "Why do you wait?" Today is the day to call on the name of the Lord.

It also encourages believers who have not been baptized to consider Jesus' command. Baptism is a way of publicly identifying with Christ and declaring one's faith.

Finally, the verse brings comfort to those burdened by guilt. Through calling on Jesus, sins are washed away. Believers can trust that their sins, however great, have been cleansed by the blood of Christ.

### Acts 22:19–21 — "I Will Send You Far from Here to the Gentiles"

verse: Acts 22:19-21

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was telling the crowd about a vision he had received in the temple soon after his conversion. While he was praying there, he fell into a trance and saw the Lord, who told him, [[v18|Hurry and get out of Jerusalem quickly, because they will not receive testimony concerning me from you]].

Paul argued with the Lord. He thought his story would be especially convincing to the people of Jerusalem: [[v19|Lord, they themselves know that I imprisoned and beat in every synagogue those who believed in you]]. [[v20|When the blood of Stephen, your witness, was shed, I also was standing by, consenting to his death, and guarding the cloaks of those who killed him]]. Paul assumed that if the people saw such a dramatic change in a former persecutor, they would listen.

Notice that Paul called Stephen "your witness." The Greek word for "witness" (martys) is the origin of the English word "martyr." Stephen bore witness to Jesus with his life and death.

But the Lord answered, [[v21|Depart, for I will send you out far from here to the Gentiles]]. God had a different plan. Paul's mission was not mainly to Jerusalem but to the nations.

When Paul said these words, the crowd erupted. They had listened quietly to his story about the vision on the road and about Ananias. But the mention of being sent to the Gentiles was too much. To them, it suggested that Gentiles could become God's people on equal terms with Jews, without becoming Jews themselves.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that God's plans are sometimes different from our own. Paul thought his testimony would persuade Jerusalem, but God knew the people would not accept it. God sent him elsewhere. God's wisdom is greater than human reasoning. Isaiah writes, [[Isaiah 55:8|“For my thoughts are not your thoughts, and your ways are not my ways,” says the LORD]].

They also show Paul's grief over his past. Paul never forgot Stephen's death. He carried the memory of his role in it. Yet his grief did not crush him; it shaped his understanding of grace. The man who held the cloaks of Stephen's killers became the apostle to the Gentiles.

Finally, these verses show that the mission to the Gentiles came from Jesus Himself. Paul did not invent it. The Lord sent him. The crowd's rage shows how radical this mission was, and how deeply it challenged the assumptions of the time.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to trust God's direction even when it differs from their plans. Believers may think they know where they can be most useful, but God may send them elsewhere. Believers can bring their reasoning to God honestly, as Paul did, and then trust His wisdom.

They also show that God can use believers' past failures for His purposes. Paul's past as a persecutor did not disqualify him; it became part of his testimony to God's grace. Believers can trust that God can redeem their past.

Finally, these verses challenge believers to embrace God's heart for all people. The crowd could not accept that God would send His message to the Gentiles. Believers today can ask whether there are people they consider outside God's concern, and ask God to give them His love for all nations.

### Acts 22:25 — Is It Lawful to Scourge a Roman?

verse: Acts 22:25

#### Simple Explanation

After Paul's speech, the crowd erupted in rage. The Roman commander still did not understand why they were so angry. He decided to find out by having Paul flogged, a common Roman method of extracting information from prisoners. Roman flogging was extremely brutal; the whip had leather straps with pieces of metal or bone attached, and it could tear the flesh and even cause death.

The soldiers stretched Paul out and tied him with straps, ready to whip him. Then [[v25|Paul asked the centurion who stood by, “Is it lawful for you to scourge a man who is a Roman, and not found guilty?”]]

Roman law protected citizens from such treatment. It was illegal to flog a Roman citizen, and especially one who had not been tried and convicted. Violating these laws could bring severe penalties on the officials responsible.

The centurion immediately reported this to the commander: [[v26|Watch what you are about to do, for this man is a Roman!]] The commander came and confirmed it with Paul. The soldiers withdrew, and the commander was alarmed that he had even bound a Roman citizen.

Paul had used his citizenship before, in Philippi, after he had already been beaten (16:37). This time he spoke up before the flogging began. Paul did not seek to suffer needlessly. When legal protections were available, he used them.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that it is not wrong for believers to use legal rights and protections. Paul was willing to suffer for Christ, but he did not seek suffering for its own sake. He used his rights as a Roman citizen to protect himself from unjust treatment. This was not a lack of faith but wise stewardship.

It also shows that God works through ordinary means, including governments and laws. Paul's citizenship protected him and eventually enabled him to appeal to Caesar and be taken to Rome (25:11). God used Roman law to carry Paul to the center of the empire, fulfilling Jesus' promise that Paul would testify in Rome (23:11).

Finally, the verse shows that justice matters. Paul's question exposed an injustice: punishment without trial. Scripture calls for justice and fair treatment (Deuteronomy 16:20). Believers can stand for justice, both for themselves and for others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse teaches believers that they may use legal rights to protect themselves and others from injustice. Believers who face mistreatment can seek protection through legal and lawful means. Following Christ does not require passively accepting abuse.

It also encourages believers to stand for justice for others. Many people around the world are punished without fair trials or denied basic rights. Believers can advocate for justice, especially for the vulnerable and the persecuted.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to trust God's providence. God used Paul's citizenship, an ordinary fact of his birth, for His purposes. God can use the circumstances of believers' lives, including their nationality, education, and position, to advance His kingdom.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 22 shows the power of personal testimony. Paul told the hostile crowd his own story: his zeal for God, his persecution of the church, his encounter with Jesus, and his calling. Every believer has a story of what God has done. Sharing it honestly and humbly can open hearts, even among those who disagree.

The chapter also shows that sincere zeal is not enough. Paul was passionate for God, yet he was fighting against Him until he met Jesus. Believers can ask God to guide their zeal by His truth, so that their passion leads to love and not to harm.

Acts 22 calls people to respond to Jesus without delay. Ananias asked Paul, "Why do you wait? Arise, be baptized, and wash away your sins, calling on the name of the Lord." Anyone who knows the truth about Jesus can respond to Him today, and believers can rest in the complete cleansing He gives.

Finally, the chapter shows that God's mission is for all nations, and that God works through ordinary means like laws and citizenship. The crowd raged at the idea of God sending Paul to the Gentiles, but God's plan would not be stopped. Believers can embrace God's love for all people, use their rights wisely to stand against injustice, and trust that God directs their lives for His purposes.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for meeting Paul on the road to Damascus and turning a zealous persecutor into a witness for the nations. Guide my zeal by Your truth, so that my passion for You always leads to love. Give me courage to share my own story of what You have done, with humility and grace, even with those who oppose me. Thank You that through calling on Your name, my sins are washed away. Help me never to delay in responding to You. When Your plans differ from mine, help me to trust Your wisdom. Give me Your heart for people of every nation. Help me to use the rights and opportunities You have given me wisely, to stand for justice, and to serve Your purposes. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
