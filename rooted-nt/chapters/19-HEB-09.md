# Hebrews 9
theme: The Blood of Christ and the Once-for-All Sacrifice
main: The first covenant had regulations for worship and an earthly sanctuary, with the Holy Place and, behind the second curtain, the Most Holy Place containing the ark of the covenant. Priests served daily in the outer room, but only the high priest entered the inner room, once a year, with blood offered for himself and for the people's sins. The Holy Spirit showed by this that the way into God's presence had not yet been opened, and that those gifts and sacrifices could not perfect the conscience of the worshiper. But Christ came as high priest of the good things now arrived, entering the greater, perfect tabernacle not made with hands, not with the blood of goats and calves but with His own blood, once for all, obtaining eternal redemption. If animal sacrifices could cleanse outwardly, how much more will Christ's blood, offered through the eternal Spirit, cleanse our consciences from dead works so we may serve the living God. He is the mediator of a new covenant, ratified by His death, since without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness. Christ entered heaven itself to appear for us before God, offering Himself once at the culmination of the ages to remove sin, and just as people die once and then face judgment, Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many and will appear a second time to bring salvation to those who eagerly wait for Him.

## Chapter Background and Story

Hebrews 9 continues the comparison between the old covenant and the new, focusing on the place of worship and the sacrifice offered there. To understand this chapter, it helps to picture the tabernacle that Israel built in the wilderness (Exodus 25–40).

The tabernacle was a portable tent surrounded by a courtyard. Inside the tent were two rooms. The first, the Holy Place, contained a lampstand, a table with the bread of the Presence, and the altar of incense near the curtain. The second, the Most Holy Place (or Holy of Holies), contained the ark of the covenant, a gold-covered chest holding the tablets of the Law, a jar of manna, and Aaron's staff that had budded. Its lid, called the mercy seat or atonement cover, was guarded by two golden cherubim. God's presence was understood to dwell above the mercy seat.

A thick curtain separated the two rooms. Priests entered the Holy Place daily for their duties. But only the high priest could enter the Most Holy Place, and only once a year, on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16). He brought the blood of a sacrificed bull for his own sins and the blood of a goat for the sins of the people, and sprinkled it on and before the mercy seat.

The author of Hebrews sees in this arrangement a powerful lesson. The system itself showed that access to God was limited and that sin had not yet been fully dealt with. Christ's sacrifice changed everything.

### The Earthly Sanctuary (9:1–5)

[[v1|Now indeed even the first covenant had ordinances of divine service and an earthly sanctuary]]. The old covenant had a God-given system of worship. [[v2|For a tabernacle was prepared. In the first part were the lamp stand, the table, and the show bread, which is called the Holy Place]]. [[v3|After the second veil was the tabernacle which is called the Holy of Holies]], [[v4|having a golden altar of incense and the ark of the covenant overlaid on all sides with gold, in which was a golden pot holding the manna, Aaron’s rod that budded, and the tablets of the covenant]]. The golden altar of incense actually stood just in front of the curtain in the Holy Place (Exodus 30:6), but it was closely connected with the Most Holy Place, especially on the Day of Atonement, when the high priest took incense from it into the inner room (Leviticus 16:12–13). The author may be describing its function rather than its exact location; some translations render the word as "censer." [[v5|and above it cherubim of glory overshadowing the mercy seat, of which things we can’t speak now in detail]]. The author does not want to explain every detail; his focus is on the main point.

### Limited Access (9:6–10)

[[v6|Now these things having been thus prepared, the priests go in continually into the first tabernacle, accomplishing the services]], [[v7|but into the second the high priest alone, once in the year, not without blood, which he offers for himself and for the errors of the people]]. Access to God's presence was severely restricted: one man, one day a year, and always with blood.

[[v8|The Holy Spirit is indicating this, that the way into the Holy Place wasn’t yet revealed while the first tabernacle was still standing]]. The very structure of the tabernacle taught a lesson. As long as the curtain stood and the old system continued, the way into God's presence was not yet open to all.

[[v9|This is a symbol of the present age, where gifts and sacrifices are offered that are incapable, concerning the conscience, of making the worshiper perfect]], [[v10|being only (with foods and drinks and various washings) fleshly ordinances, imposed until a time of reformation]]. The old sacrifices dealt with outward, ritual purity, but they could not cleanse the conscience, the inner sense of guilt before God. They were temporary, "until a time of reformation," the time when Christ would set things right.

### Christ's Better Sacrifice (9:11–14)

Christ's entrance into the true sanctuary and the power of His blood are studied below.

### The Mediator of a New Covenant (9:15–22)

[[v15|For this reason he is the mediator of a new covenant, since a death has occurred for the redemption of the transgressions that were under the first covenant, that those who have been called may receive the promise of the eternal inheritance]]. Christ's death redeems people from sins committed under the first covenant, which the animal sacrifices could not fully remove. As a result, God's people receive their eternal inheritance.

[[v16|For where a last will and testament is, there must of necessity be the death of him who made it]]. [[v17|For a will is in force where there has been death, for it is never in force while he who made it lives]]. The Greek word for "covenant" can also mean "will" or "testament." The author makes a play on words: a will takes effect only after the death of the one who made it. In the same way, the new covenant took effect through Christ's death.

[[v18|Therefore even the first covenant has not been dedicated without blood]]. [[v19|For when every commandment had been spoken by Moses to all the people according to the law, he took the blood of the calves and the goats, with water and scarlet wool and hyssop, and sprinkled both the book itself and all the people]], [[v20|saying, “This is the blood of the covenant which God has commanded you.”]] At Mount Sinai, Moses ratified the covenant with sacrificial blood (Exodus 24:3–8). Jesus echoed these words at the Last Supper: [[Mark 14:24|This is my blood of the new covenant]]. [[v21|He sprinkled the tabernacle and all the vessels of the ministry in the same way with the blood]]. The principle that underlies all of this is studied below.

### Once for All (9:23–28)

[[v23|It was necessary therefore that the copies of the things in the heavens should be cleansed with these, but the heavenly things themselves with better sacrifices than these]]. The earthly tabernacle was cleansed with animal blood. The heavenly realities required a better sacrifice. The idea of "cleansing" heavenly things is mysterious; it may mean that Christ's sacrifice opened the way into God's presence for sinners, removing every barrier.

[[v24|For Christ hasn’t entered into holy places made with hands, which are representations of the true, but into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God for us]]. Christ did not enter a copy but the reality: heaven itself. And He is there "for us," representing His people before God.

[[v25|nor yet that he should offer himself often, as the high priest enters into the holy place year by year with blood not his own]], [[v26|or else he must have suffered often since the foundation of the world. But now once at the end of the ages, he has been revealed to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself]]. The high priest had to repeat the sacrifice every year. If Christ's sacrifice worked the same way, He would have had to suffer repeatedly throughout history. But His sacrifice was once for all, at the climax of history, and it removes sin completely. The final verses are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Hebrews 9:11–12 — Eternal Redemption Through His Own Blood

verse: Hebrews 9:11-12

#### Simple Explanation

"But Christ having come as a high priest of the coming good things." Christ is the high priest of the "good things" that the old covenant only anticipated. Some manuscripts read "the good things that have come," emphasizing that these blessings are already present.

"Through the greater and more perfect tabernacle, not made with hands, that is to say, not of this creation." Christ ministers in a sanctuary greater than the earthly tabernacle, not built by human hands or belonging to this created world. This is the heavenly reality, God's own presence.

Verse 12: "nor yet through the blood of goats and calves, but through his own blood." The high priest entered with the blood of animals. Christ entered with His own blood, the blood of His sacrificial death.

"Entered in once for all into the Holy Place." Not once a year, but once for all time. His entrance into God's presence was decisive and complete.

"Having obtained eternal redemption." "Redemption" means release by payment of a price. Christ's death secured a redemption that is "eternal," permanent, never needing to be repeated.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses capture the contrast between the old and new covenants. The old high priest entered an earthly sanctuary, with animal blood, every year, achieving temporary and outward cleansing. Christ entered the heavenly sanctuary, with His own blood, once for all, achieving eternal redemption.

The phrase "his own blood" emphasizes the cost of salvation. Christ did not offer something outside Himself. He gave His own life. First Peter 1:18–19 says believers were redeemed [[1 Peter 1:18|not with corruptible things like silver or gold]] [[1 Peter 1:19|but with precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish or spot, the blood of Christ]].

"Once for all" is one of the great themes of Hebrews (7:27; 9:12, 26, 28; 10:10). Christ's sacrifice does not need to be repeated or supplemented. It is complete and sufficient forever.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can rest in the completeness of their redemption. It is "eternal," not temporary or fragile. Nothing can undo what Christ has accomplished.

This truth also frees believers from trying to add to Christ's work. They do not need to earn forgiveness through repeated efforts, rituals, or self-punishment. Christ has paid the price once for all.

Finally, the costliness of Christ's sacrifice, His own blood, invites deep gratitude and love. Believers were bought at a great price (1 Corinthians 6:20), and they can live in a way that honors the One who redeemed them.

### Hebrews 9:14 — Cleansing the Conscience

verse: Hebrews 9:14

#### Simple Explanation

Verse 13 sets up the comparison: "For if the blood of goats and bulls, and the ashes of a heifer sprinkling those who have been defiled, sanctify to the cleanness of the flesh." The old sacrifices made people ritually clean, outwardly fit to take part in worship. "The ashes of a heifer" refers to a special cleansing ritual for people who had touched a dead body (Numbers 19).

Verse 14: "how much more will the blood of Christ." If animal blood had some cleansing effect, how much greater is the power of Christ's blood.

"Who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without defect to God." Christ offered Himself willingly, empowered by the Holy Spirit. He was "without defect," sinless, the perfect sacrifice, like the unblemished animals required by the Law (Leviticus 22:21).

"Cleanse your conscience from dead works to serve the living God." Christ's blood does what animal blood never could: it cleanses the conscience, the inner person. "Dead works" may refer to sinful deeds that lead to death, or to religious rituals that cannot give life. Either way, believers are set free from them to serve the living God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse addresses one of the deepest human needs: a clean conscience. Many people carry guilt that no ritual, effort, or self-improvement can remove. The old sacrifices could cleanse outwardly, but they could not reach the heart. Christ's sacrifice cleanses the conscience itself.

Notice the involvement of the whole Trinity. Christ offered Himself, through the eternal Spirit, to God the Father. Salvation is the work of Father, Son, and Spirit together.

The goal of cleansing is service. Believers are not cleansed merely to feel better but to "serve the living God." A clean conscience frees them from paralyzing guilt and enables them to live for God with joy and freedom.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers who are burdened by guilt, even after confessing their sins, can take hold of this promise. Christ's blood cleanses the conscience. They do not need to carry guilt that He has already removed.

This verse also invites believers to examine whether they are relying on "dead works," religious activity or self-effort, to feel right with God. True cleansing comes only from Christ.

Finally, a cleansed conscience leads to service. Believers can ask how God is calling them to serve Him, freed from guilt and empowered by His Spirit.

### Hebrews 9:22 — Without the Shedding of Blood

verse: Hebrews 9:22

#### Simple Explanation

"According to the law, nearly everything is cleansed with blood." Under the Law, blood was used to purify the tabernacle, the priests, the people, and the objects of worship. The word "nearly" acknowledges some exceptions; for example, some things were cleansed with water or fire (Numbers 31:22–23), and very poor people could offer flour instead of an animal for certain sin offerings (Leviticus 5:11–13).

"And apart from shedding of blood there is no remission." "Remission" means forgiveness, the release from sin's penalty. The general principle of the Law was that forgiveness required the shedding of blood.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Why blood? In the Old Testament, blood represents life. Leviticus 17:11 says, [[Leviticus 17:11|For the life of the flesh is in the blood. I have given it to you on the altar to make atonement for your souls]]. The shedding of blood means the giving of a life. Sin brings death (Romans 6:23), and forgiveness requires that a life be given in place of the sinner.

This principle points to the cross. The animal sacrifices were temporary and could not truly take away sin (Hebrews 10:4), but they taught that forgiveness is costly. Jesus fulfilled this principle by giving His own life. His blood was shed so that sins could be forgiven. At the Last Supper, He said, [[Matthew 26:28|This is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the remission of sins]].

This truth shows the seriousness of sin and the greatness of God's love. Sin is not a small matter that can be overlooked. It requires the giving of a life. And God Himself, in Christ, gave that life.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Some people find the language of blood and sacrifice uncomfortable. But it reveals something important: forgiveness is not cheap. It cost Christ His life. Understanding this deepens appreciation for God's grace.

Believers can also take comfort in this verse. Because Christ's blood has been shed, forgiveness is available. They do not need to offer anything else. The price has been paid.

Finally, this verse invites believers to take sin seriously. If sin required the death of the Son of God, it is not something to treat lightly. Gratitude for forgiveness leads to a desire to turn from sin.

### Hebrews 9:27–28 — Once to Die, and Christ Will Appear a Second Time

verse: Hebrews 9:27-28

#### Simple Explanation

The author draws a parallel. "Inasmuch as it is appointed for men to die once, and after this, judgment." Every person dies once. There is no second chance at life, no reincarnation. After death comes judgment before God.

Verse 28: "so Christ also, having been offered once to bear the sins of many." Just as people die once, Christ died once. His single death was enough to "bear the sins of many," taking their sins upon Himself, as Isaiah 53:12 foretold: [[Isaiah 53:12|he bore the sins of many]].

"Will appear a second time, not to deal with sin, but to save those who are eagerly waiting for him." Christ will come again. His first coming dealt with sin through His sacrifice. His second coming will not be to deal with sin again, because that work is finished. He will come to complete the salvation of His people, bringing them into the fullness of eternal life.

"Those who are eagerly waiting for him." Believers are those who long for Christ's return.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses echo the Day of Atonement. On that day, the people waited outside while the high priest entered the Most Holy Place. When he came back out, it was a sign that the sacrifice had been accepted and the people were forgiven. In the same way, Christ has entered God's presence with His sacrifice, and believers wait for Him to come out, that is, to return, bringing the full blessing of salvation.

The reality of death and judgment is sobering. Every person will one day stand before God. But for those who trust in Christ, this is not a cause for terror, because Christ has already borne their sins. Romans 8:1 says, [[Romans 8:1|There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus]]. Judgment for believers is not about whether they will be condemned, but the final revelation of Christ's saving work.

The phrase "eagerly waiting" describes the posture of believers. They are not afraid of Christ's return; they long for it. It will be the day their salvation is complete.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses remind everyone that life is a single, unrepeatable opportunity. Death is certain, and judgment follows. This truth calls for seriousness about how one lives and what one believes.

For believers, these verses bring hope. Christ has borne their sins, and He will return to bring them into the fullness of salvation. They can face death and judgment with confidence, not fear.

Finally, believers can cultivate an eager expectation of Christ's return. That hope shapes daily life, giving perspective in hardship, motivation for holiness, and joy in waiting.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Hebrews 9 shows that the old covenant's system of worship, though God-given, was limited. The tabernacle itself taught that access to God was restricted: only one high priest, once a year, with animal blood. Its sacrifices could cleanse outwardly but could not cleanse the conscience. They pointed forward to something better.

Christ fulfilled all that the tabernacle and sacrifices foreshadowed. He entered not an earthly sanctuary but heaven itself, not with animal blood but with His own, not repeatedly but once for all, securing eternal redemption. His blood, offered through the eternal Spirit, cleanses believers' consciences from dead works so that they may serve the living God. Those who carry guilt can find complete cleansing in Him.

The chapter also explains why Christ's death was necessary. A covenant, like a will, takes effect through death. And without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness. Sin is serious, and forgiveness is costly. Christ gave His life so that sins could be forgiven. Believers can respond with gratitude, take sin seriously, and rest in the fact that the price has been fully paid.

Finally, the chapter points to the future. Just as people die once and then face judgment, Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many, and He will appear a second time, not to deal with sin, but to bring salvation to those who eagerly wait for Him. Believers can live with confidence and hope, cleansed by His blood and longing for His return.
