# Matthew 18
theme: Humility, Care for the Vulnerable, and Forgiveness in God's Family
main: When the disciples ask who is greatest, Jesus answers by setting a child among them and teaching that greatness comes through humility. He warns severely against causing vulnerable believers to stumble, describes the Father's search for every lost sheep, gives a careful process for addressing sin within the community, and teaches through a parable that those who have been forgiven an unpayable debt must forgive others from the heart.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 18 is the fourth of Jesus' five major teaching sections in Matthew. It is often called the Community Discourse, because it deals with how Jesus' followers should live together. After His transfiguration and His second prediction of His death (Matthew 17), Jesus turns to the relationships among His disciples. The previous chapter ended with Jesus paying the temple tax to avoid causing offense. This chapter develops that concern: how should members of God's family treat one another, especially the weak, the wandering, and those who sin against them?

### Who Is the Greatest? (18:1–5)

The disciples came to Jesus and asked, [[v1|Who then is greatest in the Kingdom of Heaven?]] Mark and Luke tell us that this question arose from an argument among them about which of them was the greatest (Mark 9:33–34; Luke 9:46). Despite Jesus' teaching about taking up the cross, they were still thinking about rank and status.

Jesus called a little child and placed the child in the middle of them. In the ancient world, children had little status. They were loved by their families, but they had no legal rights, no power, and no social standing. Jesus said that unless they turned and became like children, they would never enter the kingdom. Whoever humbles himself like this child is the greatest in the kingdom. Then He added that whoever welcomes such a child in His name welcomes Him. The disciples were asking about greatness; Jesus answered by pointing to the lowliest.

### Warnings About Causing Others to Stumble (18:6–9)

Jesus then gave a severe warning: whoever causes [[v6|one of these little ones who believe in me]] to stumble would be better off having a large millstone hung around his neck and being drowned in the sea. "Little ones" here includes children and also humble, vulnerable believers. A "huge millstone" was the large stone turned by a donkey to grind grain; it weighed hundreds of pounds. Drowning was considered a dreadful death, especially without burial. Jesus' point is that leading a vulnerable believer into sin or away from faith is extremely serious in God's eyes.

He acknowledged that in a fallen world, causes of stumbling will come, but He pronounced woe on the person through whom they come. Then He repeated, in slightly different form, the vivid language from Matthew 5:29–30: if your hand, foot, or eye causes you to sin, cut it off or tear it out. As before, this is not a command for literal self-injury but a dramatic way of saying that whatever leads us into sin must be dealt with decisively, because sin's consequences are eternal.

### The Lost Sheep (18:10–14)

Jesus warned His disciples not to despise these little ones, adding that [[v10|in heaven their angels always see the face of my Father]]. This suggests that God's attention is constantly directed toward the most vulnerable members of His family. (Verse 11, about the Son of Man coming to save the lost, is not in many early manuscripts and may have been added from Luke 19:10.)

He then told a parable. If a man has a hundred sheep and one wanders away, does he not leave the ninety-nine on the hillside to search for the one that strayed? When he finds it, he rejoices more over that one than over the ninety-nine that never wandered. In the same way, [[v14|it is not the will of your Father who is in heaven that one of these little ones should perish]]. In Luke 15, Jesus tells a similar parable about sinners who repent. Here in Matthew, the emphasis is on the community's responsibility to care for believers who wander away.

### Addressing Sin in the Community (18:15–20)

Jesus then gave instructions for what to do when a fellow believer sins. These instructions are studied in detail below. The process moves from a private conversation, to a small group of witnesses, to the whole church, with the goal of restoring the brother or sister.

Jesus gave the community authority to "bind" and "loose," the same authority given to Peter in Matthew 16:19. In Jewish teaching, these terms referred to forbidding and permitting, and to making decisions about discipline. The community's decisions, made faithfully, reflect heaven's will. Jesus added that if two of them agree on earth about anything they ask, it will be done by the Father, and [[v20|where two or three are gathered together in my name, there I am in the middle of them]]. In this context, the promise especially concerns the community gathered to seek God's will in matters of discipline and reconciliation. Jesus promises to be present, guiding and confirming their decisions. The promise also assures believers more broadly of His presence whenever they gather in His name.

### How Often Should I Forgive? (18:21–35)

Peter then asked how many times he should forgive a brother who sins against him. Was seven times enough? Some rabbis taught that a person should forgive the same offense three times, so Peter thought he was being generous. Jesus answered, [[v22|I don’t tell you until seven times, but, until seventy times seven]]. The Greek can mean seventy-seven times or seventy times seven, but the point is the same: forgiveness should not be counted. This may echo Genesis 4:24, where Lamech boasted that he would take vengeance seventy-seven times. Jesus reverses unlimited revenge into unlimited forgiveness.

He then told a parable. A king wanted to settle accounts with his servants. One servant owed ten thousand talents. A talent was the largest unit of money in that world, equal to about twenty years' wages for a laborer. Ten thousand talents was an unimaginable sum, more than the annual tax revenue of entire provinces. The servant could never repay it. The king ordered that he, his wife, his children, and everything he owned be sold to recover part of the debt, a practice known in the ancient world. The servant fell on his knees and begged for patience, promising to repay everything, an impossible promise. The king, moved with compassion, cancelled the entire debt.

That same servant then went out and found a fellow servant who owed him a hundred denarii, about a hundred days' wages. This was a real debt but tiny compared to what he had been forgiven. He grabbed his fellow servant by the throat and demanded payment. The fellow servant fell down and begged for patience, using almost the same words the first servant had used. But the first servant refused and had him thrown into prison until he could pay.

The other servants were deeply distressed and reported what happened to the king. The king called the first servant in and said, [[v32|You wicked servant! I forgave you all that debt because you begged me]], and asked whether he should not have shown mercy to his fellow servant as the king had shown mercy to him. In anger, the king handed him over to the jailers until he paid everything he owed. Jesus concluded: [[v35|So my heavenly Father will also do to you, if you don’t each forgive your brother from your hearts for his misdeeds]].

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 18:3–4 — Becoming Like a Child

verse: Matthew 18:3-4

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words in response to the disciples' question, [[v1|Who then is greatest in the Kingdom of Heaven?]] The disciples had been arguing about rank. They had just seen Peter, James, and John go up the mountain with Jesus, and perhaps that sparked rivalry. Despite everything Jesus had taught about humility and the cross, they were still thinking in terms of status and position.

Jesus answered by calling a little child and placing the child in the middle of them. Then He said, [[v3|unless you turn and become as little children, you will in no way enter into the Kingdom of Heaven]]. The word "turn" means to change direction, a word closely related to repentance. Jesus was saying that the disciples needed to turn away from their focus on greatness. More than that, the question was not who would be greatest in the kingdom but whether they would enter it at all.

What does it mean to become like a child? Jesus explains in verse 4: [[v4|Whoever therefore humbles himself as this little child is the greatest in the Kingdom of Heaven]]. The key quality is humility. In that culture, children were not admired for innocence or purity as they often are today. They were valued by their families, but socially they were among the least important members of society. They were dependent, without power, and unable to claim status. To become like a child means accepting a lowly position, depending on God rather than oneself, and giving up the quest for importance.

Jesus turned the disciples' question upside down. They wanted to know who would be at the top; He told them that greatness belongs to those willing to be at the bottom.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal that entrance into God's kingdom requires humility. No one can earn a place in the kingdom by achievement or status. People enter it as dependent children, receiving it as a gift. This connects with the first Beatitude: [[Matthew 5:3|Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven]].

The teaching also redefines greatness. The world measures greatness by power, influence, wealth, and recognition. Jesus measures it by humility and service. He Himself is the supreme example. Later He says that the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve and to give His life (Matthew 20:28). Paul writes that Jesus humbled Himself and became obedient to death on a cross, and therefore God highly exalted Him (Philippians 2:8–9).

Becoming like a child does not mean being childish, naive, or immature. Paul urges believers to be mature in their thinking (1 Corinthians 14:20). Rather, it means relating to God with humble trust and dependence, and relating to others without seeking status over them.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to examine their desire for recognition and status. In workplaces, churches, families, and social settings, it is easy to compare oneself with others, seek credit, or want to be seen as important. Jesus calls His followers to let go of such ambitions and to take the lower place willingly.

They also call believers to depend on God like children depend on their parents. This means bringing needs to Him in prayer, trusting His care, and acknowledging that everything good comes from Him. Pride says, "I can handle this"; childlike faith says, "Father, I need You."

Finally, these verses encourage believers to value those whom society overlooks. Jesus placed a child, someone of little status, at the center of His teaching. Followers of Jesus can give attention, respect, and care to children and to others who lack power or influence, recognizing that such people are honored in God's kingdom.

### Matthew 18:15 — Going to a Brother Who Has Sinned

verse: Matthew 18:15

#### Simple Explanation

This verse begins Jesus' instructions for dealing with sin within the community of His followers. It comes right after the parable of the lost sheep, in which Jesus said that the Father does not want any of His little ones to perish. The instructions that follow are a practical way of seeking a wandering brother or sister.

[[v15|If your brother sins against you]]. "Brother" refers to a fellow member of God's family, a fellow believer. Some ancient manuscripts do not include "against you," which would make the instruction about any sin a fellow believer commits, but the following conversation with Peter about forgiving a brother who sins "against me" suggests that personal offenses are especially in view.

Jesus says, [[v15|go, show him his fault between you and him alone]]. The first step is private. Rather than gossiping, complaining to others, or nursing resentment, the offended person is to go directly to the one who sinned. "Show him his fault" means to help him see what he has done wrong. Keeping it private protects the person's dignity and gives the best opportunity for repentance.

[[v15|If he listens to you, you have gained back your brother]]. The goal is not winning an argument or getting revenge but restoring a relationship. "Gained" means won back, as the shepherd recovered the lost sheep.

If the private conversation fails, Jesus describes further steps (vv. 16–17). Bring one or two others, following the principle in Deuteronomy 19:15 that matters should be established by two or three witnesses. If the person still refuses, tell the church. If he refuses to listen even to the church, treat him as [[v17|a Gentile or a tax collector]], that is, as someone outside the community. Even this final step has the goal of restoration, since Jesus Himself sought out Gentiles and tax collectors with mercy.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that sin within the community matters to God. Followers of Jesus are not to ignore sin or pretend it does not exist. Love sometimes requires honest confrontation. Paul writes, [[Galatians 6:1|Brothers, even if a man is caught in some fault, you who are spiritual must restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness]].

At the same time, the process is designed to protect people. Keeping the matter private at first prevents gossip and public shame. Involving witnesses ensures fairness and helps clarify whether there truly is a sin or a misunderstanding. Bringing it to the church happens only after other steps have failed. The process reflects both justice and mercy.

The goal throughout is restoration. Jesus' instructions come right after the parable of the lost sheep and right before His teaching on unlimited forgiveness. Discipline in God's family is never meant to be harsh or vindictive but to bring back those who have wandered.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to handle conflict directly and privately when possible. It is tempting to complain to others, post vague criticisms online, or avoid the person entirely. Jesus calls His followers to go to the person who has wronged them, with humility and a desire for reconciliation. Before doing so, believers can examine their own hearts, remembering Jesus' teaching about removing the beam from one's own eye (Matthew 7:3–5).

It also requires wisdom. This teaching applies to ordinary sins and conflicts within the church. It does not require a victim of abuse or a crime to meet privately with an abuser, or to keep serious wrongdoing secret. In such cases, safety, the involvement of appropriate leaders, and reporting to civil authorities are right and necessary. Jesus' concern for protecting "little ones" in this same chapter supports this.

Finally, the goal of "gaining your brother" should shape the tone of any confrontation. Believers can speak truthfully but gently, listen carefully, be open to correction themselves, and rejoice when relationships are restored.

### Matthew 18:21–22 — Forgiveness Without Counting

verse: Matthew 18:21-22

#### Simple Explanation

These verses record Peter's question and Jesus' answer about forgiveness. Jesus had just taught about confronting a brother who sins and about the church's role in restoring wandering believers. Peter, thinking about this, asked a practical question: [[v21|Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Until seven times?]]

Peter's suggestion seemed generous. Some Jewish teachers taught that a person should forgive someone three times for the same offense, based on passages in Amos where God judges nations after "three transgressions, and for four." Peter doubled that and added one, reaching seven, a number associated with completeness.

Jesus answered, [[v22|I don’t tell you until seven times, but, until seventy times seven]]. Whether the number means seventy-seven or four hundred ninety, the point is that forgiveness is not something to count. Anyone keeping track of how many times they have forgiven has missed the point. Jesus' words may intentionally reverse Genesis 4:24, where Lamech, a descendant of Cain, boasted that if Cain was avenged sevenfold, he would be avenged seventy-sevenfold. Lamech wanted unlimited revenge; Jesus calls His followers to unlimited forgiveness.

Jesus then told the parable of the unforgiving servant to explain why forgiveness must be so generous. The servant who had been forgiven an impossible debt refused to forgive a much smaller one. The parable ends with Jesus' warning that the Father will not forgive those who refuse to forgive others from their hearts.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses, together with the parable, show that forgiveness flows from having been forgiven. The ten thousand talents represent the enormous debt of sin each person owes to God, a debt no one could ever repay. God's forgiveness through Christ cancels that debt completely. In comparison, the wrongs others commit against us, though real and sometimes very painful, are small. Paul writes, [[Ephesians 4:32|forgiving each other, just as God also in Christ forgave you]].

The warning at the end of the parable is serious. It does not mean that people earn God's forgiveness by forgiving others. It means that a person who has truly received God's mercy will be changed by it. A persistent refusal to forgive reveals that someone has not really understood or received God's grace. This echoes Jesus' teaching on the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:12, 14–15).

It is important to understand what forgiveness is and is not. Forgiveness means releasing the debt, giving up the right to revenge, and leaving justice to God. It does not mean pretending the wrong did not happen, excusing it, or necessarily trusting the person again immediately. Reconciliation requires repentance and rebuilt trust, and in some cases, especially where there has been abuse, it may not be safe or wise to restore a relationship. Forgiveness also does not cancel the need for legal or civil consequences for serious wrongdoing. A person can forgive from the heart while still maintaining boundaries and seeking justice through proper channels.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses call believers to stop keeping score. Many people forgive once or twice but then keep a mental record of offenses, ready to bring them up later. Jesus calls His followers to release such records. This may require ongoing effort, especially for deep wounds. Forgiveness is often a process, and it may need to be renewed each time painful memories return.

Remembering how much God has forgiven is the key to forgiving others. When believers reflect on the debt of sin they have been released from, it becomes easier to release others from what they owe. Praying for those who have hurt us, and asking God for help to forgive, are practical ways of beginning.

Finally, believers can forgive while also using wisdom. Forgiveness does not require remaining in harmful situations or allowing someone to continue causing harm. Believers can forgive from the heart, refuse bitterness and revenge, and still protect themselves and others, set boundaries, and seek help when needed.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 18 gives practical teaching about life in God's family. It begins with humility. The disciples wanted to know who was the greatest; Jesus told them to become like children. Believers can apply this by letting go of the desire for status, serving others without seeking recognition, and relating to God with humble dependence. Churches can reflect this by honoring those who are often overlooked and refusing to organize their life around power and prestige.

The chapter calls for deep care for the vulnerable. Jesus warns severely against causing little ones to stumble. This includes children and new or struggling believers. Churches and believers are responsible for protecting children, for being careful about how their words and actions affect others, and for never using authority to harm. Any form of abuse within a community of faith is a grave betrayal of Jesus' teaching, and it must be stopped, reported, and addressed.

The parable of the lost sheep and the instructions for addressing sin show that God cares about those who wander. Believers are called to seek out those who are drifting, to address sin honestly but gently, and always to aim for restoration. This requires courage to speak truthfully, humility to listen, and patience to keep seeking those who have strayed.

Finally, the chapter ends with forgiveness. Believers who have been forgiven an unpayable debt by God are called to forgive others from the heart, without counting. This is one of the hardest commands Jesus gives, and it cannot be obeyed without God's grace. But it brings freedom from bitterness and reflects the mercy believers have received. At the same time, forgiveness can coexist with wise boundaries and the pursuit of justice. The heart releases revenge, while wisdom protects against further harm.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, forgive me for the times I have sought to be great rather than humble. Teach me to come to You like a child, depending on You for everything, and help me to honor those whom the world overlooks. Make me careful never to cause one of Your little ones to stumble, and give me courage to protect the vulnerable.

Thank You that You seek every lost sheep and rejoice when one is found. Give me a heart that cares for those who wander, and wisdom to speak truth gently when a brother or sister has sinned, always with the hope of restoration. Thank You for forgiving the enormous debt of my sin through Jesus. Help me to forgive others from my heart, without keeping count, and to release the desire for revenge. Give me wisdom to set right boundaries where needed, and fill me with the mercy You have shown me. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
