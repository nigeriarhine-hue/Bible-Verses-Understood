# Mark 1
theme: The Good News Begins, and the Kingdom Breaks In
main: Mark begins abruptly with the announcement that his book is the good news about Jesus Christ, the Son of God. After John the Baptist prepares the way and Jesus is baptized and tested, Jesus bursts onto the scene in Galilee, calling people to repent and believe, summoning fishermen to follow Him, driving out demons, healing the sick, and withdrawing to pray. His authority amazes everyone, yet He refuses to let popularity set His agenda.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark is the shortest of the four Gospels and probably the earliest. Early Christian tradition, recorded by writers such as Papias in the early second century, says that Mark (also called John Mark, Acts 12:12) wrote down the preaching of the apostle Peter, likely in Rome in the 60s A.D. The book is written in fast-moving, vivid Greek. One of Mark's favorite words is "immediately," which appears eleven times in chapter 1 alone. The pace gives the impression of urgency: God's kingdom is breaking into the world, and there is no time to waste.

Unlike Matthew and Luke, Mark includes no birth stories or genealogy. He begins with Jesus as an adult and moves quickly from His baptism into His ministry. Mark often explains Jewish customs and translates Aramaic words, which suggests he was writing mainly for Gentile readers, possibly Christians in Rome who were facing suspicion and, before long, persecution under the emperor Nero.

### The Beginning of the Good News (1:1–8)

Mark's first sentence serves as the title and theme of his whole book: [[v1|The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God]]. This verse is studied below.

Mark then quotes the prophets. The words come from Malachi 3:1 and Isaiah 40:3, but Mark names Isaiah, the more prominent prophet, which was a common way of citing combined texts. Both passages promise a messenger who would prepare the way for the Lord's coming. Mark identifies that messenger as John the Baptist.

John appeared in the wilderness, [[v4|preaching the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins]]. People came from all over Judea and Jerusalem to be baptized in the Jordan River, confessing their sins. John's clothing of camel's hair and leather belt recalled the prophet Elijah (2 Kings 1:8), and his diet of locusts and wild honey reflected life in the desert. John pointed beyond himself: someone mightier was coming, whose sandal strap he was not worthy to stoop down and untie. Untying sandals was a task for the lowest slave. John baptized with water, but the coming one [[v8|will baptize you in the Holy Spirit]], fulfilling the prophets' promise that God would pour out His Spirit (Joel 2:28; Ezekiel 36:26–27).

### Jesus' Baptism and Testing (1:9–13)

Jesus came from Nazareth, a small, obscure village in Galilee, and was baptized by John in the Jordan. As He came up out of the water, He saw [[v10|the heavens parting]]. Mark uses a strong word meaning "torn open," the same word he uses at the end of the Gospel when the temple curtain is torn in two (Mark 15:38). Isaiah had prayed, [[Isaiah 64:1|Oh that you would tear the heavens, that you would come down]]. Now heaven was torn open, the Spirit descended on Jesus like a dove, and a voice declared, [[v11|You are my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased]].

Immediately the Spirit [[v12|drove him out into the wilderness]], where He was tempted by Satan for forty days. Mark's account is very brief compared with Matthew and Luke, but it includes a detail they do not: Jesus [[v13|was with the wild animals]], and angels served Him. The wilderness was dangerous, but Jesus was protected there. Some interpreters see an echo of Adam, who lived peacefully with the animals before sin, and of the prophetic hope that the Messiah's reign would bring peace even among wild creatures (Isaiah 11:6–9).

### The Message of the Kingdom (1:14–15)

After John was arrested, Jesus went into Galilee preaching the good news of God. His message is summarized in verse 15: [[v15|The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News]]. This message is studied below.

### Calling the First Disciples (1:16–20)

Walking beside the Sea of Galilee, Jesus saw Simon and his brother Andrew casting their nets. He said, [[v17|Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men]], and they immediately left their nets and followed Him. A little farther on, He called James and John, the sons of Zebedee, who were mending nets in their boat. They left their father in the boat [[v20|with the hired servants]]. That detail, unique to Mark, tells us that Zebedee had a family business large enough to employ workers. These men left a measure of financial security to follow Jesus. (Matthew 4:19 is studied in depth in Matthew's commentary.)

### Authority in the Synagogue at Capernaum (1:21–28)

They went to Capernaum, a fishing town on the northwest shore of the lake, which became Jesus' home base. On the Sabbath, Jesus taught in the synagogue. The people were astonished because [[v22|he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes]]. Scribes usually taught by quoting earlier authorities. Jesus taught as one who spoke with God's own authority.

Suddenly a man with an unclean spirit cried out, [[v24|What do we have to do with you, Jesus, you Nazarene? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are: the Holy One of God!]] The demon recognized who Jesus was and knew that His coming meant defeat for the forces of evil. Jesus rebuked the spirit, saying, [[v25|Be quiet, and come out of him!]] The spirit shook the man violently and came out with a shriek. The people were amazed that Jesus' authority extended even to evil spirits, and news about Him spread throughout Galilee.

Jesus' command for the demon to be quiet is the first instance of what scholars call the "messianic secret" in Mark: Jesus repeatedly silences demons and tells people not to publicize His identity or His miracles. He did not want His identity proclaimed by demons, and He did not want people to misunderstand His mission as merely that of a miracle-worker or political liberator before they understood that He had come to suffer and die.

### Healing at Peter's House and at Sunset (1:29–34)

Leaving the synagogue, they went to the home of Simon and Andrew. Simon's mother-in-law was in bed with a fever. Jesus took her by the hand and raised her up, and the fever left her. She began to serve them. That evening, after sunset (when the Sabbath ended and people could carry the sick without breaking Sabbath rules), the whole town gathered at the door. Jesus healed many and drove out many demons, again not allowing the demons to speak because they knew who He was.

### Withdrawing to Pray (1:35–39)

Very early the next morning, while it was still dark, Jesus went to a solitary place to pray. Simon and the others searched for Him and, when they found Him, said, [[v37|Everyone is looking for you]]. They expected Jesus to return to Capernaum and build on His popularity. Instead, He said, [[v38|Let’s go elsewhere into the next towns, that I may preach there also, because I came out for this reason]]. This scene is studied below.

### Cleansing a Leper (1:40–45)

A man with leprosy came to Jesus, knelt, and begged, [[v40|If you want to, you can make me clean]]. Mark says Jesus was [[v41|moved with compassion]] (some manuscripts say He was "indignant," perhaps at the disease and the suffering it caused). He reached out and touched the man, which no one would normally do because touching a leper made a person ritually unclean, and said, [[v41|I want to. Be made clean]]. The leprosy left him immediately. (Matthew's parallel account of this healing is explored in depth at Matthew 8:3.)

Jesus sternly warned the man not to tell anyone but to show himself to the priest and offer the sacrifices required by Moses (Leviticus 14). But the man went out and talked freely, spreading the news everywhere. As a result, Jesus could no longer enter a town openly and stayed in deserted places. The chapter ends with an ironic reversal: the man who had been forced to live outside the towns because of his disease could now enter them, while Jesus, who had touched him, was now the one staying in lonely places. Yet people still came to Him from everywhere.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 1:1 — The Beginning of the Good News

verse: Mark 1:1

#### Simple Explanation

This short verse is Mark's opening line, and it works like a title for his entire book. Unlike Matthew, who begins with a genealogy, or Luke, who begins with a formal introduction, Mark plunges straight into his subject.

[[v1|The beginning]] may refer to the start of Mark's account, but it also echoes the first words of the Bible: [[Genesis 1:1|In the beginning, God created]]. Mark may be hinting that something as momentous as a new creation is starting with Jesus.

"Good News" translates the Greek word "euangelion," from which we get "evangelism" and "evangelical." In the Roman world, this word was used for important public announcements, such as the birth of an emperor or a military victory. An inscription from 9 B.C. celebrated the birthday of Caesar Augustus as "the beginning of the good news for the world." Mark's readers in the Roman Empire would have heard his opening as a bold claim: the truly good news for the world is not about Caesar but about Jesus. In the Old Testament, Isaiah used the same idea to announce God's coming to save His people (Isaiah 40:9; 52:7).

[[v1|of Jesus Christ]] names the subject. "Jesus" was His personal name, meaning "the LORD saves." "Christ" is the Greek translation of "Messiah," meaning the anointed king promised by God.

[[v1|the Son of God]]. (A few early manuscripts omit these words, but most include them.) This title frames the whole Gospel. The Father declares Jesus His beloved Son at the baptism (1:11) and the transfiguration (9:7), demons recognize Him as God's Son (3:11; 5:7), and at the climax, a Roman centurion at the cross says, [[Mark 15:39|Truly this man was the Son of God!]] Mark's Gospel moves from this opening claim to that confession.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse tells readers that Mark's book is not merely biography but proclamation. It announces good news that calls for a response. The message centers on a person: Jesus, the Messiah and Son of God. The gospel is not first a set of ideas or rules but the announcement of who Jesus is and what God has done through Him.

The word "beginning" also suggests that the whole of Mark's story, from John's preaching to the empty tomb, is only the beginning. The good news continues to spread through Jesus' followers into the world. Mark's Gospel famously ends abruptly, with the women fleeing from the empty tomb (Mark 16:8), as if inviting readers to continue the story themselves.

Finally, by using language associated with Caesar, Mark makes a quiet but powerful claim: Jesus, not any earthly ruler, is the true King whose coming brings good news to the world. This claim would cost many early Christians their lives, yet they believed it was true.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites readers to receive the story of Jesus as good news, not just as religious information. The life, death, and resurrection of Jesus are news that changes everything: forgiveness is offered, God's kingdom has come near, and death has been defeated. Believers can let this good news shape their outlook, especially in times of discouragement.

It also challenges believers to consider what they treat as the "good news" in their lives. Many people look to political leaders, economic success, or personal achievements as the source of hope. Mark insists that the deepest good news is found in Jesus, the Son of God.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to see themselves as part of the continuing story. The gospel's "beginning" in Jesus continues as His followers share it with others. Believers can tell the story of Jesus in their own words and live in a way that shows it is truly good news.

### Mark 1:15 — Repent and Believe the Good News

verse: Mark 1:15

#### Simple Explanation

This verse summarizes Jesus' message as He began His public ministry in Galilee. Mark says that after John the Baptist was arrested, Jesus came into Galilee, [[v14|preaching the Good News of God’s Kingdom]]. The timing is significant: John's ministry of preparation had ended, and Jesus' ministry of fulfillment was beginning.

Jesus' message has two announcements and two commands. The first announcement is [[v15|The time is fulfilled]]. The word for "time" here means a decisive moment or appointed season, not just chronological time. God's long-awaited moment had arrived. All the centuries of promise and waiting were reaching their goal.

The second announcement is [[v15|God’s Kingdom is at hand]]. God's kingdom means His reign, His saving and ruling power coming to set the world right. "At hand" means it has come near; in Jesus, it is arriving. The miracles in the rest of the chapter, driving out demons, healing the sick, cleansing a leper, show what the kingdom's arrival looks like.

Then come the two commands: [[v15|Repent, and believe in the Good News]]. To repent means to turn around, to change one's mind and direction, turning away from sin and self-rule and toward God. To believe means to trust and rely on the good news, placing one's confidence in what God is doing through Jesus. Matthew's summary emphasizes repentance (Matthew 4:17); Mark adds the explicit call to believe. Repentance and faith are two sides of one response: turning from sin and turning to God in trust.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse captures the heart of the gospel. God has acted in history; His appointed time has come; His kingdom is breaking into the world through Jesus. The proper response is not to earn God's favor but to turn from sin and trust His good news. Paul summarizes his own preaching the same way: [[Acts 20:21|repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord Jesus]].

The order is important. The announcements come first, and the commands follow. Repentance and faith are not ways of making God's kingdom come; they are responses to the kingdom that God is already bringing. Grace comes first, and human response follows.

The verse also shows that the gospel is both good news and a summons. It announces what God has done, but it also calls for a change of life. Believing the good news without repenting would be like hearing that a doctor has a cure and refusing to take it. Repenting without believing would be like trying to cure oneself. Both belong together.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls every person to respond to Jesus. For those who have never trusted Him, it is an invitation to turn from going their own way and to trust the good news about Jesus. This is not a matter of becoming religious but of turning to God and relying on what He has done.

For believers, repentance and faith continue throughout life. They can regularly turn away from sins God reveals and renew their trust in His promises. Each day offers fresh opportunities to repent of specific attitudes and actions and to rely on God's grace rather than their own efforts.

Finally, the announcement that "the time is fulfilled" encourages believers to recognize the urgency of the gospel. God's kingdom has come near in Jesus. This is not a message to put off until later. Believers can respond wholeheartedly now and share the invitation with others who need to hear it.

### Mark 1:35 — Jesus Withdraws to Pray

verse: Mark 1:35

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes after one of the busiest days of Jesus' ministry recorded in Mark. On the Sabbath in Capernaum, He had taught in the synagogue, driven out an unclean spirit, healed Peter's mother-in-law, and then, after sunset, healed many sick people and cast out many demons as the whole town gathered at the door. It would have been late at night before the crowds left.

Yet [[v35|Early in the morning, while it was still dark]], Jesus got up and went out to [[v35|a deserted place]] to pray. The Greek phrase suggests very early, perhaps between three and six in the morning. Mark uses the word "deserted" or "wilderness," the same word used for the place where Jesus was tested in verses 12–13. Jesus deliberately sought solitude away from the crowds and even from His disciples.

Mark does not tell us what Jesus prayed. But the following verses show what resulted. Simon and the others searched for Jesus and, when they found Him, said, [[v37|Everyone is looking for you]]. They expected Him to return to Capernaum and continue the successful ministry there. But Jesus answered that they should go to other towns so that He could preach there too, [[v38|because I came out for this reason]]. His time in prayer seems to have clarified His mission and kept Him from being directed by popular demand.

Mark records Jesus withdrawing to pray at three key moments: here at the beginning of His ministry, after feeding the five thousand (6:46), and in Gethsemane before His death (14:32–42). Each time, He was facing a temptation to turn from His mission: popularity, political expectations, and suffering.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus, though He was the Son of God, lived in constant dependence on His Father through prayer. If Jesus needed time alone with the Father, His followers need it even more. Prayer is not an optional extra for especially spiritual people; it is the source of strength and direction for the life of faith.

The verse also shows the connection between prayer and faithfulness to one's calling. Success in Capernaum could have pulled Jesus into a different agenda. Through prayer, He remained focused on what the Father had sent Him to do. Prayer helps believers discern between what is merely urgent or popular and what God is truly calling them to.

Finally, Jesus' example reveals a rhythm of engagement and withdrawal. He gave Himself fully to people's needs, and He withdrew to be alone with God. Both were necessary. Service without prayer leads to exhaustion and drift; prayer without service becomes self-focused. Jesus held both together.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to make regular time for prayer, especially in busy seasons. Many people find that early morning, before the demands of the day begin, is a good time to pray, as Jesus did. Others may find different times. What matters is setting aside time to be alone with God, away from distractions.

It also encourages believers to seek solitude. Modern life is full of noise, screens, and constant demands. Finding a quiet place, turning off devices, and being still before God can be difficult but deeply restorative.

Finally, Jesus' example shows the value of prayer for discernment. When opportunities, pressures, or expectations pull believers in many directions, prayer helps them recognize what God is calling them to do. Believers can bring their decisions to God in prayer, asking Him to keep them faithful to His purposes rather than driven by others' expectations or their own desire for success.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 1 moves quickly, and its pace conveys urgency. The good news of Jesus is not a leisurely idea to be considered someday; it is an announcement that God's time has come and His kingdom is breaking in. Readers are called to respond by repenting and believing. For those who have not yet trusted Jesus, this chapter is an invitation to turn to Him. For believers, it is a reminder to keep turning from sin and trusting the good news every day.

The chapter shows the authority of Jesus over every area of life: He teaches with authority, commands evil spirits, heals diseases, and touches the untouchable. Believers can bring every part of their lives under His authority, trusting that no problem, fear, or burden is beyond His power. His compassion for the leper shows that His authority is joined with tender love for those who are suffering and excluded.

The calling of the fishermen shows that following Jesus involves real decisions and real costs. James and John left a family business with hired workers. Following Jesus may require believers to reorder priorities, leave behind security, or step into unfamiliar paths. Jesus' promise, "I will make you," assures them that He will shape them for His purposes.

Finally, Jesus' early morning prayer offers a model for sustainable discipleship. He was busy, popular, and in demand, yet He made time to be alone with the Father, and that time kept Him focused on His mission. Believers can build regular rhythms of prayer into their lives, especially in busy seasons, so that their activity flows from communion with God rather than from pressure or the desire for approval.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for the good news that in You God's appointed time has come and His kingdom has drawn near. I turn from my own way and trust in You. Help me to keep repenting and believing every day, not only once, and to live as one who has heard the best news in the world.

Thank You for Your authority over evil, sickness, and everything that harms Your people, and for Your compassion that reaches out to touch the untouchable. Make me willing to follow You when You call, even when it means leaving security behind. Teach me to rise and seek You in prayer as You sought Your Father, so that I may stay faithful to Your purposes and not be driven by pressure or praise. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
