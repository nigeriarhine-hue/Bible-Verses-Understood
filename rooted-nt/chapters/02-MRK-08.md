# Mark 8
theme: Seeing Clearly Who Jesus Is and What It Costs
main: Jesus feeds four thousand people, refuses to give a sign to skeptical Pharisees, and gently rebukes His disciples for failing to understand what His miracles reveal. After He heals a blind man in two stages, Peter confesses that Jesus is the Christ, yet Peter still sees only partially, rejecting Jesus' teaching that the Messiah must suffer. Jesus then calls everyone who would follow Him to deny themselves, take up their cross, and value their soul above the whole world.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 8 is the turning point of Mark's Gospel. The first half of the book has been building toward the question the disciples asked in the storm: [[Mark 4:41|Who then is this?]] In this chapter Peter finally answers: "You are the Christ." But immediately Jesus reveals something the disciples did not expect: the Christ must suffer, die, and rise again. From this point, the story moves steadily toward Jerusalem and the cross. The second half of Mark focuses on what it means to follow a suffering Messiah.

The chapter is carefully arranged around the theme of seeing and understanding. The disciples are slow to see who Jesus is. A blind man is healed gradually, first seeing partially, then clearly. Peter sees that Jesus is the Messiah but does not yet see that the Messiah must suffer. Mark invites readers to move from partial sight to clear sight.

### Feeding the Four Thousand (8:1–10)

A large crowd had been with Jesus for three days and had nothing left to eat. This was likely still in the Gentile region of the Decapolis, where Jesus had healed the deaf man (Mark 7:31). Jesus said, [[v2|I have compassion on the multitude]], because if He sent them away hungry, some might collapse on the way home, since some had come a long distance.

The disciples asked, [[v4|From where could one satisfy these people with bread here in a deserted place?]] Their question is surprising, since they had already seen Jesus feed five thousand people with five loaves (Mark 6:35–44). They had seven loaves this time. Jesus had the crowd sit down, gave thanks, broke the loaves, and gave them to the disciples to distribute. They also had a few small fish, which He blessed and distributed. Everyone ate and was satisfied, and the disciples collected seven baskets of leftovers. About four thousand people were fed.

This second feeding mirrors the first, but its setting among Gentiles is significant. Jesus' compassion and provision extended beyond Israel to all people. The number seven, a symbol of completeness, may also suggest the fullness of God's provision for the nations.

### The Pharisees Demand a Sign (8:11–13)

Jesus crossed the lake to the region of Dalmanutha, probably on the western shore near Magdala. The Pharisees came and began to argue with Him, demanding [[v11|a sign from heaven]] to test Him. They had heard of His miracles, but they wanted something more spectacular, perhaps a dramatic sign in the sky, to prove that His authority came from God.

Jesus [[v12|sighed deeply in his spirit]], expressing grief and frustration at their unbelief. He said that no sign would be given to this generation. He refused to perform miracles on demand for those who had already decided not to believe. Then He left them and crossed back to the other side of the lake.

### The Yeast of the Pharisees and Herod (8:14–21)

In the boat, the disciples realized they had forgotten to bring bread; they had only one loaf. Jesus warned them, [[v15|Take heed: beware of the yeast of the Pharisees and the yeast of Herod]]. Yeast is a small amount of fermenting dough that spreads through an entire batch. Jesus was warning about the corrupting influence of the Pharisees' unbelief and hypocrisy and Herod's worldly, power-seeking attitude. Both the Pharisees, who demanded signs, and Herod, who wanted to see Jesus perform (Luke 23:8), shared an unwillingness to believe.

The disciples misunderstood and thought He was talking about their lack of bread. Jesus responded with a series of sharp questions, studied below. He reminded them of the two feedings: how many baskets were left over from the five thousand? Twelve. From the four thousand? Seven. Then He asked, [[v21|Don’t you understand yet?]] The one loaf in the boat was enough, because the One who had fed thousands was in the boat with them. Some interpreters see Jesus Himself as the "one loaf" with them.

### The Two-Stage Healing of a Blind Man (8:22–26)

At Bethsaida, a fishing village on the northeast shore of the lake, people brought a blind man and begged Jesus to touch him. Jesus took him by the hand and led him outside the village. He spat on the man's eyes, laid His hands on him, and asked, "Do you see anything?" The man looked up and said, [[v24|I see men, but I see them like walking trees]]. He could see shapes but not clearly. Jesus laid His hands on his eyes again, and the man's sight was fully restored; he saw everything clearly. Jesus sent him home, telling him not to go back into the village.

This is the only healing in the Gospels that happens in two stages. It is studied below. Its placement, right after the disciples' failure to understand and right before Peter's confession, suggests that it is an acted parable about the disciples' own partial sight.

### Peter's Confession (8:27–30)

Jesus and His disciples traveled north to the villages around Caesarea Philippi, a city near the foot of Mount Hermon, filled with temples to pagan gods and to Caesar. On the way, Jesus asked, [[v27|Who do men say that I am?]] They reported various opinions: John the Baptist, Elijah, or one of the prophets. Then Jesus asked, [[v29|But who do you say that I am?]] Peter answered, [[v29|You are the Christ]].

Mark records Peter's confession more briefly than Matthew, who adds "the Son of the living God" and Jesus' blessing of Peter (Matthew 16:16–19, studied in that commentary). Mark's focus is on what follows. Jesus warned them not to tell anyone about Him, because popular ideas of the Messiah as a political liberator would lead to misunderstanding.

### Jesus Predicts His Death (8:31–33)

Then Jesus began to teach them that [[v31|the Son of Man must suffer many things, and be rejected by the elders, the chief priests, and the scribes, and be killed, and after three days rise again]]. This is the first of three times in Mark that Jesus predicts His death and resurrection (see 9:31 and 10:33–34). Mark adds, [[v32|He spoke to them openly]]. After so many hidden sayings and parables, Jesus now spoke plainly.

Peter took Jesus aside and began to rebuke Him. He could not accept a Messiah who would suffer and die. Jesus turned, looked at His disciples, and rebuked Peter: [[v33|Get behind me, Satan! For you have in mind not the things of God, but the things of men]]. Peter, without realizing it, was repeating the temptation Satan had offered: glory without the cross. Mark notes that Jesus looked at the disciples as He said this, perhaps because they shared Peter's thinking. Like the blind man after Jesus' first touch, Peter saw something true about Jesus, but his vision was still blurred.

### The Cost of Following Jesus (8:34–38)

Jesus called the crowd together with His disciples. His next words were not only for the Twelve but for anyone who wants to follow Him: [[v34|Whoever wants to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me]]. (This saying is studied in depth at Matthew 16:24.) He explained that whoever tries to save their life will lose it, but whoever loses their life for His sake and for the gospel will save it. Mark alone adds "and the sake of the Good News," linking discipleship with the mission of spreading the gospel.

Then Jesus asked two questions about the value of a human life, studied below. Finally, He warned that whoever is ashamed of Him and His words in this adulterous and sinful generation, the Son of Man will be ashamed of when He comes in His Father's glory with the holy angels. Mark's first readers in Rome, facing hostility and possibly persecution, would have felt the force of this warning. Following Jesus might cost them their reputation, safety, and even their lives, but being ashamed of Him would cost them far more.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 8:18 — Eyes That Do Not See, Ears That Do Not Hear

verse: Mark 8:18

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to His disciples in a boat on the Sea of Galilee. Shortly before, He had fed four thousand people with seven loaves, and the Pharisees had demanded a sign from heaven. Now the disciples realized they had forgotten to bring bread and had only one loaf with them. When Jesus warned them about the "yeast" of the Pharisees and Herod, they thought He was scolding them for forgetting bread.

Jesus responded with a series of questions. He asked why they were talking about having no bread, and whether they still did not understand. He asked, [[v17|Is your heart still hardened?]] Then He asked the questions in this verse: [[v18|Having eyes, don’t you see? Having ears, don’t you hear? Don’t you remember?]]

These words echo Old Testament passages. Jeremiah 5:21 and Ezekiel 12:2 describe Israel as a people with eyes that do not see and ears that do not hear, because they refused to understand God's ways. In Mark 4:12, Jesus applied similar words from Isaiah to those "outside." Now, startlingly, He applies them to His own disciples. They had physical sight and hearing, but they were not perceiving the spiritual meaning of what they had witnessed.

"Don't you remember?" Jesus then asked them to recall the details of the feedings: how many baskets of leftovers there were. They answered correctly, twelve and seven. They remembered the facts, but they had not drawn the right conclusion. If Jesus could feed thousands with a few loaves, there was no reason to worry about having only one loaf. The question was not about bread but about who Jesus was.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that it is possible to be close to Jesus, to witness His work, and still fail to understand. The disciples were not hostile like the Pharisees, but they were slow and spiritually dull. Their minds were focused on immediate concerns (bread) rather than on what Jesus was revealing about Himself.

It also shows the importance of remembering. Throughout Scripture, God calls His people to remember what He has done. Israel was commanded to remember the exodus (Deuteronomy 8:2). Remembering God's past faithfulness strengthens trust in the present. The disciples' forgetfulness led to anxiety; their remembering should have led to faith.

Finally, Jesus' questions are not condemnation but invitation. He was not giving up on His disciples. He was prodding them to think, to connect the dots, and to see. The healing of the blind man that follows immediately shows that Jesus can open eyes, even if the process is gradual.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to ask whether they are seeing and hearing what God is showing them. It is possible to read Scripture, attend church, and experience God's provision while still worrying as though God were absent. Believers can pause and ask what God has already done and what it reveals about His character.

It also encourages the practice of remembering. Keeping a record of answered prayers, reflecting on how God has provided in the past, and regularly giving thanks help believers trust God in new situations. When anxieties arise about money, health, or the future, recalling God's past faithfulness can calm fears.

Finally, this verse offers hope to those who feel spiritually dull. The disciples were slow, but Jesus did not give up on them. Believers can ask Jesus to open their eyes and ears, trusting that He will continue to teach them patiently.

### Mark 8:24–25 — From Blurry Sight to Clear Vision

verse: Mark 8:24-25

#### Simple Explanation

These verses describe a unique healing that took place in Bethsaida, a fishing village on the northeastern shore of the Sea of Galilee. People brought a blind man to Jesus and begged Him to touch him. Jesus took the man by the hand and led him outside the village, giving him personal attention away from the crowd. He spat on the man's eyes, laid His hands on him, and asked whether he could see anything.

The man looked up and said, [[v24|I see men, but I see them like walking trees]]. His sight had begun to return, but it was blurry. He could tell there were people moving around, but they looked like tree trunks walking. The fact that he knew what trees and men looked like suggests he may not have been blind from birth.

Then Jesus [[v25|again he laid his hands on his eyes]]. This time, [[v25|He looked intently, and was restored, and saw everyone clearly]]. The healing was complete.

This is the only healing in the Gospels that happens in stages. Some readers wonder whether Jesus' power was limited. But Mark presents Jesus' power over blindness as complete in the end. The two-stage healing seems to be intentional, serving as a living illustration. It comes right after the disciples' failure to understand the meaning of the loaves (vv. 14–21) and right before Peter's confession, in which Peter sees that Jesus is the Christ but does not yet understand that the Christ must suffer (vv. 27–33). The disciples were like the man after the first touch: they saw something, but not clearly.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This healing illustrates the gradual nature of spiritual understanding. The disciples did not come to a full understanding of Jesus all at once. They saw Him first as a teacher and miracle worker, then as the Messiah, and only after the resurrection and the coming of the Spirit did they understand the meaning of the cross. Spiritual sight often grows in stages.

It also shows Jesus' patience and persistence. He did not leave the man with partial sight. He touched him again until he saw clearly. In the same way, Jesus continues to work in His followers until they see clearly. Paul expresses confidence that [[Philippians 1:6|he who began a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ]].

Finally, the verse reminds believers that in this life, their understanding is still partial. Paul writes that now we see [[1 Corinthians 13:12|in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face]]. Full sight will come when Christ returns. Until then, believers grow in understanding while humbly recognizing their limitations.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers who feel that their understanding of God is incomplete or blurry. Faith often grows gradually. Believers can be patient with themselves and others, trusting that Jesus continues to work, touching their lives again and again until they see more clearly.

They also invite believers to be honest with Jesus about what they see. The man told Jesus plainly that his sight was not yet clear. Believers can bring their questions, doubts, and confusion to Jesus in prayer, asking Him to help them understand.

Finally, these verses call for humility. Like Peter, believers may see some truths clearly while misunderstanding others. They can hold their understanding humbly, remain open to correction from Scripture, and continue seeking deeper insight into who Jesus is and what He asks of them.

### Mark 8:36 — What Profit to Gain the World and Lose Your Life?

verse: Mark 8:36

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to the crowd and His disciples near Caesarea Philippi, right after telling them that anyone who wants to follow Him must deny themselves, take up their cross, and follow Him. He has just said that whoever tries to save their life will lose it, and whoever loses their life for His sake and the gospel's will save it.

Now He asks a question that makes people think about the value of their lives: [[v36|For what does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his life?]] The word "profit" is a business term meaning gain or advantage. Jesus imagines someone who gains everything the world can offer: wealth, power, pleasure, fame. But in the process, they forfeit their "life," the Greek word "psyche," which can mean life, soul, or self. They lose what matters most: their true life before God.

The next verse continues: [[v37|For what will a man give in exchange for his life?]] Once a person's soul is lost, there is nothing valuable enough to buy it back. No amount of money, achievement, or worldly success can replace eternal life with God.

The setting is significant. Caesarea Philippi was a city built to honor Caesar, the man who seemed to have gained the whole world. Jesus asks whether even Caesar's success would be worth anything if a person lost their soul. Mark's first readers in Rome lived under the power of Caesar and faced pressure to deny Christ to preserve their safety and status. Jesus' question would have been very real to them.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that the human soul is more valuable than the entire world. God created human beings in His image and for eternal relationship with Him. Nothing in the material world can compare with that value. Jesus is calling His followers to see life from an eternal perspective.

It also exposes the false promises of worldly success. People often pursue wealth, achievement, and recognition as if these things will give life meaning and security. But they are temporary, and they cannot save. Jesus warns that pursuing them at the cost of one's relationship with God is the worst possible exchange.

The verse also connects with the call to take up one's cross. Following Jesus may cost a person worldly advantages. But Jesus promises that losing one's life for Him is the way to save it. What seems like loss is actually the greatest gain. Paul expresses this when he says he counts everything as loss compared to knowing Christ (Philippians 3:7–8).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine their priorities. Many people spend most of their energy pursuing career success, financial security, or social status. These things are not evil in themselves, but they can become idols. Believers can ask whether their pursuit of worldly goals is drawing them away from God, compromising their integrity, or crowding out their relationship with Christ.

It also encourages believers facing pressure to compromise their faith. Whether it is pressure to be dishonest at work, to hide their faith to fit in, or to abandon convictions for the sake of acceptance, Jesus' words remind them that nothing the world offers is worth losing their soul.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to share the gospel. If the soul is worth more than the whole world, then helping someone come to know Christ is of eternal value. Believers can care for the souls of others as well as their own, sharing the hope of life in Christ.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 8 calls believers to see Jesus clearly and to follow Him faithfully. The feeding of the four thousand shows His compassion and His power to provide, even in a deserted place. The disciples' forgetfulness reminds believers to remember what God has done, so that they do not worry as though He were absent. Taking time to recall God's past faithfulness strengthens trust in present needs.

The warnings about the yeast of the Pharisees and Herod challenge believers to guard against influences that subtly corrupt faith: demanding proof on our own terms, hypocrisy, worldliness, and the pursuit of power. These attitudes can spread quietly, like yeast, until they shape the whole of life.

The two-stage healing and Peter's confession remind believers that spiritual understanding often grows gradually. Like Peter, believers may see some truths about Jesus clearly while resisting others, especially those involving suffering and self-denial. They can ask Jesus to keep touching their eyes until they see clearly, and they can humbly accept correction when their thinking reflects human values rather than God's.

Finally, Jesus' call to deny oneself, take up the cross, and follow Him is addressed to everyone who wants to be His disciple. Following Him may cost comfort, status, or safety. But Jesus asks what good it would be to gain the whole world and lose one's soul. Believers can choose to follow Him wholeheartedly, refuse to be ashamed of Him, and trust that losing their lives for His sake is the way to find true life.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for Your compassion on the hungry and for Your power to provide. Forgive me for the times I have worried as though You were not with me. Help me to remember what You have already done, so that I trust You more fully today.

Guard my heart from the yeast of unbelief, hypocrisy, and worldliness. Open my eyes, as You opened the eyes of the blind man, and keep touching me until I see You clearly. Like Peter, I confess that You are the Christ. Teach me also to accept Your way of the cross. Give me grace to deny myself, take up my cross, and follow You. Remind me that nothing in this world is worth more than my soul, and help me never to be ashamed of You or Your words. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
