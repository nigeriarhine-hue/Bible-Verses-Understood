# Romans 7
theme: The Holy Law, the Power of Sin, and the Cry for Deliverance
main: Using the illustration of a marriage ended by death, Paul explains that believers have died to the Law through Christ so that they might belong to the risen Christ and bear fruit for God. The Law itself is holy, righteous, and good, but sin used the commandment to awaken desire and bring death. Paul then describes an intense inner struggle: wanting to do good but not doing it, delighting in God's law inwardly while finding another power at work in the body. The chapter ends with the cry, "Who will deliver me out of the body of this death?" and the answer, "I thank God through Jesus Christ, our Lord!"

## Chapter Background and Story

Romans 7 is one of the most discussed chapters in Paul's letters. It answers two questions raised by chapter 6. First, Paul had said that believers are "not under law, but under grace" (6:14). How can believers be free from the Law that God Himself gave? Second, if the Law does not free people from sin, is the Law itself evil? Paul's answer is that the Law is good, but sin is so powerful that it uses even the good Law to bring death. The problem is not the Law but human sinfulness. The solution is not trying harder to keep the Law but deliverance through Jesus Christ and life in the Spirit, which Paul describes in chapter 8.

### Released from the Law (7:1–6)

Paul begins with an illustration from marriage. The law binds a married woman to her husband as long as he lives. If he dies, she is free to marry another without being called an adulteress. Paul's point is not to give teaching about marriage and divorce, which he addresses elsewhere (1 Corinthians 7), but to illustrate a principle: death ends legal obligations.

The application is studied below. Paul then contrasts the old life and the new: [[v5|For when we were in the flesh, the sinful passions which were through the law worked in our members to bring out fruit to death]]. "In the flesh" refers to life apart from Christ, dominated by sinful human nature. In that state, the Law actually stirred up sinful desires. [[v6|But now we have been discharged from the law, having died to that in which we were held; so that we serve in newness of the spirit, and not in oldness of the letter]]. Believers now serve God not by external rules written on stone, but by the new life of the Spirit written on their hearts (2 Corinthians 3:6; Jeremiah 31:33).

### Is the Law Sin? (7:7–13)

If the Law stirred up sinful passions, is the Law itself sinful? [[v7|May it never be!]] The Law revealed sin: [[v7|I wouldn’t have known coveting unless the law had said, “You shall not covet.”]] The tenth commandment (Exodus 20:17) is unique because it addresses inner desire rather than outward action. It exposes the heart.

[[v8|But sin, finding occasion through the commandment, produced in me all kinds of coveting]]. Sin is pictured as an active power that used the commandment as a base of operations. Being told not to do something can make people want it more. [[v9|I was alive apart from the law once, but when the commandment came, sin revived and I died]]. Paul may be describing his own experience as a child before becoming responsible for the Law, or Israel's experience when the Law was given at Sinai, or Adam's experience in the garden when he received God's command. All three readings have been proposed, and each sheds light on the passage.

[[v10|The commandment which was for life, this I found to be for death]]. God gave the Law to show the way of life (Leviticus 18:5), but sin twisted it into an instrument of death. [[v11|for sin, finding occasion through the commandment, deceived me, and through it killed me]]. The word "deceived" recalls the serpent in Eden, who deceived Eve (Genesis 3:13). Paul's conclusion about the Law is studied below.

### The Inner Struggle (7:14–25)

Paul now describes a painful inner conflict, studied below. Interpreters have long debated who the "I" in this passage is. Some believe Paul is describing his experience as a mature Christian, who still struggles with sin even while delighting in God's law. They point to the present tense, Paul's delight in God's law (7:22), and the experience of many believers. Others believe Paul is describing life under the Law without the Spirit, perhaps his experience as a devout Jew before conversion, or the experience of anyone who tries to obey God by their own strength. They point to phrases like "sold under sin" (7:14) and "captivity under the law of sin" (7:23), which seem to contradict Paul's description of believers as freed from sin in chapter 6, and to the fact that the Holy Spirit is not mentioned in this section but appears constantly in chapter 8. Still others see Paul speaking for Israel as a whole. Each view has strong supporters among faithful scholars.

Whatever view one takes, the passage shows that the Law alone cannot overcome sin, and that human willpower is not enough. Many believers recognize their own struggles in Paul's words, and the passage points them to the only source of victory: Jesus Christ and the power of the Spirit.

Paul says, [[v14|For we know that the law is spiritual, but I am fleshly, sold under sin]]. The Law comes from God's Spirit, but human nature is weak and enslaved. [[v17|So now it is no more I that do it, but sin which dwells in me]]. Paul is not excusing himself but describing sin as a foreign power living within. [[v18|For I know that in me, that is, in my flesh, dwells no good thing. For desire is present with me, but I don’t find it doing that which is good]].

[[v22|For I delight in God’s law after the inward person]], [[v23|but I see a different law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity under the law of sin which is in my members]]. Two "laws" or principles are at war: the desire to obey God and the power of sin. Paul's cry for help, and the answer, are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Romans 7:4 — Joined to Another

verse: Romans 7:4

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had used an illustration from marriage. A woman is bound to her husband by law as long as he lives. If he dies, she is free to marry someone else. Death ends the legal bond.

Now Paul applied this to believers and the Law: [[v4|Therefore, my brothers, you also were made dead to the law through the body of Christ]]. Believers have died in relation to the Law. How? "Through the body of Christ," meaning through Christ's death on the cross. When Christ died, believers died with Him (6:3–6). The Law's claim on them as a way of gaining righteousness ended.

[[v4|that you would be joined to another, to him who was raised from the dead]]. The purpose of this death was not to leave believers alone and free to do as they please. It was so that they could be "joined to another," to belong to the risen Christ. Believers are like a bride joined to a new husband. The church is often pictured as the bride of Christ (Ephesians 5:25–32; Revelation 19:7).

[[v4|that we might produce fruit to God]]. The result of this new union is fruitfulness. A marriage produces children; union with Christ produces the fruit of a transformed life. Paul describes this fruit elsewhere: [[Galatians 5:22|But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faith]].

Notice the change from "you" to "we." Paul includes himself. He too, though a devout Jew, had died to the Law through Christ and now belonged to Him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that the Christian life is a relationship with Christ, not merely obedience to rules. The Law gave commands but could not give life. Union with the risen Christ gives both new life and the power to bear fruit.

It also shows that freedom from the Law is not freedom from righteousness. Believers are not set free to do whatever they want; they are set free to belong to Christ and bear fruit for God. Paul says that the righteous requirement of the Law is fulfilled in those who walk by the Spirit (Romans 8:4).

Finally, the verse shows the intimacy of believers' relationship with Christ. The image of marriage speaks of love, belonging, and faithfulness. Believers are not merely Christ's servants; they are united to Him in love.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to see their faith as a relationship with Christ. The Christian life is not primarily about keeping rules but about belonging to Jesus and living in His love.

It also frees believers from trying to earn God's approval through rule-keeping. Believers have died to the Law as a way of salvation. They can rest in their union with Christ.

Finally, the verse calls believers to fruitfulness. Union with Christ is meant to produce a changed life. Believers can ask God to produce the fruit of the Spirit in them, in love, kindness, patience, and goodness.

### Romans 7:12 — The Law Is Holy, Righteous, and Good

verse: Romans 7:12

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had explained that sin used the Law to stir up sinful desires and bring death. A reader might conclude that the Law itself was evil. Paul had already asked, [[v7|Is the law sin? May it never be!]]

Now he gave his conclusion: [[v12|Therefore the law indeed is holy, and the commandment holy, righteous, and good]]. The Law of God is "holy," because it comes from the holy God and reflects His character. Each commandment is "holy," set apart by God. It is "righteous," because it shows what is right and just. And it is "good," because it was given for people's benefit.

The problem was never the Law. The problem was sin, which took the good Law and used it to produce death. Paul compares it to a doctor's diagnosis: an X-ray reveals a disease, but the X-ray does not cause it. The Law reveals sin, but it does not cause it. Sin, like a parasite, uses the Law for its own purposes.

[[v13|Did then that which is good become death to me? May it never be! But sin, that it might be shown to be sin, was producing death in me through that which is good]]. Sin is so evil that it can take something good and use it to destroy. This shows how deeply sinful sin really is: [[v13|that through the commandment sin might become exceedingly sinful]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the high value Paul placed on God's Law. Paul is sometimes misunderstood as being against the Law. In fact, he honored it as holy, righteous, and good. The psalmist also delighted in God's law: [[Psalm 19:7|The LORD’s law is perfect, restoring the soul]].

It also shows that the Law has an important purpose, even though it cannot save. It reveals God's character, shows people their sin, and points them to their need for Christ. Paul writes elsewhere that the Law was a guardian leading people to Christ (Galatians 3:24).

Finally, the verse shows the terrible power of sin. Sin can corrupt even the best things. This explains why people can misuse good gifts, including religion, Scripture, and morality, in harmful ways. The answer is not to reject good things but to be freed from sin's power.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to honor God's Law. While believers are not saved by keeping the Law, the moral commands of Scripture reveal God's will and remain a guide for living. Believers can study and treasure God's commands.

It also helps believers understand why rules alone cannot change hearts. Many people try to become better by following rules, but find that rules alone do not give power to obey. Believers need the transforming power of the Spirit.

Finally, the verse warns believers against being deceived by sin. Sin can make good things seem harmful and harmful things seem good. Believers can ask God for wisdom to recognize sin's deceptions.

### Romans 7:18–19 — The Good I Desire, I Don't Do

verse: Romans 7:18-19

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was describing an intense inner struggle. He had said that the Law is spiritual, but he was "fleshly, sold under sin." He did not understand his own actions. He wanted to do good, but he did not do it. He hated evil, but he did it.

[[v18|For I know that in me, that is, in my flesh, dwells no good thing]]. "Flesh" here refers to human nature as affected by sin. Paul is careful to say "in my flesh." He does not say there is nothing good in him at all, since he desires to do good. But his sinful nature contains nothing good.

[[v18|For desire is present with me, but I don’t find it doing that which is good]]. The problem is not a lack of desire. Paul wants to do good. The problem is a lack of power. Desire alone is not enough.

[[v19|For the good which I desire, I don’t do; but the evil which I don’t desire, that I practice]]. This is the heart of the struggle. Paul wants to do good but fails. He does not want to do evil but does it anyway.

Many people throughout history have recognized themselves in these words. Even the Roman poet Ovid wrote, "I see the better things and approve them, but I follow the worse." Human beings often know what is right and want to do it, yet find themselves unable to follow through.

As explained in the chapter background, Christians differ on whether Paul is describing the experience of a believer or of someone trying to live under the Law without the Spirit. Either way, the passage reveals that human willpower and the Law alone cannot defeat sin.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the depth of human sinfulness. Sin is not merely a matter of bad choices; it is a power that dwells within, working against good intentions. Jesus said, [[Matthew 26:41|The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak]].

They also show that knowledge and desire are not enough. Knowing what is right and wanting to do it do not guarantee success. People need a power beyond themselves. This sets the stage for Romans 8, where Paul describes the power of the Holy Spirit.

Finally, these verses show honesty about the struggle with sin. Paul does not pretend that the Christian life, or the moral life, is easy. Believers who struggle with sin are not alone; even the great apostle described this battle vividly.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses bring comfort to believers who struggle with sin. Many believers feel discouraged because they keep failing in the same areas. Paul's words show that this struggle is real and common. Believers do not need to despair or pretend.

They also show that trying harder is not the full answer. Willpower alone cannot overcome sin. Believers need to depend on Christ and the Holy Spirit, as Paul will explain in chapter 8.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to be honest with God and with one another about their struggles. Confession brings sin into the light, where it loses some of its power (James 5:16; 1 John 1:9). Believers can support one another in the fight against sin.

### Romans 7:24–25 — Who Will Deliver Me?

verse: Romans 7:24-25

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had described an intense inner conflict: wanting to do good but failing, delighting in God's law but finding another law at work within him, waging war and taking him captive to sin. Now he reaches a breaking point.

[[v24|What a wretched man I am!]] The word "wretched" means miserable, distressed, worn out by struggle. Paul feels the full weight of his inability to overcome sin by his own strength.

[[v24|Who will deliver me out of the body of this death?]] Paul does not ask "What will deliver me?" but "Who?" He knows he needs a person, a rescuer. "The body of this death" may refer to his physical body as the place where sin works, or to his whole existence under sin's power, which leads to death. Some have compared it to an ancient punishment in which a murderer was chained to the corpse of his victim. Whatever the exact image, Paul feels trapped and needs rescue.

[[v25|I thank God through Jesus Christ, our Lord!]] Suddenly, the cry of despair turns into a shout of thanksgiving. Paul knows who will deliver him: God, through Jesus Christ. Deliverance comes not from within but from outside, through Christ.

[[v25|So then with the mind, I myself serve God’s law, but with the flesh, sin’s law]]. Paul summarizes the struggle he has described. Some see this as describing the ongoing tension of the Christian life until final redemption; others see it as a summary of the situation apart from the Spirit, which Romans 8 resolves. Either way, the answer is in Christ.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the answer to sin is a Person, not a method. Paul does not find deliverance in better techniques, stronger willpower, or more rules. He finds it in Jesus Christ. Jesus said, [[John 8:36|If therefore the Son makes you free, you will be free indeed]].

They also show that recognizing one's helplessness is the doorway to deliverance. Paul's cry, "What a wretched man I am!" is not a sign of failure but a step toward grace. Only those who know they need rescue will look for a Rescuer. Jesus said that He came not for the healthy but for the sick (Mark 2:17).

Finally, these verses show that deliverance includes both present and future. Christ delivers believers now from sin's condemnation and gives them the Spirit's power to resist sin. And He will deliver them completely in the future, when they receive resurrection bodies free from sin and death (Romans 8:23).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers who feel overwhelmed by sin. When believers feel like crying out, "What a wretched person I am!" they can turn to Jesus. He is the answer to their struggle.

They also teach believers to move from despair to thanksgiving. Paul did not stay in his misery. He turned to Christ and gave thanks. Believers can do the same, remembering that they are not left alone in their struggle.

Finally, these verses point believers to Romans 8, which describes the power of the Spirit and the freedom of those who are in Christ. Believers who feel stuck in the struggle of Romans 7 can move forward into the hope and victory of Romans 8.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Romans 7 teaches that believers have died to the Law through Christ and now belong to the risen Lord. Their relationship with God is not based on rule-keeping but on union with Christ, which produces fruit for God. Believers can rest in this relationship rather than trying to earn God's approval through their own efforts.

The chapter also affirms that God's Law is holy, righteous, and good. The Law reveals God's character and shows people their sin. The problem is not the Law but sin, which uses even good things to bring death. Believers can honor God's commands while recognizing that the commands alone cannot give the power to obey.

Romans 7 describes the struggle with sin honestly. Many believers recognize themselves in Paul's words: wanting to do good but failing, delighting in God's law but feeling the pull of sin. This struggle is real. Believers do not need to hide it or despair over it. They can be honest with God and with trusted believers about their struggles.

Finally, the chapter points to the only answer: Jesus Christ. Paul's cry, "Who will deliver me?" is answered with "I thank God through Jesus Christ, our Lord!" Believers can bring their struggles to Christ, trusting Him for forgiveness and for the power of the Spirit to live a new life, which Paul will describe in the next chapter.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord God, thank You that through the death of Christ I have been set free from the Law as a way of earning Your favor, and joined to Him who was raised from the dead. Make my life fruitful for You. Thank You for Your Law, which is holy, righteous, and good, and which shows me my need for You. I confess that I often fail to do the good I want to do, and I do the things I don't want to do. I cannot overcome sin by my own strength. When I feel overwhelmed and cry out, "Who will deliver me?", remind me that You have already answered through Jesus Christ my Lord. Thank You for Your forgiveness and for the power of Your Spirit. Lead me from struggle into thanksgiving and freedom. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
