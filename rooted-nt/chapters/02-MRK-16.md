# Mark 16
theme: He Has Risen: Good News for Frightened Followers
main: At dawn on the first day of the week, three women come to anoint Jesus' body and find the stone rolled away and a messenger announcing that He has risen. They are told to tell the disciples "and Peter" that Jesus is going ahead of them to Galilee, but they flee in fear and astonishment. The longer ending, found in many manuscripts, records appearances of the risen Jesus, the disciples' slowness to believe, His commission to preach the good news to all creation, and His ascension.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 16 is the climax of Mark's Gospel. Jesus had been crucified on Friday and buried before sunset in a tomb belonging to Joseph of Arimathea (Mark 15:42–47). Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses had watched where He was laid. The Sabbath followed, from sunset Friday to sunset Saturday, a day of rest when no work could be done.

Throughout Mark's Gospel, Jesus had predicted that He would be killed and would rise on the third day (8:31; 9:31; 10:34). The disciples had not understood. Now the prediction is fulfilled.

### A Note on the Ending of Mark

Before looking at the chapter, it is important to know that the ending of Mark is one of the best-known questions in the study of the New Testament text. The two oldest complete Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, both from the fourth century, end Mark's Gospel at verse 8. Some early church writers, such as Eusebius and Jerome, also noted that the most accurate copies they knew ended there. Other manuscripts, including the majority of later ones, contain verses 9–20, known as the "longer ending." A few manuscripts contain a short alternative ending, and some contain both.

Many scholars believe that verses 9–20 were added early in the church's history to give Mark a fuller conclusion, drawing on resurrection accounts found in the other Gospels and Acts. Others defend the longer ending as original or believe Mark's original ending was lost. Most modern Bibles, including the WEB, print verses 9–20 with a note explaining the situation. The longer ending has been read in churches for many centuries and contains nothing that contradicts the rest of the New Testament, though some of its details (such as handling snakes and drinking poison) should be read with particular care. This commentary treats verses 1–8 as the core of Mark's account and explains verses 9–20 as an early summary of the resurrection appearances and the church's mission.

### The Empty Tomb (16:1–8)

When the Sabbath was over, on Saturday evening, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome bought spices to anoint Jesus' body. Anointing with aromatic oils and spices was a way of honoring the dead and reducing the odor of decay. Because Jesus had been buried hurriedly before the Sabbath, these women wanted to complete the task. Very early on Sunday morning, just after sunrise, they went to the tomb.

On the way, they asked one another, [[v3|Who will roll away the stone from the door of the tomb for us?]] Tombs cut into rock were often sealed with a large, disk-shaped stone that rolled in a channel in front of the entrance. Mark notes that it was very large. The women had come out of love, without a plan for this obstacle.

But when they looked up, they saw that the stone had already been rolled back. Entering the tomb, they saw [[v5|a young man sitting on the right side, dressed in a white robe]], and they were alarmed. Matthew identifies him as an angel (Matthew 28:2). His white robe indicates heavenly glory, as at Jesus' transfiguration (Mark 9:3).

He said, [[v6|Don’t be amazed. You seek Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified. He has risen! He is not here. See the place where they laid him!]] This is studied below.

Then he gave them a commission: [[v7|But go, tell his disciples and Peter, ‘He goes before you into Galilee. There you will see him, as he said to you.’]] Jesus had promised on the night of His arrest that after He was raised, He would go ahead of them into Galilee (Mark 14:28). This verse is studied below.

The women [[v8|went out, and fled from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment had come on them. They said nothing to anyone; for they were afraid]]. If Mark's Gospel originally ended here, it is a startling conclusion. This verse is studied below.

### The Longer Ending: Appearances, Commission, and Ascension (16:9–20)

The longer ending begins by stating that Jesus, having risen early on the first day of the week, appeared first to Mary Magdalene, [[v9|from whom he had cast out seven demons]] (see Luke 8:2). She went and told those who had been with Him, who were mourning and weeping. But when they heard that He was alive and had been seen by her, they did not believe it. (John 20:11–18 tells this story in detail.)

Afterward, He appeared in a different form to two of them as they were walking in the country. They went back and told the others, but they did not believe them either. (Luke 24:13–35 tells this story of the two disciples on the road to Emmaus.)

Later, Jesus appeared to the Eleven as they were eating. He [[v14|rebuked them for their unbelief and hardness of heart]], because they had not believed those who saw Him after He rose. This theme of the disciples' slowness to believe runs throughout Mark's Gospel and appears in the other resurrection accounts too (Luke 24:11, 25; John 20:24–29).

Then Jesus commissioned them: [[v15|Go into all the world and preach the Good News to the whole creation]]. This is studied below. He added, [[v16|He who believes and is baptized will be saved; but he who disbelieves will be condemned]]. The emphasis falls on belief; condemnation is connected with unbelief, not with lack of baptism. Christians have generally understood baptism as the normal outward expression of faith, while recognizing that salvation rests on faith in Christ (compare the thief on the cross in Luke 23:43).

Jesus said that certain signs would accompany those who believe: casting out demons in His name, speaking in new languages, picking up snakes, being unharmed if they drank anything deadly, and healing the sick by laying hands on them. The book of Acts records the apostles casting out demons, speaking in other languages at Pentecost (Acts 2), healing the sick, and Paul surviving a viper's bite (Acts 28:3–6). These verses describe signs that God gave to confirm the apostles' message. They are not commands to deliberately handle snakes or drink poison to test God, which Jesus Himself refused to do (Matthew 4:5–7). Tragically, some groups have taken these verses as instructions to handle venomous snakes in worship, resulting in deaths. That practice misreads the passage.

After speaking to them, the Lord Jesus was taken up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God, the place of supreme authority, fulfilling Psalm 110:1, which Jesus had quoted (Mark 12:36; 14:62). The disciples went out and preached everywhere, and the Lord worked with them, confirming His word by the signs that accompanied it.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 16:6 — "He Has Risen! He Is Not Here"

verse: Mark 16:6

#### Simple Explanation

These words are spoken by a young man in a white robe to three women at Jesus' tomb. Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome had come very early on Sunday morning with spices to anoint Jesus' body. They had watched Him die on Friday and had seen where He was buried. They came expecting to care for a corpse. On the way, they worried about who would roll away the heavy stone. But they found it already rolled back, and inside the tomb sat a young man in white. They were alarmed.

The messenger said, [[v6|Don’t be amazed]]. Then he identified exactly whom they were looking for: [[v6|You seek Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified]]. He described Jesus by His hometown, Nazareth, and by His death, crucifixion. There was no mistake about identity. The same Jesus who had been crucified was the one he was speaking about.

[[v6|He has risen!]] In Greek, this is a single word in the passive voice, meaning "He has been raised," emphasizing that God raised Him. Then: [[v6|He is not here]]. The body was gone. Then the invitation: [[v6|See the place where they laid him!]] The women could see the empty place where Jesus' body had been. The evidence was before their eyes.

Jesus had predicted this three times. Each time, He said that after being killed, He would rise again (Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:34). The disciples had not understood. Now His word was fulfilled.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse announces the heart of the Christian faith: Jesus, who was crucified, has risen from the dead. The resurrection shows that Jesus is who He claimed to be, the Son of God. It shows that His death was not a defeat but a victory, the ransom He came to give (Mark 10:45). It shows that God accepted His sacrifice. And it shows that death itself has been defeated.

The phrase "who has been crucified" is significant. The risen Jesus is still the crucified one. The resurrection does not erase the cross; it vindicates it. Paul preached "Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23) because the risen Lord is the one who died for sins.

The empty tomb is also important evidence. The women saw the place where Jesus had been laid, and it was empty. Christian faith rests not on a spiritual idea alone but on a historical event: Jesus' body was raised from the dead.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to live in the reality of the resurrection. Because Jesus is risen, His promises are true, His sacrifice is effective, and death is not the end. Believers can face fear, suffering, and grief with hope.

It also invites believers to come and see. The messenger invited the women to see the empty tomb. Believers can examine the evidence for the resurrection and grow in confidence that their faith rests on solid ground. They can also invite others to "come and see" who Jesus is.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that Jesus is not among the dead. He is alive and active today. Faith is not merely honoring a great teacher of the past but following a living Lord who is present with His people.

### Mark 16:7 — "Tell His Disciples and Peter"

verse: Mark 16:7

#### Simple Explanation

These words continue the message the young man gave the women at the empty tomb. After announcing that Jesus had risen, he gave them a mission: [[v7|But go, tell his disciples and Peter]]. The women would be the first messengers of the resurrection.

Notice the specific mention of Peter. Peter was one of the disciples, so why name him separately? Only a few days earlier, Peter had denied Jesus three times in the courtyard of the high priest, even calling down curses and swearing that he did not know Him (Mark 14:66–72). When the rooster crowed, Peter remembered Jesus' prediction and broke down weeping. He had promised that he would never deny Jesus, even if he had to die with Him. He had failed completely.

Peter may have wondered whether he was still a disciple at all, or whether Jesus would ever want to see him again. The message "and Peter" answered that question. Jesus specifically included him. Peter was not cut off. He was still wanted.

The message continued: [[v7|He goes before you into Galilee. There you will see him, as he said to you]]. On the night of His arrest, Jesus had predicted that all the disciples would fall away, but He had also promised, [[Mark 14:28|However, after I am raised up, I will go before you into Galilee]]. That promise was given before they failed, and it was still in effect after they failed. Jesus, the Shepherd who had been struck, would gather His scattered sheep again.

Galilee was where the disciples had first been called, where Jesus had taught and healed, and where their journey with Him had begun. Jesus would meet them there to begin again.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is one of the most tender in Mark's Gospel. It shows that Jesus' grace is greater than His followers' failures. Peter had denied Him, and all the disciples had fled. Yet the risen Jesus sent a message specifically including Peter and promising to meet them all again. Early Christian tradition connects Mark's Gospel with Peter's preaching. If that is true, Peter himself may have often told how the angel said, "and Peter," as a testimony to Jesus' grace toward him.

It also shows that Jesus keeps His promises. He had said He would go ahead of them to Galilee. Their failure did not cancel His promise. God's faithfulness does not depend on human faithfulness. Paul writes, [[2 Timothy 2:13|If we are faithless, he remains faithful]].

Finally, the verse points to restoration. John 21 tells how the risen Jesus met Peter by the Sea of Galilee and three times asked him, "Do you love me?", matching his three denials, and then commissioned him to feed His sheep. The message at the tomb was the beginning of that restoration.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse brings hope to anyone who has failed Jesus. Many believers carry guilt over times they denied Christ, compromised their faith, or acted in ways that dishonored Him. They may wonder if Jesus still wants them. The words "and Peter" show that He does. Believers can return to Him, confess their failures, and receive His forgiveness and restoration.

It also encourages believers to extend grace to others who have failed. If Jesus specifically reached out to Peter after his denial, believers can reach out to those who have stumbled, offering welcome rather than rejection, while still taking wrongdoing seriously.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that Jesus keeps His promises. Even when they have been unfaithful, He remains faithful. They can trust His promises of forgiveness, His presence, and His future return, knowing that their failures do not cancel His faithfulness.

### Mark 16:8 — Fear, Trembling, and Silence

verse: Mark 16:8

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes the women's response to the angel's message. They had come to the tomb expecting to anoint a dead body. Instead, they found the stone rolled away, the tomb empty, and a heavenly messenger who told them that Jesus had risen and that they should tell the disciples and Peter.

Mark tells us: [[v8|They went out, and fled from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment had come on them]]. The women were overwhelmed. "Trembling" describes physical shaking from fear; "astonishment" describes being beside oneself with amazement. They had encountered something completely beyond their expectations: a heavenly messenger and the news that the dead had been raised.

[[v8|They said nothing to anyone; for they were afraid]]. At first, they were too frightened to speak. Of course, the other Gospels tell us that the women did eventually tell the disciples (Matthew 28:8; Luke 24:9), and Mark's own longer ending says that Mary Magdalene went and told them (v. 10). The point of verse 8 is their immediate reaction of awe and fear.

If Mark's Gospel originally ended at this verse, as the oldest manuscripts suggest, it is an unusual and powerful conclusion. Throughout Mark, people who were told to keep silent often spread the news anyway. Now, at the end, those who were told to speak are silent out of fear. The ending leaves the story open, inviting readers to consider their own response. Will they, too, be overwhelmed into silence, or will they go and tell?

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that the resurrection is an overwhelming event. It is not a comforting idea that fits easily into ordinary life. It is a world-changing act of God that produces awe and even fear. In the Bible, encounters with God's power and holiness often cause fear (Isaiah 6:5; Mark 4:41; 9:6). The resurrection is the greatest revelation of God's power.

It also shows that even faithful followers can respond with fear and hesitation. These women had stayed with Jesus at the cross when the male disciples fled. They were among the most faithful followers in Mark's Gospel. Yet even they were overwhelmed. This honesty is part of the Gospels' credibility. The early church did not invent heroic stories about its first witnesses; it told the truth about their fear.

Finally, an ending at verse 8 would invite readers to complete the story. Mark began his Gospel with "the beginning of the Good News" (1:1). The rest of the story continues as believers go and tell. The message entrusted to the women is now entrusted to every reader.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse acknowledges that fear can keep believers from sharing their faith. Many believers know the good news of Jesus' resurrection but are afraid to speak about it because of what others might think, fear of not knowing what to say, or uncertainty about how it will be received. The women's fear shows that this struggle is common.

It also invites believers to move beyond fear. The women's silence was not the end of the story; they did eventually tell the disciples, and the message spread throughout the world. Believers can ask God for courage to share what He has done, trusting that He works through ordinary, even frightened, messengers.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to respond to the resurrection with awe. In a world that often treats Jesus casually, the resurrection calls for wonder and worship. Believers can take time to reflect on the astonishing truth that Jesus has risen, allowing it to fill them with reverent joy.

### Mark 16:15 — Go Into All the World

verse: Mark 16:15

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is part of the longer ending of Mark, which many early manuscripts include and which has been read in churches for centuries, though it is absent from the oldest complete manuscripts. In this section, the risen Jesus appears to the eleven disciples while they are eating. He rebukes them for their unbelief and hardness of heart, because they had not believed those who saw Him after He rose. Then He gives them their mission.

[[v15|Go into all the world]]. The disciples were not to stay in Jerusalem or even in Israel. Their mission was to extend to every nation and people. This echoes Jesus' teaching that the gospel must first be preached to all nations (Mark 13:10).

[[v15|and preach the Good News]]. "Preach" means to proclaim publicly like a herald announcing important news. "Good News" (gospel) is the message about Jesus: His life, death, and resurrection, and the salvation He offers. Mark's Gospel began with [[Mark 1:1|The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God]]. Now the disciples are sent to continue proclaiming it.

[[v15|to the whole creation]]. This phrase emphasizes the universal scope of the mission. The good news is for everyone, everywhere.

The following verses describe the response to this preaching (belief and baptism lead to salvation; unbelief leads to condemnation) and signs that would accompany believers. The section ends with Jesus' ascension and the disciples going out to preach everywhere, with the Lord working with them.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse expresses the same mission found at the end of Matthew (28:19–20), Luke (24:47), John (20:21), and Acts (1:8). In each Gospel, the risen Jesus sends His followers into the world with His message. This consistent testimony shows that mission is central to the church's identity. The good news is not meant to be kept within a small group but shared with all people.

It also shows that Jesus entrusted His mission to imperfect people. Just before giving this command, He rebuked the disciples for their unbelief. Yet He still sent them. God does not wait for His followers to become perfect before using them. He sends them in their weakness and works through them.

Finally, the verses that follow show that the Lord works with His messengers: [[v20|the Lord working with them and confirming the word by the signs that followed]]. The mission is not accomplished by human effort alone but by Christ's continuing presence and power.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls every believer to participate in sharing the good news. Not everyone will travel to distant countries, but everyone can share the gospel with people around them, support those who go to other places, and pray for the spread of the good news throughout the world.

It also encourages believers who feel inadequate. Jesus sent disciples who had just been rebuked for unbelief. He uses imperfect people. Believers can share their faith not because they are perfect but because the message is true and the Lord works with them.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that the gospel is for the whole creation. No person or people group is outside its reach. Believers can pray for and care about people of every nation, culture, and background, and seek to make the good news known to them.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 16 announces the most important news in the world: Jesus, who was crucified, has risen from the dead. Everything in the Christian faith depends on this event. Believers can live with confidence that Jesus' promises are true, His sacrifice is effective, and death is defeated. In times of grief, fear, or uncertainty, they can hold onto the hope of the resurrection.

The message "and Peter" shows the depth of Jesus' grace. Peter had denied Jesus, yet he was specifically included in the invitation to meet the risen Lord. Believers who have failed can know that Jesus still wants them and offers restoration. They can also extend that same grace to others who have stumbled.

The women's fear and silence acknowledge that the resurrection is overwhelming and that sharing it can be frightening. Believers often struggle with fear when it comes to speaking about their faith. Yet the story does not end with silence. The message spread from frightened women and doubting disciples to the whole world. Believers can ask God for courage to share the good news.

Finally, Jesus' commission to go into all the world and preach the gospel to all creation is the mission of the church. Believers can participate by sharing their faith, supporting others in mission, and praying for the spread of the gospel. They do so with confidence that the Lord works with them, confirming His word. Mark began with "the beginning of the Good News," and the rest of the story continues through every believer who carries that news to others.

## Chapter Prayer

Risen Lord Jesus, thank You that the tomb is empty and that You have been raised from the dead, just as You said. Thank You that the crucified One is alive, and that Your death has won forgiveness and Your resurrection has defeated death. Fill me with awe and joy at this good news.

Thank You for the words "and Peter," which show that You do not abandon those who fail You. When I have denied You in word or action, draw me back and restore me. Help me to show the same grace to others. When fear makes me silent, give me courage to speak. Send me into the world to share the good news, and work through me as You worked through Your first disciples. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
