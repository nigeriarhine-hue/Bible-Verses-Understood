# 2 Corinthians 5
theme: Living by Faith and the Ministry of Reconciliation
main: Paul looks past his aging, suffering body to the eternal home God has prepared, and he lives with confidence because the Spirit is God's down payment on that future. Knowing that everyone will stand before the judgment seat of Christ, and compelled by Christ's love, he no longer lives for himself. Anyone who is in Christ is a new creation, and God, who reconciled the world to Himself through Christ, has made His people ambassadors who plead with the world to be reconciled to God.

## Chapter Background and Story

Chapter 5 continues the thought that closed chapter 4. Paul had just written that his outward body was wasting away while his inner self was being renewed day by day, and that the things that are seen are temporary while the things that are not seen are eternal (2 Corinthians 4:16–18). Now he explains what he means by that unseen, eternal reality. He talks about death, about the resurrection body, about the coming judgment, and then about the heart of his whole ministry: God's work of reconciling the world to Himself through Christ.

This chapter was written by a man who had recently faced death. In 2 Corinthians 1:8–9, Paul described a crisis in the province of Asia so severe that he "despaired even of life." When he writes about the tent of his body being taken down, he is not speaking in the abstract. He had felt that tent shake.

At the same time, Paul is still answering critics in Corinth who questioned his motives and his credentials. Some of what he says in verses 11–13 responds to them. But he quickly lifts the discussion above personal defense to the love of Christ and the message of reconciliation, which is why this chapter has become one of the most treasured passages in the New Testament.

### The Earthly Tent and the Heavenly House (5:1–5)

[[v1|For we know that if the earthly house of our tent is dissolved, we have a building from God, a house not made with hands, eternal, in the heavens]]. Paul was a tentmaker by trade (Acts 18:3), so the image came naturally to him. A tent is a temporary shelter. It wears out, tears, and is eventually folded up. The present human body is like that. But God has prepared a "building," a permanent dwelling that will never wear out.

Paul admits that life in the tent involves groaning. [[v2|For most certainly in this we groan, longing to be clothed with our habitation which is from heaven]]. The groaning is not complaint against God; it is the longing of a person who knows something better is coming. Paul switches images from a house to clothing. He does not want to be "unclothed," existing as a bodiless spirit, but "clothed," receiving the resurrection body, [[v4|that what is mortal may be swallowed up by life]]. This echoes 1 Corinthians 15:53–54, where Paul wrote that the perishable must put on the imperishable and death will be swallowed up in victory.

Some Greek thinkers of Paul's day regarded the body as a prison and hoped to escape it. Paul's hope is different. He does not long to be free of a body; he longs for a body that is free of death. The Christian hope is resurrection, not merely the survival of the soul.

How can Paul be sure? [[v5|Now he who made us for this very thing is God, who also gave to us the down payment of the Spirit]]. The word translated "down payment" was used in business for a first installment that guaranteed the rest would follow (see also 2 Corinthians 1:22 and Ephesians 1:14). The Holy Spirit living in believers now is God's pledge that the full inheritance, including the resurrection body, will surely come.

### Walking by Faith (5:6–10)

Because of this pledge, Paul says, [[v6|we are always confident]]. He knows that as long as he is at home in the body, he is away from the Lord in the sense of not yet seeing Him face to face. Paul's statement about faith and sight in verse 7 is studied below.

[[v8|We are courageous, I say, and are willing rather to be absent from the body and to be at home with the Lord]]. Paul expresses a preference. If he had to choose, he would rather be with Christ. He says something similar in Philippians 1:21–23, where he writes that to depart and be with Christ is "very much better." Many Christians have found great comfort in this verse when grieving believers who have died. Paul does not describe everything about the state of those who have died before the resurrection, but he is clear that to be absent from the body is to be at home with the Lord.

Yet Paul does not live with a death wish. [[v9|Therefore also we make it our aim, whether at home or absent, to be well pleasing to him]]. Whether he lives or dies, his goal is the same: to please Christ. He then gives the reason: [[v10|For we must all be revealed before the judgment seat of Christ that each one may receive the things in the body according to what he has done, whether good or bad]].

The Greek word for "judgment seat" is *bema*. Corinth had a large stone *bema* in its marketplace, the very platform where Paul had once been brought before the Roman governor Gallio (Acts 18:12–17). The Corinthians knew exactly what Paul was picturing. At Christ's judgment seat, everything will be brought into the light, and what is done in this present bodily life will matter. Paul includes himself in the word "we"; he is not threatening others while exempting himself.

This raises an important question: how does judgment according to works fit with salvation by grace through faith (Romans 3:23–24; Ephesians 2:8–9)? Most interpreters understand Paul to mean that believers' standing with God rests entirely on Christ, who bore their condemnation (Romans 8:1), so that the judgment seat is an evaluation of how they lived rather than a question of whether they will be accepted (compare 1 Corinthians 3:12–15). Others see here a reminder that genuine faith always shows itself in a changed life, so that the works revealed are the evidence of faith rather than its basis. In either reading, the Judge is the same Christ who died for His people. That truth gives weight to ordinary faithfulness, kindness no one notices and service done in obscurity, while removing the fear of rejection.

### The Love of Christ Compels Us (5:11–15)

[[v11|Knowing therefore the fear of the Lord, we persuade men]]. The "fear of the Lord" here is a deep reverence and awareness that one will give an account to Him. It moves Paul to persuade others to believe the gospel.

Paul again addresses the charge that he was commending himself. He explains that he is giving the Corinthians something to say in his defense to [[v12|those who boast in appearance and not in heart]]. His opponents valued outward impressiveness. Paul cared about the heart.

[[v13|For if we are beside ourselves, it is for God. Or if we are of sober mind, it is for you]]. Some apparently thought Paul was unbalanced, perhaps because of his intense devotion or his spiritual experiences. Paul answers that his intensity is directed toward God, and his sober reasoning is for the good of the church. Either way, he is not living for himself. The reason he gives in verses 14–15 is studied below.

### A New Way of Seeing (5:16–17)

[[v16|Therefore we know no one according to the flesh from now on. Even though we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know him so no more]]. "According to the flesh" means by merely human standards: background, status, appearance, or worldly achievement. Before his conversion, Paul had judged Jesus that way. He saw a crucified Galilean and concluded that Jesus could not be the Messiah. Now he sees Christ as the risen Lord. And if his view of Christ has changed so completely, his view of everyone else must change too. He can no longer size people up by worldly measures. The new creation in verse 17 is studied below.

### The Ministry of Reconciliation (5:18–21)

[[v18|But all things are of God, who reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ, and gave to us the ministry of reconciliation]]. Reconciliation means restoring a broken relationship. The initiative is entirely God's. Humans were the ones who had turned away, yet God is the one who acts to bring them back. Paul then explains the content of that ministry: [[v19|namely, that God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not reckoning to them their trespasses, and having committed to us the word of reconciliation]]. The call to be ambassadors and the great exchange of verse 21 are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### 2 Corinthians 5:7 — We Walk by Faith, Not by Sight

verse: 2 Corinthians 5:7

#### Simple Explanation

This short verse sits in the middle of Paul's reflections on death and eternal life. He has just said that while he lives in his present body he is "absent from the Lord," meaning he does not yet see the Lord face to face. Then he explains how believers live in the meantime: by faith and not by sight.

To "walk" in Paul's letters means to live, to conduct one's daily life. Paul is not describing a single moment of decision but a whole way of life. Every day, believers make choices, face troubles, and hold on to hope based on what God has said rather than on what they can see.

"Sight" here means what is visible and immediately evident. Paul's circumstances were often discouraging to look at. He was beaten, imprisoned, misunderstood, and physically worn down. If he had judged by sight alone, he might have concluded that God had abandoned him or that his ministry was a failure. But faith looks beyond present appearances to the promises of God and the reality of the risen Christ.

This does not mean faith is blind or irrational. Faith is trust in a God who has shown Himself trustworthy, above all in raising Jesus from the dead. Paul's faith rested on real events and on God's proven character. Walking by faith means letting that trust, rather than the shifting appearance of circumstances, steer one's life.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse connects directly with 2 Corinthians 4:18, where Paul says believers look not at the things that are seen but at the things that are not seen. The unseen realities include Christ seated in glory, the Spirit at work within, and the coming resurrection. These are not less real than visible things; they are more lasting.

Hebrews 11 offers a long portrait of people who walked by faith: Abraham setting out without knowing where he was going, Moses who [[Hebrews 11:27|endured, as seeing him who is invisible]]. In each case, faith meant acting on God's word before its fulfillment was visible. The life of faith is a pilgrimage lived between promise and fulfillment.

There is also a note of honest longing here. Walking by faith is not the final state. One day faith will give way to sight, when believers see Christ face to face (1 Corinthians 13:12). Until then, faith is how the people of God travel.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people walk through seasons where nothing they can see seems hopeful: a diagnosis, a job loss, a strained marriage, a long wait for answered prayer. This verse does not ask believers to pretend those things are not real. It invites them to place God's promises alongside those circumstances and to let His faithfulness have the final word.

Walking by faith also shows up in small, daily choices: being honest when dishonesty seems profitable, forgiving when resentment feels justified, giving generously when money feels tight. Each of these is a step taken on the strength of God's word rather than on what appears safest. Over a lifetime, those steps become a path.

### 2 Corinthians 5:14–15 — The Love of Christ Compels Us

verse: 2 Corinthians 5:14-15

#### Simple Explanation

Paul has been explaining why he lives and ministers the way he does. His critics questioned his motives. Here he names the true force behind his life: the love of Christ.

"The love of Christ" most likely means Christ's love for Paul and for the world, shown supremely on the cross, though it surely also produces Paul's love for Christ in return. The word translated "compels" carries the idea of pressing in on all sides, holding firmly, or controlling. Christ's love was not a distant idea for Paul. It gripped him and directed his choices.

Paul then explains the reasoning: [[v14|one died for all, therefore all died]]. Christ's death was on behalf of all. When He died, those who belong to Him are counted as having died with Him. Their old life, the life centered on self, came to an end at the cross.

Verse 15 states the purpose: Christ died for all [[v15|that those who live should no longer live to themselves, but to him who for their sakes died and rose again]]. The new life believers receive is not meant to be spent on themselves. It belongs to the one who gave His life for them.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal the motivation at the heart of Christian living. It is not guilt, fear, or the desire for approval. It is love answering love. Because Christ gave Himself completely, His people find themselves drawn to give themselves back to Him.

The phrase "therefore all died" connects with Paul's teaching in Romans 6:3–11 and Galatians 2:20 that believers are united with Christ in His death and resurrection. The self-centered life has been crucified with Christ, and a new life, lived for Him, has begun. This is not merely a change of behavior; it is a change of identity.

Scholars have discussed whether "all" in these verses means every person without exception or all who are in Christ. Paul certainly teaches that Christ's death is offered to the whole world, as verse 19 shows. At the same time, the new life described in verse 15 belongs to "those who live," those who have received that life through faith. Either way, the love of Christ is wide enough to embrace anyone who comes to Him.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people serve in churches, families, and communities out of obligation until they are exhausted and resentful. These verses point to a deeper source. When service flows from gratitude for Christ's love rather than from pressure, it becomes sustainable and joyful.

"No longer live to themselves" also challenges the self-focused patterns that come naturally to everyone. It reshapes how believers think about careers, money, time, and relationships. The question shifts from "What do I want?" to "How can my life honor the one who died and rose for me?" That shift does not erase personal desires, but it places them under a greater love.

### 2 Corinthians 5:17 — A New Creation

verse: 2 Corinthians 5:17

#### Simple Explanation

This is one of the most beloved verses in Paul's letters. It follows directly from his statement in verse 16 that he no longer views anyone by merely human standards. The reason is that in Christ, something entirely new has begun.

"In Christ" is one of Paul's favorite phrases. It describes a person who is united with Christ by faith. That person "is a new creation." The Greek is even more compact; it could be read "if anyone is in Christ, new creation!" Paul is describing not only a change within the individual but the arrival of God's new world, which the individual has entered.

"The old things have passed away." The old life of sin, guilt, and separation from God, along with the old ways of measuring worth, no longer define the person in Christ. "Behold, all things have become new." Paul uses "Behold" to call attention to something remarkable. God has done something wonderful that deserves to be noticed.

This new creation is a present reality, but it is also still unfolding. Believers still struggle with sin and still live in bodies that grow old, as chapter 4 made clear. But their deepest identity has changed, and God is steadily bringing every part of their lives into line with that new identity.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The language of "new creation" echoes the prophets. Through Isaiah, God promised, [[Isaiah 65:17|behold, I create new heavens and a new earth]], and He said, [[Isaiah 43:19|Behold, I will do a new thing]]. Paul sees the resurrection of Christ as the beginning of that promised new creation. Every person who is joined to Christ becomes part of it, a first sign of what God will one day do for the whole universe (Revelation 21:5).

This means the Christian life is not mainly self-improvement. It is God's creative work. Just as God spoke light into darkness at the beginning (2 Corinthians 4:6), He brings new life where there was spiritual death. The change originates with God and is sustained by God.

It also explains why Paul can no longer judge people "according to the flesh." If God can make anyone new, no one is beyond hope, and no one's worth is determined by their past, status, ethnicity, or reputation.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people carry the weight of their past: failures, addictions, regrets, or wrongs done to them. This verse declares that in Christ, the past is not the final word on who they are. That does not erase consequences or the need to make amends where possible, but it frees people from being defined by what they once were.

The verse also changes how believers see others. The coworker who seems difficult, the family member far from God, the stranger with a troubled history: each of them could become a new creation. That hope can soften judgment and inspire patience, prayer, and kindness.

### 2 Corinthians 5:20–21 — Ambassadors of Reconciliation

verse: 2 Corinthians 5:20-21

#### Simple Explanation

Paul has explained that God reconciled the world to Himself through Christ and has given His people the message of reconciliation. Now he describes the role this gives them: [[v20|We are therefore ambassadors on behalf of Christ]].

An ambassador represents a ruler in a foreign land. The ambassador does not invent the message but faithfully delivers what the ruler has said. Paul sees himself, and by extension all who carry the gospel, as representatives of Christ in the world. Through them, God Himself is making an appeal: "be reconciled to God."

Notice the tone. Paul does not say "we command you" but "we beg you." The God of the universe, through His messengers, pleads with people to come back to Him. That is a remarkable picture of God's humility and love.

Verse 21 explains how reconciliation is possible: [[v21|For him who knew no sin he made to be sin on our behalf, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God]]. Jesus never sinned. Yet God placed the burden and consequence of human sin on Him at the cross. In return, those who are united with Christ receive God's righteousness, a right standing with God that they could never earn.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Verse 21 is often called "the great exchange." Christ takes what belongs to sinners, and sinners receive what belongs to Christ. Some interpreters understand "made to be sin" to mean that Christ was treated as a sinner, bearing sin's penalty. Others note that in the Greek Old Testament the word for "sin" can also mean "sin offering" (as in Leviticus 4), so Paul may be saying Christ was made a sin offering. Both readings point to the same truth: Christ stood in the place of sinners, as Isaiah 53:5–6 foretold.

"The righteousness of God" is a phrase Paul uses throughout Romans (Romans 1:17; 3:21–22). Here it describes the right standing believers have with God because they are "in him." Some traditions emphasize that this righteousness is credited to believers as a gift; others emphasize that it also includes their transformation into people who reflect God's justice and faithfulness. Paul likely holds both together: believers are declared right with God and are being made new.

The order of these verses matters. The appeal of verse 20 rests on the accomplished work of verse 21. God is not asking people to reconcile themselves by effort; He is asking them to receive a reconciliation He has already made possible.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Every believer represents Christ wherever they are: at work, at home, online, in their neighborhood. An ambassador's conduct reflects on the one they represent. This calls for words and actions that make the message of reconciliation believable, marked by grace, truth, and humility.

The ministry of reconciliation also has a horizontal side. People who have been reconciled to God are called to pursue peace with others as far as it depends on them (Romans 12:18). This may mean apologizing, forgiving, or reaching across divisions of race, class, or politics. Reconciliation never requires a person to remain in danger or to excuse abuse; safety and genuine repentance matter. But wherever reconciliation is possible, Christ's ambassadors can work toward it.

For anyone weighed down by guilt, verse 21 offers a clear word: the sin has been carried, and righteousness is offered as a gift. The invitation, "be reconciled to God," is still open.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Second Corinthians 5 teaches believers to hold the present life with both seriousness and hope. The body is a tent. It ages, aches, and will one day be taken down. Paul does not deny this. But God has prepared a permanent home, and the Holy Spirit is His guarantee that the promise will be kept. This frees believers to face aging, illness, and even death without despair. When grieving the loss of a believer, Christians can take comfort that to be away from the body is to be at home with the Lord.

Between now and that day, believers walk by faith. They live by God's promises when circumstances look bleak, and they remember that their lives will be revealed before Christ. That awareness does not need to produce fear for those who are in Christ. Instead, it gives significance to everyday faithfulness, the unseen acts of love and integrity that Christ sees and will honor.

The driving force of this life is the love of Christ. Because He died for all, His people no longer live for themselves. This love reshapes ambitions and priorities, turning attention outward toward God and neighbor. It also changes how believers see people. No one should be judged by mere outward standards, because anyone can become a new creation in Christ. That truth gives hope for oneself and patience with others.

Finally, the chapter gives every believer a calling. God has reconciled the world to Himself through Christ, and He now makes His appeal through ordinary people. Christians are ambassadors, called to carry a message of reconciliation with the same humility Paul showed when he wrote, "we beg you." They share it in words, and they show it by pursuing peace in their own relationships, wisely and safely, while resting in the great exchange: Christ took their sin, and they receive His righteousness.

## Chapter Prayer

Heavenly Father, thank You that this body, which grows tired and weak, is not the end of my story. Thank You for the home You have prepared and for the gift of Your Spirit, Your promise that everything You have said will come to pass. Teach me to walk by faith when I cannot see the way ahead. Help me to live each day in a way that pleases You, knowing that my life is seen by Christ. Let the love of Christ hold me and move me, so that I no longer live for myself but for Him who died and rose again for me. Thank You that in Christ I am a new creation; help me to see others with the same hope. Make me a faithful ambassador of Your reconciliation, gentle and truthful in word and deed. Thank You that Jesus, who knew no sin, carried my sin so that I might stand in Your righteousness. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
