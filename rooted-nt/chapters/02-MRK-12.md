# Mark 12
theme: Questions in the Temple and the Gift of a Poor Widow
main: In the temple courts, Jesus tells a parable exposing the leaders' rejection of God's Son and then answers a series of challenging questions about taxes, resurrection, and the greatest commandment. He commends a scribe who understands that love for God and neighbor matters more than sacrifices, warns against religious leaders who seek honor while exploiting widows, and praises a poor widow whose two small coins were worth more than all the gifts of the wealthy.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 12 takes place in the temple courts during Jesus' final week in Jerusalem. The day before, Jesus had driven out the merchants and money changers, declaring that God's house should be a house of prayer for all nations (Mark 11:15–17). The chief priests, scribes, and elders then challenged His authority, and Jesus refused to answer because they would not admit the truth about John the Baptist (11:27–33). Mark 12 continues the confrontation. Different groups come to Jesus with questions designed to trap Him, and each time He answers with wisdom that leaves them silent.

The chapter moves from conflict to a surprising moment of agreement with a scribe, then to Jesus' own question about the Messiah, a warning about hypocritical leaders, and finally the quiet scene of the widow's offering. (Matthew's parallel account in Matthew 21:33–22:46 studies several of these exchanges in depth; this chapter focuses on what Mark distinctively emphasizes.)

### The Parable of the Tenants (12:1–12)

Jesus spoke to the religious leaders in a parable. A man planted a vineyard, put a wall around it, dug a pit for the winepress, and built a watchtower. These details echo Isaiah 5:1–7, the "Song of the Vineyard," in which God planted a vineyard representing Israel and looked for good fruit but found only bad. Jesus' listeners would have recognized the allusion immediately.

The owner leased the vineyard to tenant farmers and went away. At harvest time, he sent a servant to collect his share of the fruit. The tenants beat him and sent him away empty-handed. He sent another servant, whom they struck on the head and treated shamefully, and then another, whom they killed. He sent many others; some they beat, others they killed. These servants represent the prophets God sent to Israel over the centuries, many of whom were rejected and persecuted.

Finally, the owner had one left to send, [[v6|his beloved son]]. He sent him last, thinking they would respect his son. But the tenants said, [[v7|This is the heir. Come, let’s kill him, and the inheritance will be ours]]. They seized him, killed him, and threw his body out of the vineyard. The words "beloved son" echo the Father's voice at Jesus' baptism and transfiguration (Mark 1:11; 9:7). Jesus was speaking about Himself and predicting His death.

Jesus asked what the owner would do: he would come, destroy the tenants, and give the vineyard to others. Then He quoted Psalm 118:22–23, studied below. The leaders realized He had spoken the parable against them. They wanted to arrest Him but feared the crowd, so they left Him and went away. (This parable is discussed at Matthew 21:33–46, which also notes that it was addressed to the leaders who rejected Jesus, not to the Jewish people as a whole.)

### Paying Taxes to Caesar (12:13–17)

The leaders then sent some Pharisees and Herodians to trap Jesus in His words. They flattered Him, praising His integrity and impartiality, and then asked whether it was lawful to pay taxes to Caesar. This was a carefully designed trap. If Jesus said yes, He would lose the support of people who resented Roman rule. If He said no, He could be reported to the Romans as a rebel.

Jesus, knowing their hypocrisy, asked for a denarius. When they brought it, He asked whose image and inscription were on it. "Caesar's," they said. Jesus answered, [[v17|Give to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s]]. They were amazed at Him. (This answer is studied in depth at Matthew 22:21.)

### The Question About Resurrection (12:18–27)

Next came the Sadducees, who denied the resurrection. They described a woman who married seven brothers in turn, according to the law of levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5–6), and asked whose wife she would be in the resurrection. Jesus said they were mistaken because they did not know the Scriptures or the power of God. In the resurrection, people will not marry but will be like the angels. Then He pointed to God's words to Moses at the burning bush: "I am the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob." [[v27|He is not the God of the dead, but of the living. You are therefore badly mistaken]]. Mark alone records the final phrase, a strong rebuke. (This exchange is studied at Matthew 22:32.)

### The Greatest Commandment (12:28–34)

One of the scribes had been listening and noticed that Jesus answered well. Unlike the previous questioners, this scribe seems to have asked sincerely: [[v28|Which commandment is the greatest of all?]]

Jesus answered by quoting the Shema, the confession of faith devout Jews recited daily: [[v29|Hear, Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one]] (Deuteronomy 6:4). Mark alone includes this opening line. Then Jesus continued, [[v30|You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind, and with all your strength]]. The second commandment is [[v31|You shall love your neighbor as yourself]] (Leviticus 19:18). No other commandment is greater. (These commands are studied at Matthew 22:37–39.)

Mark alone records what happened next. The scribe agreed with Jesus and added that loving God and neighbor [[v33|is more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices]]. This was a remarkable statement to make in the temple courts, where sacrifices were offered daily. It echoes prophetic teaching such as Hosea 6:6 and 1 Samuel 15:22. Jesus saw that he had answered wisely and said, [[v34|You are not far from God’s Kingdom]]. This is studied below. After this, no one dared to ask Him any more questions.

### Whose Son Is the Messiah? (12:35–37)

While teaching in the temple, Jesus asked how the scribes could say that the Messiah is the son of David, when David himself, speaking by the Holy Spirit, called the Messiah "Lord" in Psalm 110:1: [[v36|The Lord said to my Lord, “Sit at my right hand, until I make your enemies the footstool of your feet.”]] If David called the Messiah "Lord," how could he be merely David's son? Jesus was pointing to His own identity as more than a human descendant of David. He is David's Lord, the Son of God. Mark notes, [[v37|The common people heard him gladly]].

### Beware of the Scribes (12:38–40)

Jesus warned the crowds about scribes who liked to walk around in long robes, receive respectful greetings in the marketplaces, and have the best seats in synagogues and the places of honor at banquets. Long robes marked them as distinguished men of learning. They loved public recognition.

Worse, they [[v40|devour widows’ houses, and for a pretense make long prayers]]. Scribes sometimes served as legal advisors or managed estates, and some may have taken advantage of widows who trusted them, perhaps by charging excessive fees or exploiting their hospitality. Widows were among the most vulnerable people in that society, and the law of Moses repeatedly commanded care for them (Exodus 22:22–24). Meanwhile, these scribes prayed long prayers to appear devout. Jesus said they would receive greater condemnation. Religious leaders who exploit the vulnerable while performing religious acts will face God's judgment.

### The Widow's Offering (12:41–44)

Jesus then sat down opposite the temple treasury, where people placed their offerings. The temple had thirteen trumpet-shaped receptacles for gifts in the Court of the Women. Jesus watched as many rich people threw in large amounts. Then a poor widow came and put in two small copper coins, called "lepta" in Greek, the smallest coins in circulation. Mark explains for his Roman readers that they equaled a "quadrans," a small Roman coin. Two lepta were worth only a fraction of a day's wage.

Jesus called His disciples and told them that this poor widow had given more than all the others. They gave out of their abundance, but she, out of her poverty, gave everything she had to live on. This scene is studied below. Its placement right after Jesus' warning about scribes who devour widows' houses is striking: the religious system was exploiting widows like her, yet she gave all she had to God.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 12:10 — The Rejected Stone Becomes the Cornerstone

verse: Mark 12:10

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words at the end of the parable of the tenants. In the parable, a vineyard owner sent servants to collect fruit from his tenants, but they beat or killed each one. Finally, he sent his beloved son, and they killed him too and threw him out of the vineyard. Jesus asked what the owner would do and answered that he would destroy the tenants and give the vineyard to others.

Then Jesus asked the religious leaders, [[v10|Haven’t you even read this Scripture]], a pointed question for experts in the law. He quoted Psalm 118:22–23: [[v10|The stone which the builders rejected was made the head of the corner]]. The next verse continues, [[v11|This was from the Lord. It is marvelous in our eyes]].

Psalm 118 was one of the psalms pilgrims sang at Passover, and the crowds had just quoted from it when Jesus entered Jerusalem: [[Mark 11:9|Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord]] (Psalm 118:26). In the psalm, the image of a stone rejected by builders but chosen for the most important place describes someone despised by people but vindicated by God.

In building, the "head of the corner" was the cornerstone, the main stone that held two walls together and determined the alignment of the whole building, or possibly the capstone at the top of an arch. Builders who rejected a stone judged it unfit. Jesus applied this image to Himself. The "builders," Israel's religious leaders, were rejecting Him. But God would make Him the cornerstone of His new work. The parable ends with the son killed; the psalm adds the hope of vindication. Jesus was pointing beyond His death to His resurrection.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse became one of the most important Old Testament passages for the early church. Peter quoted it before the same religious council that condemned Jesus: [[Acts 4:11|He is ‘the stone which was regarded as worthless by you, the builders, which has become the head of the corner.’]] Peter also used it in his first letter, describing Jesus as a living stone, rejected by people but chosen by God and precious (1 Peter 2:4–8). Paul describes Christ as the cornerstone of God's household (Ephesians 2:20).

The verse reveals God's pattern of reversing human judgments. What people reject, God chooses. What appears to be defeat becomes the foundation of victory. The cross, which looked like the end of Jesus, became the cornerstone of salvation. This is "marvelous in our eyes" because it is so unexpected.

The verse also carries a warning. Those who reject the cornerstone set themselves against God's work. Jesus applied the parable to leaders who were rejecting God's Son. But the same warning applies to anyone who rejects Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to build their lives on Jesus as the cornerstone. Everything else in life, including values, decisions, relationships, and hopes, should be aligned with Him. A life built on any other foundation will not stand.

It also encourages believers who feel rejected or overlooked. God has a pattern of honoring what people despise. Jesus Himself was rejected, yet God made Him the cornerstone. Believers who suffer rejection for their faith can trust that God sees and will vindicate them.

Finally, this verse warns against dismissing Jesus. Many people today, like the builders, consider Jesus unimportant or irrelevant. Believers can share with humility and conviction that the stone others reject is the one God has chosen, and that He is the only secure foundation for life.

### Mark 12:34 — [[Mark 12:34|You Are Not Far From God's Kingdom]]

verse: Mark 12:34

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes a conversation between Jesus and one of the scribes. After a series of hostile questions from Pharisees, Herodians, and Sadducees, this scribe approached Jesus with a sincere question: which commandment is the most important? Jesus answered by quoting the Shema, "Hear, Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one," and the command to love God with all one's heart, soul, mind, and strength. Then He added the second command: love your neighbor as yourself.

The scribe responded with agreement. He affirmed that God is one and that loving God with all one's being and loving one's neighbor as oneself [[v33|is more important than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices]]. This was a bold statement to make in the temple courts, where sacrifices were offered every day. The scribe understood something the prophets had taught: God values love and obedience more than ritual (1 Samuel 15:22; Hosea 6:6; Micah 6:6–8).

Mark says, [[v34|When Jesus saw that he answered wisely]]. The word means thoughtfully or with understanding. The scribe had grasped the heart of God's law. Then Jesus said, [[v34|You are not far from God’s Kingdom]].

This is a remarkable statement. Jesus commended the scribe's understanding. He was close to the kingdom. But "not far" also implies that he was not yet in it. Understanding the right answers about God's commands was a good step, but he still needed to take the final step: to recognize and follow Jesus, the King of the kingdom.

Mark adds that after this, [[v34|No one dared ask him any question after that]]. Jesus' wisdom had silenced all His challengers.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that not all religious leaders opposed Jesus. Some, like this scribe, were sincerely seeking God and recognized truth when they heard it. Jesus responded warmly to such people. Mark's Gospel has shown many scribes as opponents, so this exception is striking. It reminds readers not to stereotype groups but to see individuals.

It also shows that being close to the kingdom is not the same as being in it. A person can understand religious truth, agree with Jesus' teaching, and even be morally serious, yet still not have entered God's kingdom through personal faith and commitment to Jesus. Knowledge and agreement are important, but they must lead to trust and following.

Finally, the verse highlights the priority of love. The scribe understood that love for God and neighbor is more important than sacrifices. This principle is fulfilled in Jesus, who offered Himself as the final sacrifice (Hebrews 10:10–14) and who calls His followers to live lives of love.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites readers to ask whether they are near the kingdom or truly in it. Many people respect Jesus, agree with His moral teaching, and understand the importance of love. But Jesus calls for more than agreement: He calls for faith, repentance, and following Him. Those who are "not far" can take the final step of trusting Christ.

It also encourages believers to recognize and affirm sincere seekers. Some people are honestly searching and are closer to faith than they appear. Believers can engage them with respect, affirm what is true in their understanding, and gently invite them to take the next step toward Jesus.

Finally, this verse calls believers to keep love at the center of their faith. Religious practices matter, but they are not more important than loving God wholeheartedly and loving others as oneself. Believers can regularly examine whether their faith is expressed in genuine love.

### Mark 12:43–44 — The Widow Who Gave All She Had

verse: Mark 12:43-44

#### Simple Explanation

These verses record Jesus' comment after watching people give offerings in the temple. Jesus had sat down opposite the treasury, where offerings were placed in large trumpet-shaped containers. He watched as many rich people threw in large amounts of money. Then a poor widow came and put in two small copper coins, the smallest coins in use at that time. Together they were worth only a tiny fraction of a day's wage.

Jesus called His disciples to Him and said, [[v43|Most certainly I tell you, this poor widow gave more than all those who are giving into the treasury]]. The phrase "most certainly" signals an important teaching. By human calculation, the widow gave the least. But Jesus said she gave the most.

He explained why: [[v44|for they all gave out of their abundance, but she, out of her poverty, gave all that she had to live on]]. The rich gave from their surplus; their gifts did not affect their lifestyle. The widow gave everything she had. God measures gifts not by their amount but by their cost to the giver and the heart behind them.

The setting gives this scene added significance. Just before this, Jesus had warned about scribes who [[v40|devour widows’ houses]]. The religious system that should have protected widows was exploiting them. Yet this widow gave her last coins to God. Some interpreters see Jesus' comment as purely praise for her devotion. Others also see a lament over a system that took everything from the poor. Both may be true: Jesus honors her wholehearted devotion while condemning those who exploit the vulnerable.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage reveals that God sees what others overlook. No one in the temple would have noticed the poor widow's tiny gift. The rich drew attention, but Jesus saw her. God values what is given in faith and sacrifice, not what impresses people.

It also shows that true giving involves trust in God. The widow gave all she had to live on, trusting God to provide for her. Her gift was an act of faith. Paul later praised the Macedonian churches who gave generously out of [[2 Corinthians 8:2|their deep poverty]], first giving themselves to the Lord.

The widow's gift also foreshadows Jesus' own self-giving. Within days, He would give everything, His very life, for others. The widow who gave her whole livelihood points to the Savior who gave His whole life.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to examine their giving. Do they give only from their surplus, what they will not miss? Or does their giving reflect real trust in God? Generosity is measured not by the amount but by the heart and the sacrifice involved. This does not mean believers should neglect necessities or responsibilities to their families, but it does challenge them to give in ways that express faith.

They also encourage those who feel their contributions are small. Many believers have little money, time, or ability compared to others. Jesus sees and values what they give in faith, even if others overlook it. Small gifts given wholeheartedly are precious to God.

Finally, these verses call believers and churches to care for the vulnerable. Jesus condemned those who exploited widows. Churches should never take advantage of the poor or pressure them to give beyond their means. Instead, believers can protect and provide for widows, the elderly, and others in need, as Scripture commands.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 12 shows Jesus facing hostile questions with wisdom and truth. The parable of the tenants reveals the seriousness of rejecting God's messengers and His Son, and Psalm 118 shows that God turns rejection into victory. Believers can build their lives on Jesus, the cornerstone, and trust that God vindicates those the world rejects.

The questions about taxes and resurrection remind believers to honor their responsibilities to governing authorities while giving themselves wholly to God, and to trust that God is the God of the living, who will raise His people. The greatest commandment and the scribe's response call believers to love God with their entire being and to love their neighbors as themselves. Such love is more important than any religious ritual.

Jesus' words to the scribe, "You are not far from God's kingdom," invite every reader to consider whether they have moved from understanding to faith. Knowing the right answers is not enough; entering the kingdom means trusting and following Jesus.

Finally, the contrast between the scribes and the widow challenges believers' values. The scribes sought honor and exploited the vulnerable while praying long prayers. The widow quietly gave all she had. Believers can resist the desire for recognition, protect those who are vulnerable, and give generously and sacrificially, trusting that God sees and values what is given in faith.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the stone the builders rejected, whom God made the cornerstone. Help me to build my whole life on You. Thank You for answering every challenge with wisdom and truth, and help me to give to others what I owe them while giving myself wholly to God.

Teach me to love God with all my heart, soul, mind, and strength, and to love my neighbor as myself. If I am only near Your kingdom, draw me fully in. Guard me from seeking honor and recognition, and from ever taking advantage of those who are vulnerable. Give me the faith and generosity of the poor widow, who gave all she had. Thank You that You see what others overlook, and that You gave Your whole life for me. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
