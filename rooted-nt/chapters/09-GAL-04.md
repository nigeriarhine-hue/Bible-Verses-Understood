# Galatians 4
theme: No Longer Slaves, but Children and Heirs
main: Paul explains that before Christ came, God's people were like underage heirs, no better off than slaves. But when the fullness of time came, God sent His Son, born of a woman and under the Law, to redeem those under the Law so that they might be adopted as God's children, and He sent the Spirit of His Son into their hearts crying, "Abba, Father." Paul pleads with the Galatians not to return to slavery, recalls their warm welcome when he first came to them in illness, and longs for Christ to be formed in them. Using the story of Hagar and Sarah, he shows that believers are children of promise and of freedom.

## Chapter Background and Story

Chapter 4 continues Paul's argument from chapter 3. He has shown that believers are children of God and heirs of the promise to Abraham through faith in Christ (Galatians 3:26–29). Now he develops what it means to be a child and heir, and he warns the Galatians about the folly of going back to a state of slavery.

The chapter moves through three sections. First, Paul uses an illustration from inheritance law to explain the difference between life before Christ and life in Christ (4:1–11). Second, he makes a heartfelt personal appeal, reminding the Galatians of their relationship with him (4:12–20). Third, he offers a figurative reading of the story of Abraham's two sons, Ishmael and Isaac, to show that believers in Christ are children of the free woman, heirs of God's promise (4:21–31).

Throughout, the key contrast is between slavery and sonship, bondage and freedom. Paul is deeply concerned that the Galatians, who had been set free, were on the verge of putting themselves back into chains.

### Heirs Under Guardians (4:1–3)

[[v1|But I say that so long as the heir is a child, he is no different from a bondservant, though he is lord of all]], [[v2|but is under guardians and stewards until the day appointed by the father]]. In the ancient world, a young heir to a great estate had no more freedom than a household slave until he came of age. He was under the authority of guardians and managers until the time his father had set.

[[v3|So we also, when we were children, were held in bondage under the elemental principles of the world]]. "Elemental principles" translates a word that can mean basic principles, the ABCs of something, or the elemental spiritual forces believed to govern the world. Paul seems to use it broadly for the basic, immature religious systems people lived under before Christ, whether the Law for Jews or pagan religion for Gentiles. Both were forms of bondage compared with the freedom of God's children.

### God Sent His Son and His Spirit (4:4–7)

These central verses are studied below.

### Do Not Turn Back (4:8–11)

[[v8|However at that time, not knowing God, you were in bondage to those who by nature are not gods]]. Before they believed, the Galatians had worshiped idols. The question in verse 9 is studied below.

[[v10|You observe days, months, seasons, and years]]. The Galatians had begun to keep the Jewish religious calendar, such as Sabbaths, new moons, and festivals, as requirements for righteousness. Paul is not condemning the observance of special days in itself; in Romans 14:5–6 he allows believers freedom on such matters. His concern is that the Galatians were treating these observances as necessary to be right with God, which was a return to bondage. [[v11|I am afraid for you, that I might have wasted my labor for you]].

### A Personal Appeal (4:12–20)

Paul's tone becomes tender and personal. [[v12|I beg you, brothers, become as I am, for I also have become as you are. You did me no wrong]]. Paul, a Jew, had lived like a Gentile among them, free from the Law's requirements, in order to reach them. Now he asks them to become like him, free in Christ.

[[v13|but you know that because of weakness in the flesh I preached the Good News to you the first time]]. Paul had first come to Galatia because of an illness. We do not know what it was. Some suggest malaria, which was common along the coast, leading him to travel to the higher, cooler region. Others connect it with his eyes because of verse 15. [[v14|That which was a temptation to you in my flesh, you didn’t despise nor reject; but you received me as an angel of God, even as Christ Jesus]]. In the ancient world, illness was often seen as a sign of divine displeasure, and some people might have spat or turned away from someone sick. The Galatians did not. They welcomed Paul warmly.

[[v15|What was the blessing you enjoyed? For I testify to you that, if possible, you would have plucked out your eyes and given them to me]]. Their love had been so great they would have given him anything, perhaps literally their own eyes if his illness affected his sight. [[v16|So then, have I become your enemy by telling you the truth?]] Paul is pained that their affection has cooled because he told them the truth.

[[v17|They zealously seek you in no good way. No, they desire to alienate you, that you may seek them]]. The false teachers were courting the Galatians, but for selfish reasons. They wanted to cut them off from Paul so the Galatians would depend on them. Paul's deep longing for them is studied below.

### Hagar and Sarah (4:21–31)

[[v21|Tell me, you that desire to be under the law, don’t you listen to the law?]] "The law" here means the books of Moses, including Genesis. Paul turns to a story from Genesis to make his point.

[[v22|For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by the servant, and one by the free woman]]. Abraham's first son, Ishmael, was born to Hagar, Sarah's Egyptian servant, when Abraham and Sarah tried to fulfill God's promise by human means (Genesis 16). His second son, Isaac, was born to Sarah, the free woman, in her old age, as God had promised (Genesis 21). [[v23|However, the son by the servant was born according to the flesh, but the son by the free woman was born through promise]].

[[v24|These things contain an allegory, for these are two covenants. One is from Mount Sinai, bearing children to bondage, which is Hagar]]. Paul uses the story figuratively. Hagar represents the covenant from Mount Sinai, the Law, which, when used as a way of being right with God, produces bondage. [[v25|For this Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia, and answers to the Jerusalem that exists now, for she is in bondage with her children]]. "The Jerusalem that exists now" refers to the religious system centered on the Law that the false teachers were promoting. [[v26|But the Jerusalem that is above is free, which is the mother of us all]]. The heavenly Jerusalem, God's new covenant community, is free, and all believers are her children.

[[v27|For it is written, “Rejoice, you barren who don’t bear. Break out and shout, you who don’t travail. For the desolate women have more children than her who has a husband.”]] Paul quotes Isaiah 54:1, a promise of joy to a desolate Jerusalem after exile. Just as Sarah, once barren, became the mother of a great people, so God's new community, born by His promise, would have countless children.

[[v28|Now we, brothers, as Isaac was, are children of promise]]. Believers, like Isaac, were born by God's power and promise, not by human effort. [[v29|But as then, he who was born according to the flesh persecuted him who was born according to the Spirit, so also it is now]]. Jewish tradition interpreted Genesis 21:9 as Ishmael mocking or mistreating Isaac. Paul sees a parallel in how the false teachers were pressuring the Galatian believers.

[[v30|However, what does the Scripture say? “Throw out the servant and her son, for the son of the servant will not inherit with the son of the free woman.”]] Quoting Sarah's words in Genesis 21:10, Paul calls the Galatians to reject the false teaching that would bring them into bondage. [[v31|So then, brothers, we are not children of a servant, but of the free woman]].

It is important to read this passage carefully. Paul is contrasting two ways of relating to God, by human effort under the Law or by God's promise and Spirit, and he is addressing false teachers within the church. He is not condemning the Jewish people as a whole, whom he loved deeply and for whom he prayed (Romans 9:1–5; 10:1). Nor should this passage be used to demean Hagar or her descendants. Genesis shows God's tender care for Hagar: He saw her in her distress, called her by name, and promised to bless her son (Genesis 16:7–13; 21:17–20). Hagar is the only person in Scripture who gives God a name, calling Him [[Genesis 16:13|You are a God who sees]]. Paul's figurative use of the story makes a point about covenants, not about the worth of persons.

## Important Verses Explained

### Galatians 4:4–5 — When the Fullness of Time Came

verse: Galatians 4:4-5

#### Simple Explanation

These verses describe the turning point of history. "When the fullness of the time came" means when the time set by the Father had arrived, like the date appointed for an heir to come of age (v2). God had been preparing for this moment for centuries.

"God sent out his Son." The Son existed before He was sent. He came from the Father into the world. This shows Christ's divine origin.

"Born to a woman." Jesus was fully human, born as every human being is born. This phrase emphasizes His real humanity.

"Born under the law." Jesus was born a Jew, circumcised on the eighth day, and lived under the Law of Moses. He kept it perfectly.

The purpose is twofold: "that he might redeem those who were under the law," setting them free from its bondage and curse (Galatians 3:13), and "that we might receive the adoption as children." Through Christ, people who were like slaves become God's adopted children with full rights in His family.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses are a compact summary of the incarnation. The eternal Son of God became fully human and entered fully into the human condition, including life under the Law, in order to rescue those who were under it. He stood where they stood so that He could bring them where He is.

The phrase "fullness of the time" suggests that God prepared the world for Christ's coming. Many have noted that the first century provided remarkable conditions for the spread of the gospel: a common language (Greek), Roman roads and relative peace, Jewish communities scattered throughout the empire with synagogues and the Scriptures, and widespread longing for spiritual truth. More importantly, it was the moment God had appointed in His plan, the moment when the promises to Abraham and the prophets would be fulfilled.

"Adoption" was a significant legal practice in the Roman world. An adopted son received a new identity, a new name, and full rights of inheritance, and his old debts were canceled. Paul uses this image to describe what God does for believers. They are not merely forgiven servants; they are beloved children with a full place in the family.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses remind believers that God's timing is purposeful. He sent His Son at exactly the right moment. In seasons when God seems slow, believers can trust that He is working toward the fullness of His time in their lives as well.

They also invite believers to rest in their identity as God's adopted children. Adoption is not a lesser form of belonging; it is a deliberate choice of love. Those who feel unwanted, rejected, or out of place can know that God chose to bring them into His family at great cost, through the sending of His Son.

### Galatians 4:6–7 — The Spirit Cries, "Abba, Father"

verse: Galatians 4:6-7

#### Simple Explanation

God not only sent His Son to make people His children; He also sent His Spirit to make them know it. "Because you are children, God sent out the Spirit of his Son into your hearts."

The Spirit is called "the Spirit of his Son." The same Spirit who filled Jesus now lives in believers, and He shares with them Jesus' own relationship with the Father.

The Spirit cries out, "Abba, Father!" "Abba" is an Aramaic word for father, used by children and adults for their own fathers. It expresses closeness, trust, and affection. Jesus used this word when He prayed in Gethsemane (Mark 14:36). Now believers, by the Spirit, can address God the same way.

"So you are no longer a bondservant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ." Paul switches from "you" plural to "you" singular, making it personal for each reader. Each believer is no longer a slave but a child, and therefore an heir. In the ancient world, "son" was the legal term for an heir; Paul applies it to all believers, men and women alike (see Galatians 3:28; 2 Corinthians 6:18).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the cooperation of the Father, Son, and Spirit in salvation. The Father sent the Son to redeem, and the Father sent the Spirit to dwell in believers' hearts. Salvation is the work of the whole Trinity, bringing people into the very life of God.

Paul writes something very similar in Romans 8:15–16, where he says believers received the Spirit of adoption, by whom they cry "Abba! Father!", and the Spirit testifies with their spirit that they are God's children. Assurance of belonging to God is not only a matter of reasoning; it is also the inner witness of the Spirit, who draws believers to pray to God as Father.

The contrast between slave and child is central. A slave serves out of obligation and fear, never sure of their place. A child belongs, is loved, and will inherit. Returning to the Law as a way of being right with God would be like a child choosing to live as a slave. Paul cannot imagine why anyone would do that.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many believers relate to God more like servants than children. They work hard to please Him, fear His disapproval, and are unsure of their standing. These verses invite them into the warmth of a child's relationship with a loving Father. Prayer can be the simple, trusting cry of "Abba, Father."

For people whose earthly fathers were absent, harsh, or abusive, the word "father" may be painful. God is not like a harmful earthly father. He is the perfect Father, full of love, faithfulness, and care. The Spirit can gradually heal the wounds of the past and teach the heart to trust God as the Father it has always needed.

Being an heir also gives hope. Believers have a future inheritance in God that cannot be lost. Their present struggles are not the whole story; they are heirs of God through Christ.

### Galatians 4:9 — Known by God

verse: Galatians 4:9

#### Simple Explanation

Paul reminds the Galatians of the change that had happened in their lives. "But now that you have come to know God." Once they had worshiped idols (v8); now they knew the true God.

Then Paul corrects himself: "or rather to be known by God." The more important truth is not that they had come to know God, but that God had come to know them. In the Bible, to be "known" by God means more than His awareness of someone. It means being personally chosen, loved, and claimed by Him.

Paul's question follows: "why do you turn back again to the weak and miserable elemental principles, to which you desire to be in bondage all over again?" Having been known and loved by the living God, why would they return to the "weak and miserable" religious systems that could never save them? Paul is astonished that they would trade such a relationship for bondage.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The phrase "known by God" is deeply comforting. A believer's relationship with God depends ultimately not on how well they know Him, which is always partial, but on His knowledge and love of them, which is complete and steadfast. Paul writes similarly in 1 Corinthians 8:3: [[1 Corinthians 8:3|But anyone who loves God is known by him]]. And Jesus said of His sheep, [[John 10:27|I know them]].

Paul's surprising grouping of Jewish Law observance with pagan religion as "elemental principles" needs careful reading. He is not saying the Law was the same as idolatry. The Law was given by God and was holy (Romans 7:12). His point is that any religious system, whether pagan rituals or the Law, when used as the means of being right with God apart from Christ, leaves people in bondage. Only Christ sets people free.

The word "weak" echoes Romans 8:3, which says the Law was weak through the flesh. The Law could not give the power to obey. "Miserable" or "poor" means it could not provide the riches that come only through Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

There are seasons when believers feel distant from God or unsure of how well they know Him. This verse turns attention to what matters most: God knows them. His knowledge is personal, loving, and secure. That can steady the heart when feelings fluctuate.

This verse also warns against drifting back into old patterns of bondage. These might be religious, such as relying on rituals or rule-keeping for acceptance, or they might be the idols of the culture, such as success, approval, or pleasure. Having been known by God, believers are invited to live in the freedom of His love rather than returning to what cannot satisfy.

### Galatians 4:19 — Until Christ Is Formed in You

verse: Galatians 4:19

#### Simple Explanation

Paul's appeal reaches its most tender point. "My little children." He speaks with the affection of a parent. He was their spiritual father (or here, mother), since they came to faith through his ministry.

"Of whom I am again in travail." Paul uses the image of childbirth. He had already gone through the pain of bringing them to faith once. Now, because they were in danger of turning away, he feels as if he must go through labor pains all over again.

"Until Christ is formed in you." Paul's goal is not merely that they agree with correct doctrine but that Christ Himself would take shape in their lives. "Formed" was used of a baby developing in the womb. Paul longs for Christ's character to grow in them until it is fully developed.

Verse 20 adds his frustration: he wishes he could be with them and speak in a different tone, because he is perplexed about them. Letters can be easily misunderstood; Paul longs for face-to-face conversation.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the goal of all Christian ministry and the goal of the Christian life: Christlikeness. Paul expresses the same aim elsewhere. Believers are being transformed into Christ's image (2 Corinthians 3:18), predestined to be conformed to the image of God's Son (Romans 8:29), and called to grow to the full stature of Christ (Ephesians 4:13).

It is remarkable that Paul, a man, uses the image of a mother in labor. He also describes himself as a nursing mother caring for her children in 1 Thessalonians 2:7. These images show the intensity and tenderness of his love. True spiritual leadership is not cold or authoritarian; it involves deep, costly, nurturing love.

The process of Christ being formed in a believer takes time. Like a baby in the womb, it grows gradually and cannot be rushed. It also involves pain, both for those who are growing and for those who love and serve them.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse gives a clear goal for spiritual growth. The aim is not simply knowing more, doing more, or being more religious. It is Christ being formed in the heart: His love, humility, patience, purity, and faithfulness becoming part of who a person is.

It also shows what it means to care for others spiritually. Parents, mentors, pastors, and friends who invest in others' faith often experience something like Paul's labor pains: prayers, tears, patience, and disappointment along the way. This is part of love. Those who feel weary in this work can take heart that Paul felt the same, and that God is faithful to complete what He begins.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Galatians 4 celebrates the identity of believers as God's adopted children. Before Christ, people lived like heirs who were still under guardians, no better off than slaves. But when the time was right, God sent His Son, born of a woman and under the Law, to redeem them and give them the full rights of children. He also sent His Spirit into their hearts, so that they can call God "Abba, Father." This means believers can approach God with the confidence and affection of children, not with the fear of servants.

This identity changes everything. Believers are no longer slaves to sin, to empty religion, or to the expectations of others. They are known by God, loved by Him, and heirs of His promises. Paul's warning against returning to bondage applies today whenever believers drift into trying to earn God's approval or chase the false securities of the world.

Paul's personal appeal also shows the heart of genuine Christian love. He remembered the Galatians' kindness when he was sick, grieved when their affection cooled, and longed like a mother in labor for Christ to be formed in them. This reveals the goal of spiritual growth, Christlikeness, and the patient, costly love required to help others grow.

Finally, the story of Hagar and Sarah, read figuratively, reminds believers that they are children of promise and of freedom. Their life with God rests not on human achievement but on God's faithful promise. At the same time, the original story reminds readers that God sees and cares for the overlooked and the mistreated, as He did for Hagar. Believers who have been made free in Christ are called to stand firm in that freedom and to extend His care to others.

## Chapter Prayer

Abba, Father, thank You that when the time was right You sent Your Son, born of a woman and born under the Law, to redeem me and make me Your child. Thank You for sending the Spirit of Your Son into my heart, so that I can call You Father. Help me to live as Your beloved child and heir, not as a fearful servant. Thank You that I am known by You; when I feel distant, remind me that Your love holds me. Keep me from turning back to anything that would put me in bondage again. Form Christ in me, day by day, until His character fills my life. Give me patient, tender love for those I help to grow in faith. Thank You that I am a child of promise and of freedom. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
