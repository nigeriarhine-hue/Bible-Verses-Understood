# 2 Corinthians 12
theme: My Grace Is Sufficient for You
main: Continuing his reluctant boasting, Paul describes a man in Christ caught up to the third heaven, then turns at once to the thorn in the flesh God allowed to keep him from pride. When he pleaded three times for its removal, the Lord answered that His grace is sufficient and His power is made perfect in weakness, so Paul learned to delight in weakness, for when he is weak, then he is strong. He reminds the Corinthians of the signs of an apostle done among them, assures them he seeks not their possessions but them, and expresses his fear of finding unrepented sin when he comes.

## Chapter Background and Story

Chapter 12 continues the "Fool's Speech" that Paul began in chapter 11. His rivals in Corinth boasted about extraordinary spiritual experiences: visions, revelations, and displays of power. They may have suggested that Paul's lack of such boasting showed he was spiritually inferior.

Paul responds by describing an experience more extraordinary than anything they could claim. Yet he tells it in a strange, indirect way, speaking of "a man in Christ" rather than saying "I." And he quickly moves from the vision to something far less glamorous: a painful, humbling affliction that God refused to remove. This is the heart of the chapter, and indeed one of the central messages of the whole letter. The power of Christ is most clearly seen not in human strength but in human weakness.

The chapter then turns back to Paul's relationship with the Corinthians. He is preparing for a third visit and is worried about what he will find. His love for them shines through, along with his fear that sin and division may still be present.

### Visions and Revelations (12:1–6)

[[v1|It is doubtless not profitable for me to boast, but I will come to visions and revelations of the Lord]]. Paul has no interest in boasting, but the situation forces him to speak.

[[v2|I know a man in Christ who was caught up into the third heaven fourteen years ago—whether in the body, I don’t know, or whether out of the body, I don’t know; God knows]]. Almost all interpreters agree that Paul is speaking about himself (see v7), but he refers to himself in the third person to distance himself from any sense of personal glory. Fourteen years before writing would place this experience around AD 42, during the years Paul spent in Syria and Cilicia before his missionary journeys, a period about which Acts says little.

"The third heaven" was a way of describing the very presence of God, beyond the sky (the first heaven) and the realm of the stars (the second). Paul also calls it "Paradise" (v4), the word Jesus used when He promised the dying thief, [[Luke 23:43|today you will be with me in Paradise]]. Paul honestly does not know whether he was taken there bodily or only in spirit. He leaves that to God.

[[v4|how he was caught up into Paradise and heard unspeakable words, which it is not lawful for a man to utter]]. What Paul heard was too sacred to repeat. Unlike the rival teachers, who apparently made much of their experiences, Paul kept this silent for fourteen years.

[[v5|On behalf of such a one I will boast, but on my own behalf I will not boast, except in my weaknesses]]. Paul is willing to speak about the experience as something God did, but he will not use it to build his own reputation. [[v6|But I refrain, so that no man may think more of me than that which he sees in me or hears from me]]. Paul wants to be evaluated by his life and teaching, not by private experiences that others cannot test.

### The Thorn in the Flesh (12:7–10)

Paul now turns from the heights of heaven to the reality of his weakness. The thorn, his prayers, the Lord's answer, and Paul's response are studied below.

### The Signs of an Apostle (12:11–13)

[[v11|I have become foolish in boasting. You compelled me, for I ought to have been commended by you, for I am in no way inferior to the very best apostles, though I am nothing]]. Paul ends his boasting with a gentle rebuke. The Corinthians should have defended him. Instead, they forced him to defend himself. Yet he adds "though I am nothing," a reminder that all he is comes from God's grace (1 Corinthians 15:10).

[[v12|Truly the signs of an apostle were worked among you in all perseverance, in signs and wonders and mighty works]]. When Paul founded the church in Corinth, God confirmed his message with miraculous works. Notice that Paul mentions "perseverance" first. Endurance through hardship was itself a sign of genuine apostleship. Paul does not dwell on the miracles; he simply reminds the Corinthians that they saw them.

[[v13|For what is there in which you were made inferior to the rest of the assemblies, unless it is that I myself was not a burden to you? Forgive me this wrong!]] Once again, irony. The only "wrong" Paul did was refusing to take their money.

### Not Yours, but You (12:14–18)

Paul announces his third visit and explains his continued refusal to accept support from them. His heart for them is studied below.

Some apparently accused Paul of trickery, suggesting that while he refused money personally, he used the collection to take advantage of them. [[v16|Even so, I myself didn’t burden you. But you might say that being crafty, I caught you with deception]]. Paul answers by pointing to Titus and the brother who had gone with him: [[v18|Did Titus take any advantage of you? Didn’t we walk in the same spirit? Didn’t we walk in the same steps?]] Their conduct was above reproach, and the Corinthians knew it.

### Fears for the Coming Visit (12:19–21)

[[v19|Again, do you think that we are excusing ourselves to you? In the sight of God we speak in Christ. But all things, beloved, are for your edifying]]. Paul's defense is not about protecting his own reputation. It is for their good, for building them up.

Then he voices his fears. [[v20|For I am afraid that perhaps when I come, I might find you not the way I want to, and that I might be found by you as you don’t desire, that perhaps there would be strife, jealousy, outbursts of anger, factions, slander, whisperings, proud thoughts, or riots]]. These were the very sins that had troubled the church from the beginning (1 Corinthians 1:10–11; 3:3). [[v21|that again when I come my God would humble me before you, and I would mourn for many of those who have sinned before now, and not repented of the uncleanness, sexual immorality, and lustfulness which they committed]]. Paul does not anticipate their sin with anger alone but with mourning. A shepherd grieves over sheep who have wandered. He would feel humbled by their failure, because he loved them as his own.

## Important Verses Explained

### 2 Corinthians 12:7–8 — A Thorn in the Flesh

verse: 2 Corinthians 12:7-8

#### Simple Explanation

After describing his extraordinary vision, Paul explains that it came with a cost. "By reason of the exceeding greatness of the revelations, that I should not be exalted excessively, a thorn in the flesh was given to me." The danger after such an experience was pride. To keep Paul humble, a "thorn in the flesh" was given to him.

The word translated "thorn" can refer to a sharp stake or splinter. It suggests something painful, persistent, and difficult to ignore. "In the flesh" probably means it affected his body or his earthly life.

Paul also calls it "a messenger of Satan to torment me." This is striking. The thorn was "given" to Paul, and the purpose stated is God's purpose of keeping him humble, yet the agent of the torment was Satan. As in the story of Job, Satan intended harm, but God used even this for good.

Paul responded by praying. "I begged the Lord three times that it might depart from me." He did not passively accept it. He earnestly asked for relief, repeatedly.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

What was Paul's thorn? Scripture does not say, and many suggestions have been made. Some think it was a physical illness, perhaps an eye disease (see Galatians 4:13–15, where Paul mentions a bodily illness and says the Galatians would have given him their eyes), recurring malaria, or another chronic condition. Others think it was persistent opposition from enemies of the gospel, since the Old Testament sometimes describes enemies as thorns in the side (Numbers 33:55). Still others have suggested a spiritual struggle or temptation. Because Paul does not name it, believers with many different kinds of suffering have been able to find themselves in his story.

Paul's three prayers echo Jesus in Gethsemane, who prayed three times that the cup might pass from Him, while submitting to the Father's will (Mark 14:32–41). Like Jesus, Paul brought his honest request to God, and like Jesus, he received an answer different from what he asked.

This passage holds together two truths. God is sovereign, able to use even evil and suffering for good purposes (Genesis 50:20; Romans 8:28). And suffering is real and painful, and believers are free to plead for relief. Paul did not pretend the thorn was pleasant, and he did not stop praying because he believed God was in control.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many believers carry their own "thorn": a chronic illness, a disability, a painful relationship, ongoing opposition, or a struggle that will not go away. Paul's example gives permission to pray honestly and persistently for relief. Asking God to remove suffering is not a lack of faith; it is an expression of trust in His power and care.

This passage also guards against simplistic explanations of suffering. Paul's thorn was not a punishment for sin, nor was it a sign of weak faith. It came to one of the most faithful servants of God. Those who suffer should not be told their pain is their fault or that more faith would surely remove it. God may heal, and believers should pray for healing, but He may also, as with Paul, give grace to endure.

### 2 Corinthians 12:9 — My Grace Is Sufficient

verse: 2 Corinthians 12:9

#### Simple Explanation

After Paul's three prayers, the Lord answered. But the answer was not, "Yes, I will remove it." It was, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness."

"My grace is sufficient for you." God's grace, His favor, presence, and strength, would be enough for Paul to live with the thorn. The tense of the verb "has said" in Greek suggests that this word continued to stand for Paul; it was a lasting answer he could keep returning to.

"For my power is made perfect in weakness." The word "perfect" means brought to completion or fully displayed. God's power is most clearly shown when human strength runs out. When Paul could not rely on his own strength, the power of Christ was seen more plainly.

Paul's response is remarkable: "Most gladly therefore I will rather glory in my weaknesses, that the power of Christ may rest on me." Instead of resenting his weakness, Paul embraced it, because it made room for Christ's power. The phrase "rest on me" uses a word related to "tent" or "dwelling," suggesting that Christ's power would dwell over Paul like God's presence over the tabernacle.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is the heart of 2 Corinthians. The whole letter has wrestled with the question of what true strength looks like. The rival teachers displayed power through eloquence, impressive appearance, and visions. Paul's life was marked by suffering, weakness, and humility. Here the Lord Himself declares which kind of life displays His power: the life that depends on His grace.

This pattern is seen throughout Scripture. God reduced Gideon's army to three hundred men so that Israel could not boast in its own strength (Judges 7:2). He chose David, the youngest son, over his impressive brothers (1 Samuel 16:7). He chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong (1 Corinthians 1:27). Supremely, He accomplished salvation through the weakness of the cross, which Paul calls the power of God (1 Corinthians 1:18).

"Sufficient" is a beautiful word. It does not promise an abundance of comfort or the removal of every hardship. It promises enough: enough strength for today, enough grace for this trial. Like the manna in the wilderness, God's grace is given as it is needed, and it is always enough.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

When prayers for relief seem to go unanswered, this verse offers a different kind of answer. God may not remove every hardship, but He promises His grace will be enough to carry His people through it. That grace often comes day by day, as strength for the next step.

Many people feel ashamed of their weakness, whether physical, emotional, or spiritual. This verse transforms weakness from something to hide into a place where Christ's power can rest. Believers who live with limitations can serve God faithfully, and often their dependence on Him becomes a powerful testimony to others.

This verse also challenges cultures, and churches, that celebrate only strength, success, and polish. Those who are weak, struggling, or limited are not second-class members of God's family. Often they show most clearly what God's grace can do.

### 2 Corinthians 12:10 — When I Am Weak, Then I Am Strong

verse: 2 Corinthians 12:10

#### Simple Explanation

Paul draws a conclusion from the Lord's answer. Because God's power is made perfect in weakness, Paul says, "I take pleasure in weaknesses, in injuries, in necessities, in persecutions, and in distresses, for Christ’s sake."

This list summarizes the kinds of hardship Paul described in chapter 11. "Weaknesses" are human limitations. "Injuries" can also mean insults or mistreatment. "Necessities" are hardships and times of need. "Persecutions" are attacks because of faith. "Distresses" are situations of crushing pressure.

Paul does not take pleasure in these things because he enjoys pain. He takes pleasure in them "for Christ's sake," because they are endured in serving Christ and because they become occasions for Christ's power to be revealed.

Then comes the famous paradox: "For when I am weak, then am I strong." When Paul's own strength failed, he was cast on Christ's strength. In that dependence, he found a strength far greater than his own.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This paradox lies at the center of the Christian life. Jesus said that whoever wants to save their life will lose it, but whoever loses their life for His sake will find it (Matthew 16:25). He said the last will be first and the humble will be exalted. Paul's statement belongs with these sayings of Jesus. The kingdom of God reverses the values of the world.

This does not mean believers should seek out suffering or make themselves weak on purpose. Paul did not go looking for hardship; it came to him as he served Christ. Nor does this verse mean that every kind of suffering should simply be accepted. Paul himself sought to escape danger when possible (2 Corinthians 11:32–33), appealed to his legal rights (Acts 22:25), and prayed for relief. People facing abuse or harm should seek safety. Paul's point is that, when weakness and hardship come in the course of following Christ, they need not be signs of defeat. They can become occasions for God's power.

The strength Paul describes is not the absence of weakness but the presence of Christ in it. Philippians 4:13 expresses the same truth: [[Philippians 4:13|I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people feel that they must be strong, capable, and self-sufficient. When weakness comes, through illness, loss, failure, or exhaustion, it can feel like everything is falling apart. This verse offers a new way to see those moments. They can become the very places where believers experience Christ's strength most deeply.

This truth also brings freedom in serving others. Believers do not need to wait until they feel strong or competent to be useful to God. Often God uses people precisely when they feel inadequate, because then it is clear that the power belongs to Him.

### 2 Corinthians 12:15 — Spend and Be Spent

verse: 2 Corinthians 12:15

#### Simple Explanation

Paul has just explained, once again, why he will not accept financial support from the Corinthians: [[v14|for I seek not your possessions, but you]]. He compares himself to a parent. Parents save up for their children, not the other way around. Paul sees himself as the spiritual father of the Corinthian church (1 Corinthians 4:15), and he wants to give to them, not take from them.

Then he says, "I will most gladly spend and be spent for your souls." Paul is willing to spend everything he has, his money, energy, and time, and to be spent himself, giving his very life for their spiritual good. And he will do this "most gladly," not grudgingly.

His question that follows is full of pain: "If I love you more abundantly, am I loved the less?" Paul's love for them was great, but they seemed to love him less in return. Yet his love did not depend on being loved back.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul's words reflect the heart of Christ, who gave Himself fully for people who did not love Him in return. Romans 5:8 says that God commended His love toward us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us. Paul's willingness to love without return was a reflection of the love he had received.

This verse also contrasts sharply with the rival teachers, who devoured and exploited the Corinthians (2 Corinthians 11:20). False shepherds take from the flock; true shepherds give themselves for the flock. Jesus described the same contrast in John 10:10–11: the thief comes to steal and destroy, but the good shepherd lays down His life for the sheep.

The phrase "not your possessions, but you" captures the essence of genuine ministry and genuine love. Healthy love seeks the good of the other person, not what can be gained from them.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse provides a measure for love in every setting: in families, friendships, churches, and ministry. Is the goal to receive something from others, or to seek their good? Love that says, [[v14|I seek not your possessions, but you]], gives freely without calculating return.

It also brings comfort to those who love others who do not love them back, such as parents of wayward children, spouses in difficult seasons, or pastors serving unappreciative congregations. Paul knew that pain. His example shows that love can keep giving even when it is not returned, because it is rooted in Christ's love. At the same time, love does not require anyone to remain in a situation of abuse or exploitation; seeking another person's good can include setting healthy boundaries.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Second Corinthians 12 brings the letter's central message into focus: God's power is made perfect in weakness. Paul could have boasted about his extraordinary vision of Paradise, but he chose instead to speak about the thorn in his flesh. God did not remove it despite Paul's earnest prayers, but He gave him something greater: the assurance that His grace was sufficient.

This chapter speaks to anyone living with ongoing hardship. Believers are free to pray boldly and persistently for relief, as Paul did, and God often does heal and deliver. But when relief does not come, God's grace is still enough. Weakness is not a sign of His absence or of a lack of faith. It can be the very place where Christ's power rests most clearly.

The chapter also challenges the way people measure spiritual maturity and leadership. Paul refused to build his reputation on spiritual experiences others could not test. He wanted to be known by his life and teaching. Believers today can be cautious about leaders who emphasize their own extraordinary experiences, and they can value humility, faithfulness, and character above spectacle.

Paul's love for the Corinthians offers a model of selfless care. He sought them, not their possessions, and was willing to spend and be spent for their good, even when his love was not returned. Finally, his grief over unrepented sin reminds believers that sin harms the community and that a loving leader mourns over it. The call is to repentance, not out of fear, but because God's grace is sufficient to forgive, restore, and strengthen.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You that Your grace is sufficient for me and that Your power is made perfect in weakness. I bring You the thorns in my life, the pains and struggles that will not go away, and I ask You for relief. Yet if You choose not to remove them, give me grace to endure and let Your power rest on me. Teach me not to hide my weakness or be ashamed of it, but to depend on You in it. Guard me from pride, and keep me from seeking my own glory. Give me a love like Paul's, a love that seeks the good of others rather than what I can gain from them, a love willing to spend and be spent. Where there is unconfessed sin in my life, lead me to repentance. When I am weak, be my strength. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
