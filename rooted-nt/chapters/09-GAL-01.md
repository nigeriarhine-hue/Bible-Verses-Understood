# Galatians 1
theme: There Is No Other Gospel
main: Paul opens his letter by affirming that his apostleship came directly from Jesus Christ and God the Father, and that Christ gave Himself for our sins to rescue us from this present evil age. Astonished that the Galatians are turning so quickly to a different gospel, he declares that there is no other good news and that anyone who preaches another should be under God's curse. He explains that the gospel he preached came by revelation from Jesus Christ, telling how God transformed him from a violent persecutor of the church into a preacher of the faith he once tried to destroy.

## Chapter Background and Story

Paul had planted the churches of Galatia with great joy. When he first arrived there, he was ill, yet the Galatians welcomed him as if he were an angel of God, even as Christ Jesus Himself (Galatians 4:13–14). Many believed the gospel and received the Holy Spirit. They were mostly Gentiles who had turned from idols to the living God.

After Paul moved on, other teachers came. They taught that Gentile believers needed to be circumcised and to observe the Law of Moses, including its calendar and food laws, to be full members of God's people. These teachers may have presented themselves as representatives of the Jerusalem church, and they seem to have questioned Paul's authority, suggesting that he was a second-hand apostle who had watered down the message to please Gentiles.

Paul's response is the most urgent and emotional of all his letters. He does not begin with thanksgiving, as he usually does. Instead, after a brief greeting, he expresses astonishment and alarm. For Paul, the issue was not a minor disagreement about religious customs. It was about the heart of the gospel: whether people are made right with God by faith in Christ alone, or by Christ plus the works of the Law.

### Greeting (1:1–5)

[[v1|Paul, an apostle—not from men, nor through man, but through Jesus Christ, and God the Father, who raised him from the dead—]]. From the very first sentence, Paul answers the charge that his authority came secondhand. He was not appointed by a human committee or sent by a human church. He was commissioned directly by the risen Christ on the road to Damascus (Acts 9:3–6). The mention of the resurrection is significant: Paul's apostleship came from the risen Lord.

[[v2|and all the brothers who are with me, to the assemblies of Galatia]]. The letter was written to several churches in the region. Notice that Paul offers no words of praise here, unlike most of his letters. The greeting of grace and peace, and Christ's rescuing work, are studied below.

### No Other Gospel (1:6–10)

Paul expresses his amazement that the Galatians are turning away so quickly. This section is studied below.

### The Gospel by Revelation (1:11–12)

[[v11|But I make known to you, brothers, concerning the Good News which was preached by me, that it is not according to man]]. [[v12|For I didn’t receive it from man, nor was I taught it, but it came to me through revelation of Jesus Christ]]. Paul is not saying he never learned anything from other believers. In 1 Corinthians 15:3, he says he passed on what he had received about Christ's death and resurrection. His point here is that the core of his gospel, that the crucified Jesus is the risen Lord and that salvation comes by grace through faith to Jew and Gentile alike, was revealed to him by Christ Himself. He did not invent it, and he did not receive it as an approved message from human authorities.

### Paul's Former Life (1:13–14)

[[v13|For you have heard of my way of living in time past in the Jews’ religion, how that beyond measure I persecuted the assembly of God and ravaged it]]. Paul does not hide his past. Acts tells how he approved of Stephen's death and went from house to house dragging believers off to prison (Acts 8:1–3). [[v14|I advanced in the Jews’ religion beyond many of my own age among my countrymen, being more exceedingly zealous for the traditions of my fathers]]. Paul had been more devoted to the Law and the traditions than the teachers now troubling the Galatians. He knew that path from the inside, which makes his testimony all the more powerful.

### Called by Grace (1:15–24)

God's call of Paul, studied below, changed everything. Afterward, Paul did not rush to Jerusalem for approval. [[v17|nor did I go up to Jerusalem to those who were apostles before me, but I went away into Arabia. Then I returned to Damascus]]. "Arabia" probably refers to the Nabataean kingdom, south and east of Damascus. Paul may have gone there to pray and reflect, to preach, or both. Some have noted that Mount Sinai was traditionally located in Arabia (Galatians 4:25), and that Paul, like Elijah, may have gone to the place where God gave the Law to reflect on its fulfillment in Christ.

[[v18|Then after three years I went up to Jerusalem to visit Peter, and stayed with him fifteen days]]. The word translated "visit" suggests getting to know someone, perhaps learning from him about Jesus' earthly life. [[v19|But of the other apostles I saw no one except James, the Lord’s brother]]. James was the brother of Jesus who became a leader of the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13). Paul's contact with the Jerusalem leaders was brief and personal; he did not receive his commission from them.

[[v20|Now about the things which I write to you, behold, before God, I’m not lying]]. Paul takes an oath because the accusations against him were serious.

[[v21|Then I came to the regions of Syria and Cilicia]]. Cilicia included Paul's hometown, Tarsus (Acts 9:30). [[v22|I was still unknown by face to the assemblies of Judea which were in Christ]], [[v23|but they only heard, “He who once persecuted us now preaches the faith that he once tried to destroy.”]] [[v24|So they glorified God in me]]. The churches in Judea did not know Paul personally, but the news of his transformation led them to praise God. The very believers he had once attacked now rejoiced because of what God had done in him.

## Important Verses Explained

### Galatians 1:3–5 — Christ Gave Himself to Rescue Us

verse: Galatians 1:3-5

#### Simple Explanation

Paul's greeting is short, but it is packed with the gospel. "Grace to you and peace" was Paul's usual greeting, combining a Greek word of greeting (grace) with the Hebrew greeting (shalom, peace). Both come "from God the Father and our Lord Jesus Christ."

Then Paul adds something he does not usually include in a greeting. He describes what Christ has done: He "gave himself for our sins." Jesus did not merely give teaching or an example. He gave Himself, His very life, as a sacrifice for sins.

The purpose was "that he might deliver us out of this present evil age." The word "deliver" means to rescue, as one would rescue someone from danger. "This present evil age" refers to the world as it is now, under the power of sin, death, and evil forces. Christ came to rescue people from that domain and bring them into God's new age.

All of this happened "according to the will of our God and Father." Salvation was not an accident or a backup plan. It was God's purpose from the beginning. And so Paul bursts into praise: "to whom be the glory forever and ever. Amen."

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul packs the core of his argument into this greeting. If Christ gave Himself for our sins, then nothing more is needed to deal with sin. The teachers in Galatia were implying that faith in Christ was not enough and that the Law must be added. Paul's opening words already answer them: Christ's self-giving is sufficient.

The idea of "two ages" was common in Jewish thought. There was the present age, marked by sin and suffering, and the age to come, when God would set all things right. Paul teaches that in Christ, the age to come has already broken into the present. Believers still live in the present age, but they have been rescued from its power and belong to the new creation (Galatians 6:15). This is why returning to the Law as a way of being right with God would be a step backward into the old age.

The closing praise reminds readers that the goal of salvation is God's glory. Paul cannot describe what Christ has done without breaking into worship.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people feel trapped by the patterns of this present age: addictions, anxieties, injustices, and the pull of sin. This verse proclaims that Christ came to rescue. His self-giving love is powerful enough to deliver people from what holds them captive.

It also reminds believers that their salvation rests not on what they do but on what Christ has done. On days when they feel they have not done enough, they can return to this truth: He gave Himself for their sins. Their response is not anxious striving but grateful worship, giving glory to God forever.

### Galatians 1:6–8 — No Other Gospel

verse: Galatians 1:6-8

#### Simple Explanation

Instead of giving thanks, as he usually does at this point in a letter, Paul expresses astonishment. "I marvel that you are so quickly deserting him who called you in the grace of Christ." The word "deserting" was used of soldiers abandoning their post or of someone changing allegiance. The Galatians were not just changing an opinion; they were turning away from God Himself, who had called them by grace.

They were turning "to a different 'good news'." But Paul immediately corrects himself: "there isn't another 'good news.'" There is only one gospel. What the false teachers were offering was not good news at all; it was a distortion. "There are some who trouble you and want to pervert the Good News of Christ." These teachers were causing confusion and twisting the message.

Then Paul makes a startling statement. Even if he himself, or an angel from heaven, preached a different gospel, that person should be "cursed." The word refers to being placed under God's judgment. Paul repeats it in verse 9 for emphasis.

Paul is not saying that the truth of the gospel depends on him. On the contrary, he says the gospel is so fixed and certain that even he, the one who preached it, has no authority to change it.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Why does Paul react so strongly? Because the gospel is about how people are made right with God and saved from judgment. If the message is distorted, people are led away from the only way of salvation. Adding the works of the Law as a requirement for acceptance with God would mean that Christ's death was not enough. Paul will say this directly in Galatians 2:21: if righteousness came through the Law, then Christ died for nothing.

This passage also shows that a message can keep Christian language and still be "another gospel." The false teachers likely spoke about Jesus and claimed to follow Him. Their error was adding something to faith in Christ as a requirement for belonging to God. Paul warns that "Christ plus" anything turns grace into something else.

The reference to "an angel from heaven" is striking. Spiritual experiences, visions, and claims of new revelation must all be tested by the gospel already given. No experience, however impressive, can override the truth revealed in Christ.

At the same time, Paul's severe words are directed at those who distort the core of the gospel, not at believers who differ on secondary matters. Paul himself was flexible in many practices (1 Corinthians 9:19–23) and urged patience with differing convictions (Romans 14). His curse is reserved for those who corrupt the message of salvation by grace through faith.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

In every generation, there are messages that add to the gospel. Some suggest that believers must follow certain rules, achieve certain experiences, belong to a certain group, or meet certain cultural standards to be truly accepted by God. Others subtract from the gospel, removing the cross, sin, or Christ's lordship. Paul's warning calls believers to hold firmly to the one gospel: Christ crucified and risen, received by grace through faith.

This passage also encourages careful listening. Not every teaching that uses the name of Jesus is faithful to Him. Believers can test what they hear by Scripture, asking whether it points to Christ's finished work as the basis of salvation or quietly adds human requirements.

### Galatians 1:10 — Pleasing God Rather Than People

verse: Galatians 1:10

#### Simple Explanation

Paul's opponents apparently accused him of being a people-pleaser. They may have said he dropped the requirement of circumcision to make the gospel easier for Gentiles to accept. Paul answers with a pointed question: "For am I now seeking the favor of men, or of God?"

The harsh words he has just written (vv8–9) are proof that he is not trying to please people. No one seeking popularity would pronounce a curse on those who distort the gospel.

"For if I were still pleasing men, I wouldn’t be a servant of Christ." The word "still" may refer to Paul's former life, when he sought the approval of religious leaders and advanced in Judaism. Now, as a servant of Christ, his goal is to please God alone. A servant's task is to do the master's will, not to win the approval of others.

Paul is not saying he never cares how people feel or that he tries to offend. Elsewhere he says he seeks to please others for their good and for the sake of the gospel (1 Corinthians 10:33). The point here is about ultimate loyalty. When pleasing people conflicts with faithfulness to Christ, Christ must come first.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Jesus warned His disciples about the danger of seeking human approval. He said, [[John 5:44|How can you believe, who receive glory from one another, and you don’t seek the glory that comes from the only God?]] The desire for approval can quietly shape what people believe, say, and do, pulling them away from truth.

Paul's freedom from people-pleasing came from his identity as a "servant of Christ." He belonged to a Master whose approval mattered more than anyone else's. That gave him courage to speak hard truths, endure rejection, and stand firm when others disagreed.

This verse also explains why the gospel cannot be adjusted to suit popular opinion. The message of Christ crucified has always been offensive to some and foolish to others (1 Corinthians 1:23). Those who preach it faithfully will sometimes disappoint people. Faithfulness to Christ means accepting that cost.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

The desire to be liked is strong in everyone. It can lead believers to stay silent when they should speak, to compromise convictions to fit in, or to measure their worth by others' opinions. This verse invites a reorientation: the most important approval is God's.

This does not mean being harsh or careless about others' feelings. Believers are called to speak the truth in love (Ephesians 4:15). But when faced with a choice between pleasing people and being faithful to Christ, Paul's example points the way. There is great freedom in living for an audience of One.

### Galatians 1:15–16 — Called by His Grace

verse: Galatians 1:15-16

#### Simple Explanation

After describing his violent past, Paul turns to what God did. "But when it was the good pleasure of God." The turning point in Paul's life was not his own decision but God's gracious initiative.

God "separated me from my mother's womb." Even before Paul was born, God had set him apart for a purpose. This echoes the calls of the prophets. God told Jeremiah that before he was born He had set him apart as a prophet to the nations (Jeremiah 1:5), and the Servant in Isaiah 49:1 says the LORD called him from the womb.

God "called me through his grace." Paul did not earn his calling. He was actively persecuting the church when God called him. His calling was pure grace.

The purpose of this call was "to reveal his Son in me, that I might preach him among the Gentiles." God revealed Jesus to Paul, both outwardly on the Damascus road and inwardly in his heart. And the purpose of this revelation was mission: Paul was to proclaim Christ among the non-Jewish nations.

"I didn't immediately confer with flesh and blood." "Flesh and blood" means human beings. Paul did not need human approval to begin obeying God's call.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul's story is a powerful illustration of the gospel he preached. He was not made right with God through his zeal for the Law. In fact, his zeal had led him to persecute God's people. He was saved and called entirely by grace. This is exactly what Paul wants the Galatians to understand: if the most zealous Law-keeper needed grace, then no one can be made right with God by the Law.

The phrase "reveal his Son in me" is notable. Paul speaks not only of seeing Christ but of Christ being revealed within him. This anticipates Galatians 2:20, where Paul says Christ lives in him. The gospel is not just information about Christ; it is a personal encounter with Him that transforms a person from within.

Paul's calling to the Gentiles is also part of God's ancient plan. God promised Abraham that all nations would be blessed through him (Genesis 12:3), and Paul will develop this theme in chapter 3. His mission to the Gentiles was the fulfillment of God's long-standing purpose.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Some people believe their past is too dark for God to use them. Paul's story shows otherwise. God called a violent persecutor and made him the greatest missionary of the early church. No one is beyond the reach of God's grace.

This passage also shows that God's call has a purpose. He reveals His Son to people not only for their own salvation but so that they might share Christ with others. Every believer's story of grace becomes part of God's mission in the world.

Finally, Paul's confidence in God's call encourages believers to obey God without waiting for every human approval. Wise counsel is valuable, as Paul himself later sought (Galatians 2:2). But a clear call from God, confirmed by Scripture and the community of faith over time, does not depend on popularity.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Galatians 1 sets the tone for a letter about the heart of the gospel. Paul begins by reminding his readers that Christ gave Himself for our sins to rescue us from this present evil age. This is the foundation of everything. Salvation is God's work, accomplished in Christ, according to the Father's will. Nothing needs to be added to it.

Because the gospel is so precious, Paul guards it fiercely. He is astonished that the Galatians are turning to a message that adds human requirements to faith in Christ. His strong words warn believers in every age to hold fast to the one true gospel. Messages that add rules, rituals, or achievements as conditions for God's acceptance, or that remove the cross and Christ's lordship, are not good news at all. Believers can test every teaching by asking whether it rests on Christ's finished work.

Paul's example of pleasing God rather than people encourages believers to live with courage and integrity. The approval that matters most is God's. That frees believers to speak truth with love even when it is unpopular, and to resist the pressure to shape their faith around others' opinions.

Finally, Paul's testimony shows the transforming power of grace. The persecutor became a preacher, and the churches that once feared him glorified God because of him. Whatever a person's past, God's grace can call, change, and use them. Every believer's story of grace can bring glory to God and point others to Christ.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, thank You for the gift of Your Son, who gave Himself for my sins to rescue me from this present evil age. To You be the glory forever and ever. Keep me faithful to the one true gospel. Guard my heart against every message that adds to Your grace or takes away from Christ. Give me discernment to recognize distortions of the truth, and courage to hold fast to what You have revealed. Free me from the need to please people, and let my deepest desire be to please You as a servant of Christ. Thank You that You call people by Your grace, even those with the darkest pasts. Reveal Your Son in me more and more, and use my story to bring glory to You and to point others to Jesus. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
