# John 1
theme: The Word Became Flesh: God Revealed in Jesus
main: John opens his Gospel by declaring that the eternal Word, who was with God and was God, through whom all things were made, became flesh and lived among us, full of grace and truth. John the Baptist testifies that he is not the Messiah but the voice preparing the way, and he points to Jesus as the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world. The first disciples begin to follow Jesus, inviting others to "come and see."

## Chapter Background and Story

The Gospel of John is different in style from Matthew, Mark, and Luke. Early Christian tradition identifies its author as John the apostle, the son of Zebedee, "the disciple whom Jesus loved" (John 21:20–24), writing probably near the end of the first century, likely in Ephesus. John includes many events and teachings not found in the other Gospels, such as the wedding at Cana, Jesus' conversations with Nicodemus and the Samaritan woman, the raising of Lazarus, and Jesus' long farewell discourse. John explains his purpose near the end of the book: [[John 20:31|these are written, that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in his name]].

John 1 has two main parts. The first (vv. 1–18), often called the Prologue, is a profound reflection on who Jesus is, written in poetic, deeply theological language. The second (vv. 19–51) tells how John the Baptist testified about Jesus and how the first disciples came to follow Him.

### The Word Who Was God (1:1–5)

John begins with words that deliberately echo Genesis 1:1: [[v1|In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God]]. This verse is studied below. John uses the term "Word" (Greek "Logos") to describe the eternal Son of God before He became human. The Word was the agent of creation: [[v3|All things were made through him. Without him, nothing was made that has been made]]. In the Word was life, and that life was the light of all people. [[v5|The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness hasn’t overcome it]]. The word "overcome" can also mean "understood" or "grasped." Darkness has neither understood the light nor been able to extinguish it. This anticipates the conflict throughout John's Gospel between light and darkness, which reaches its climax at the cross, where the darkness seems to win but is defeated by the resurrection.

### John the Witness (1:6–8)

John (the writer always calls him simply "John," never "John the Baptist") was [[v6|a man sent from God]]. He came as a witness to testify about the light, so that all might believe through him. [[v8|He was not the light]]. The Gospel writer is careful to show that John the Baptist, though important, was only a witness pointing to Jesus. Some people in the first century continued to follow John the Baptist as though he were the Messiah (Acts 19:1–7), so the Gospel writer clarifies his role.

### The Light Came Into the World (1:9–13)

The true light, which gives light to everyone, was coming into the world. [[v10|He was in the world, and the world was made through him, and the world didn’t recognize him]]. The tragedy is that the Creator came into His own creation, and the creation did not know Him. [[v11|He came to his own, and those who were his own didn’t receive him]]. "His own" first refers to Israel, God's chosen people, many of whom rejected Him. But there is good news, studied below: [[v12|But as many as received him, to them he gave the right to become God’s children, to those who believe in his name]].

### The Word Became Flesh (1:14–18)

Then comes the climax of the Prologue: [[v14|The Word became flesh and lived among us]]. This verse is studied below. John the Baptist testified that Jesus, though He came after John in time, existed before him. From His fullness, believers have all received [[v16|grace upon grace]], one blessing after another, an unending supply.

[[v17|For the law was given through Moses. Grace and truth were realized through Jesus Christ]]. The law was itself a gift of God, but in Jesus, God's grace and truth were revealed in their fullness. [[v18|No one has seen God at any time]]. Even Moses was not allowed to see God's face (Exodus 33:20). But [[v18|The only born Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, has declared him]]. (Many manuscripts read "the only begotten God.") Jesus, who shares the closest relationship with the Father, has made God known. To see Jesus is to see what God is like.

### John's Testimony About Himself (1:19–28)

The religious authorities in Jerusalem sent priests and Levites to ask John, "Who are you?" John said plainly, [[v20|I am not the Christ]]. They asked if he was Elijah, whom Malachi 4:5 said would return before the day of the Lord. He said no. (Jesus later said John fulfilled Elijah's role, Matthew 11:14, but John did not claim this title for himself.) They asked if he was "the Prophet," the prophet like Moses promised in Deuteronomy 18:15. He said no.

Finally John answered, [[v23|I am the voice of one crying in the wilderness, ‘Make straight the way of the Lord,’ as Isaiah the prophet said]]. John saw himself as only a voice, a messenger whose importance came entirely from the one he announced. When asked why he baptized, John said he baptized with water, but [[v26|among you stands one whom you don’t know]]. He was not worthy even to untie His sandal strap, the task of the lowest servant. These events took place at Bethany beyond the Jordan, a different Bethany from the village near Jerusalem.

### The Lamb of God (1:29–34)

The next day, John saw Jesus coming toward him and said, [[v29|Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!]] This declaration is studied below. John explained that he had not known who the Messiah would be, but God had told him that the one on whom he saw the Spirit descend and remain would be the one who baptizes with the Holy Spirit. John had seen the Spirit descend like a dove and remain on Jesus. [[v34|I have seen and have testified that this is the Son of God]].

### The First Disciples (1:35–51)

The following day, John was standing with two of his disciples when Jesus walked by. John said again, [[v36|Behold, the Lamb of God!]] The two disciples heard this and followed Jesus. Jesus turned and asked them, [[v38|What are you looking for?]] These are the first words Jesus speaks in John's Gospel, and they are a question every reader must answer. The disciples asked where He was staying. Jesus said, [[v39|Come and see]]. They went and stayed with Him that day. It was about four in the afternoon, a detail that suggests an eyewitness memory.

One of the two was Andrew, Simon Peter's brother. (The other is unnamed, and many believe he was John, the Gospel's author.) Andrew first found his brother Simon and told him, [[v41|We have found the Messiah!]] He brought Simon to Jesus. Jesus looked at him and said, [[v42|You are Simon the son of Jonah. You shall be called Cephas]], which means "rock" in Aramaic, or Peter in Greek. Jesus saw not only who Simon was but who he would become.

The next day Jesus found Philip and said, "Follow me." Philip found Nathanael and told him they had found the one Moses and the prophets wrote about: Jesus of Nazareth. Nathanael was skeptical: [[v46|Can any good thing come out of Nazareth?]] Nazareth was a small, insignificant village. Philip replied simply, [[v46|Come and see]].

When Jesus saw Nathanael coming, He said, [[v47|Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom is no deceit!]] Nathanael was surprised and asked how Jesus knew him. Jesus said He had seen him under the fig tree before Philip called him. Sitting under a fig tree was sometimes associated with studying Scripture and meditating. Nathanael was amazed and declared, [[v49|Rabbi, you are the Son of God! You are King of Israel!]] Jesus said he would see greater things: [[v51|hereafter you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man]]. This recalls Jacob's dream of a ladder reaching to heaven with angels going up and down on it (Genesis 28:12). Jesus is the true connection between heaven and earth, the meeting place of God and humanity.

## Important Verses Explained

### John 1:1 — In the Beginning Was the Word

verse: John 1:1

#### Simple Explanation

This is the opening sentence of John's Gospel, and it is one of the most important sentences in the Bible. Matthew begins with Jesus' genealogy, Mark with John the Baptist, and Luke with the births of John and Jesus. John begins before time itself.

[[v1|In the beginning]]. These words deliberately echo the first words of the Bible, Genesis 1:1: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." John takes his readers back before creation, to eternity.

[[v1|was the Word]]. The Greek word "Logos" means "word," "message," or "reason." For Jewish readers, "word" recalled God's creative word in Genesis ("God said, 'Let there be light'"), His word that came to the prophets, and the personified Wisdom of God in passages like Proverbs 8, which was with God before creation. For Greek readers, "Logos" was a philosophical term for the rational principle that orders the universe. John uses a term both audiences would recognize, and then fills it with new meaning: the Word is a person, Jesus Christ. Notice that "was" describes ongoing existence. When the beginning began, the Word already existed. The Word did not come into being; He always was.

[[v1|and the Word was with God]]. The Word was in close relationship with God. The phrase suggests face-to-face fellowship. The Word is distinct from the Father, yet in intimate communion with Him.

[[v1|and the Word was God]]. The Word is not merely like God or a lesser divine being; He is God. In Greek, the word order emphasizes "God": "and God was the Word." This shows that the Word shares fully in God's nature. Some groups have translated this "the Word was a god," but this translation does not fit the grammar or the context of John's Gospel, which repeatedly affirms Jesus' full deity (John 20:28).

Together, the second and third clauses describe a mystery: the Word is distinct from God (He was "with God") and yet is God. This is one of the foundations of the Christian doctrine of the Trinity: one God existing eternally as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

In verse 14, John identifies the Word: "The Word became flesh and lived among us." The Word is Jesus.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that Jesus is eternal and divine. He did not begin to exist at His birth in Bethlehem. He existed before creation, in perfect fellowship with the Father, and He is fully God. Jesus later said, [[John 8:58|before Abraham came into existence, I AM]], and Thomas would confess Him as [[John 20:28|My Lord and my God!]]

It also reveals that God has always been relational. Before creation, the Word was "with God," in loving fellowship. God did not create the world because He was lonely; He created out of the overflow of the love that eternally existed within Himself.

Finally, the verse shows that God has spoken. The Word is God's self-expression, His communication to the world. In Jesus, God has spoken most clearly and completely (Hebrews 1:1–2).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to worship Jesus as God. He is not merely a great teacher or prophet but the eternal Word, worthy of worship and complete trust. Believers can approach Him with reverence and love.

It also gives believers confidence that God has revealed Himself. They do not have to guess what God is like. They can look at Jesus, the Word, and know God. Reading the Gospels and meditating on Jesus' life and teaching is the clearest way to know God.

Finally, this verse invites believers to listen to God's Word. If Jesus is God's Word, then His teaching carries God's authority. Believers can listen to Him and let His words shape their lives.

### John 1:12 — The Right to Become God's Children

verse: John 1:12

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes right after a sad statement. John had written that the Word, through whom the world was made, came into the world, but the world did not recognize Him (v. 10). He came to His own people, Israel, and they did not receive Him (v. 11). Many rejected Jesus.

But this verse begins with the word [[v12|But]], introducing hope. Not everyone rejected Him. [[v12|as many as received him]]. "Received" means welcomed or accepted. Some did welcome Jesus. The verse then clarifies what receiving Him means: [[v12|to those who believe in his name]]. To receive Jesus is to believe in His name, to trust Him as who He truly is. In the Bible, a person's "name" represents their character and identity.

[[v12|to them he gave the right to become God’s children]]. "Right" can also be translated "authority" or "privilege." Those who receive Jesus are given the privilege of becoming God's children. This is a gift, not something earned. People are not automatically God's children in this sense; they become His children by receiving Christ.

The next verse explains how this happens: they are [[v13|born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God]]. Becoming God's child is not a matter of family heritage ("blood"), human desire ("the will of the flesh"), or human decision alone ("the will of man"). It is a work of God, a new birth that He brings about. Jesus will explain this new birth to Nicodemus in John 3.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of salvation: through faith in Jesus, people become children of God. This is more than forgiveness; it is adoption into God's family. Paul writes, [[Galatians 3:26|For you are all children of God, through faith in Christ Jesus]]. Believers can call God "Father" and share in the inheritance of His children.

It also shows that salvation is both a gift and a response. God gives the right to become His children; people receive Christ and believe in His name. Both God's initiative and human faith are involved, though the new birth itself is God's work (v. 13).

Finally, the verse shows that God's family is not limited by ethnicity or heritage. Some of "His own" rejected Him, but "as many as received him," Jew or Gentile, became God's children. This anticipates the inclusion of all nations in God's family.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites everyone to receive Jesus and believe in His name. Becoming God's child is not about religious background or good works but about trusting Jesus. Anyone can receive Him today.

It also gives believers a secure identity. They are children of God, loved and accepted by their heavenly Father. In times of insecurity, loneliness, or failure, they can remember who they are in Christ.

Finally, this verse calls believers to live as God's children. Children often resemble their parents. As God's children, believers can grow to reflect His character: His love, holiness, truth, and grace.

### John 1:14 — The Word Became Flesh

verse: John 1:14

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the climax of John's Prologue. In verse 1, John declared that the Word was with God and was God. In verse 3, all things were made through Him. Now John states the astonishing truth: [[v14|The Word became flesh and lived among us]].

[[v14|The Word became flesh]]. "Flesh" means full humanity, with a real human body, real human emotions, and real human limitations. The eternal Word, who was God, became a human being. This is called the incarnation, from a Latin word meaning "in flesh." The Word did not merely appear to be human or temporarily inhabit a human body; He truly became human while remaining fully God. Some early teachers claimed Jesus only seemed to have a body, but John insists on His real flesh (1 John 4:2).

[[v14|and lived among us]]. The Greek word literally means "pitched His tent" or "tabernacled." In the Old Testament, God's presence dwelt among Israel in the tabernacle, a tent in the wilderness (Exodus 25:8–9; 40:34–35). Now God's presence dwelt among people in the person of Jesus. He is the true meeting place between God and humanity.

[[v14|We saw his glory]]. "We" refers to John and the other eyewitnesses. They saw Jesus' glory, not only at the transfiguration but in His miracles, His love, His teaching, and supremely in His death and resurrection. In the Old Testament, God's glory filled the tabernacle as a cloud. Now His glory was revealed in Jesus.

[[v14|such glory as of the only born Son of the Father]]. Jesus' glory was the glory of the unique Son, sharing the Father's nature. "Only born" (often translated "only begotten" or "one and only") emphasizes His uniqueness.

[[v14|full of grace and truth]]. These words recall God's self-description to Moses in Exodus 34:6, where God revealed Himself as [[Exodus 34:6|abundant in loving kindness and truth]]. Jesus embodies God's steadfast love (grace) and faithfulness (truth). He is not grace without truth or truth without grace, but full of both.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the wonder of the incarnation. The infinite God became a finite human being. The Creator entered His creation. The eternal Word was born as a baby, grew up, worked, ate, slept, suffered, and died. Paul describes this as Jesus emptying Himself and taking the form of a servant (Philippians 2:6–8).

It also reveals that God came near. He did not remain distant but came to live among His people. This fulfills the promise of Immanuel, "God with us" (Matthew 1:23). God understands human life from the inside.

Finally, the verse shows that the incarnation was necessary for salvation. Because Jesus became human, He could represent humanity and die for human sin. Because He is God, His life and death have infinite value. Hebrews 2:14–17 explains that Jesus shared in flesh and blood so that through death He might destroy the power of death.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to marvel at God's love. God loved the world so much that He came to live among people in Jesus. This love can fill believers with gratitude and worship.

It also brings comfort to believers in their struggles. Jesus experienced human life fully, including weakness, sorrow, temptation, and pain. He understands what believers face and can sympathize with their weaknesses (Hebrews 4:15).

Finally, this verse calls believers to reflect Jesus' grace and truth. Many people lean toward either grace without truth, accepting everything, or truth without grace, being harsh and judgmental. Jesus was full of both. Believers can seek to speak truth with love and to show grace without compromising truth.

### John 1:29 — The Lamb of God Who Takes Away the Sin of the World

verse: John 1:29

#### Simple Explanation

These words were spoken by John the Baptist when he saw Jesus coming toward him at the Jordan River. The day before, religious leaders had asked John who he was, and he told them he was not the Messiah but a voice preparing the way for one who was coming. Now that one appeared.

[[v29|Behold, the Lamb of God]]. "Behold" means "Look!" John directed everyone's attention to Jesus. "Lamb of God" was a rich image for Jewish listeners. Lambs were sacrificed in Israel's worship. Each morning and evening, a lamb was offered in the temple (Exodus 29:38–42). At Passover, each family sacrificed a lamb, recalling how the blood of the Passover lamb protected Israel from judgment in Egypt (Exodus 12). Isaiah 53:7 described the Servant of the Lord as [[Isaiah 53:7|a lamb that is led to the slaughter]], who bore the sins of many. And in Genesis 22:8, Abraham told Isaac that God Himself would provide the lamb for the sacrifice.

By calling Jesus "the Lamb of God," John identified Him as the sacrifice that God Himself provides. Jesus would die like a sacrificial lamb.

[[v29|who takes away the sin of the world!]] "Takes away" means removes or carries off. Jesus would bear sin and remove it. "Sin" is singular, referring to the whole reality of human rebellion against God. "Of the world" shows the scope of His work: not only Israel's sin but the sin of all humanity. Jesus' sacrifice would be for the whole world.

John's Gospel later shows that Jesus was crucified at the time the Passover lambs were being prepared (John 19:14), and that none of His bones were broken, like the Passover lamb (John 19:36; Exodus 12:46).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the central purpose of Jesus' coming: to deal with sin by offering Himself as a sacrifice. The Old Testament sacrificial system pointed forward to Him. Hebrews explains that the blood of animals could not truly take away sins (Hebrews 10:4), but Jesus offered Himself once for all (Hebrews 10:10–14).

It also shows the universal scope of Jesus' work. He takes away the sin "of the world." His sacrifice is sufficient for every person, and the offer of forgiveness is extended to all. John later writes, [[1 John 2:2|He is the atoning sacrifice for our sins, and not for ours only, but also for the whole world]].

Finally, the image of the Lamb shows the humility and gentleness of Jesus. The one who is the eternal Word, through whom all things were made, came as a lamb to be sacrificed. In Revelation, Jesus is pictured as the Lamb who was slain, now standing on the throne, worthy of all worship (Revelation 5:6–12).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to look to Jesus for forgiveness. Whatever sins burden them, Jesus, the Lamb of God, has taken them away. They can come to Him in faith and receive forgiveness.

It also calls believers to point others to Jesus, as John the Baptist did. John did not draw attention to himself; he said, "Behold, the Lamb of God!" Believers can direct people to Jesus as the only one who can take away sin.

Finally, this verse invites believers to worship Jesus with gratitude. The Lamb of God gave Himself for them. Remembering His sacrifice can deepen love for Him and inspire lives of grateful obedience.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

John 1 reveals who Jesus is in the most profound terms. He is the eternal Word, who was with God and was God. Through Him all things were made. He is the light that shines in the darkness, the source of life. He became flesh and lived among us, full of grace and truth. He is the only Son who makes the Father known, the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world, the Son of God, and the King of Israel. Believers can respond with worship, trust, and wonder.

The chapter also shows how people respond to Jesus. Some, including many of His own people, rejected Him. But those who received Him and believed in His name were given the right to become God's children. Everyone must decide how to respond. Believers can rejoice in their identity as God's children and invite others to receive Jesus.

John the Baptist's humility provides an example. He knew he was not the light, only a witness. He pointed people away from himself to Jesus. Believers can serve faithfully while directing attention to Christ.

Finally, the first disciples show how faith spreads. Jesus invited them to "come and see." Andrew brought his brother Peter. Philip told Nathanael to "come and see." Faith often spreads through personal invitation. Believers can invite their family and friends to come and see Jesus for themselves, trusting that He will reveal Himself to those who seek Him.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the eternal Word, who was with God and is God. Through You all things were made, and in You is life and light. I worship You. Thank You that You became flesh and lived among us, full of grace and truth, so that I could see the glory of God and know the Father.

Thank You for giving me the right to become a child of God by receiving You and believing in Your name. You are the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world. Thank You for taking away my sin. Help me to point others to You, as John the Baptist did, and to invite my family and friends to come and see. Fill me with Your grace and truth, and let Your light shine through me in the darkness. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
