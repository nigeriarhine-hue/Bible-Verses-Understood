# Romans 14
theme: Welcoming One Another Across Differences of Conscience
main: Paul addresses disagreements in the Roman church over food and special days, urging believers to welcome those who are weak in faith without arguing over opinions. Those who feel free should not look down on those with stricter consciences, and those with stricter consciences should not judge the free, because God has welcomed both, and each serves the Lord. Believers belong to the Lord in life and death, and each will give an account to God, so they must stop judging one another and instead avoid placing stumbling blocks before others. The kingdom of God is not about food and drink but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit, so believers should pursue what builds others up.

## Chapter Background and Story

Romans 14 deals with a practical problem in the church in Rome. The congregation included believers from different backgrounds who disagreed about certain practices. Some believers, probably mostly Jewish believers and Gentiles influenced by Jewish customs, felt they should avoid certain foods (perhaps meat that might have been offered to idols or that was not prepared according to Jewish law) and should observe special days such as the Sabbath and festivals. Others, probably mostly Gentile believers and Jewish believers like Paul who understood their freedom in Christ, felt free to eat anything and to treat all days alike.

Paul calls the more cautious group "weak in faith" and the freer group, implicitly, "strong" (15:1). By "weak," he does not mean that their faith in Christ was weak or that they were less committed. He means that their consciences were not yet fully persuaded of the freedom they had in Christ in these particular matters. Paul himself identifies with the "strong" (15:1), yet he spends most of the chapter urging the strong to be patient and loving toward the weak.

These issues are what later Christians have called "disputable matters" or "matters of conscience," areas where Scripture does not give a clear command and sincere believers may differ. Paul is not addressing clear moral issues, such as those he lists in 1:29–31 or 13:13. Distinguishing between disputable matters and clear moral commands requires wisdom.

### Accept One Another (14:1–12)

Paul's opening command is studied below. He describes the two groups: [[v2|One man has faith to eat all things, but he who is weak eats only vegetables]]. Then he gives instructions to each: [[v3|Don’t let him who eats despise him who doesn’t eat. Don’t let him who doesn’t eat judge him who eats, for God has accepted him]]. The temptation for the "strong" was to look down on the weak as narrow-minded. The temptation for the "weak" was to judge the strong as unspiritual or careless. Both attitudes are wrong, because God has welcomed both.

[[v4|Who are you who judge another’s servant? To his own lord he stands or falls. Yes, he will be made to stand, for God has power to make him stand]]. Each believer is the Lord's servant, accountable to Him, not to other believers.

Paul then addresses special days: [[v5|One man esteems one day as more important. Another esteems every day alike. Let each man be fully assured in his own mind]]. In disputable matters, each believer should act with a clear conscience. [[v6|He who observes the day, observes it to the Lord; and he who does not observe the day, to the Lord he does not observe it. He who eats, eats to the Lord, for he gives God thanks. He who doesn’t eat, to the Lord he doesn’t eat, and gives God thanks]]. Both groups were trying to honor the Lord. Paul's teaching on belonging to the Lord and on accountability is studied below.

### Don't Cause Others to Stumble (14:13–23)

Paul then turns to the responsibility of the strong. [[v14|I know and am persuaded in the Lord Jesus that nothing is unclean of itself; except that to him who considers anything to be unclean, to him it is unclean]]. Paul agrees with the strong: no food is unclean in itself (Mark 7:19; Acts 10:15). But a person who believes something is wrong would sin against their own conscience by doing it.

[[v15|Yet if because of food your brother is grieved, you walk no longer in love. Don’t destroy with your food him for whom Christ died]]. If a strong believer's freedom causes a weaker believer to stumble, the strong believer is not acting in love. Christ died for that weaker believer; how can a strong believer harm them over something as unimportant as food? Paul's statement about the kingdom of God is studied below.

[[v20|Don’t overthrow God’s work for food’s sake]]. The "work of God" here is the weaker believer, whom God is building up. [[v21|It is good to not eat meat, drink wine, nor do anything by which your brother stumbles, is offended, or is made weak]]. Love is willing to give up freedom for the sake of others.

[[v22|Do you have faith? Have it to yourself before God. Happy is he who doesn’t judge himself in that which he approves]]. Strong believers can enjoy their freedom privately before God, without flaunting it in ways that harm others. [[v23|But he who doubts is condemned if he eats, because it isn’t of faith; and whatever is not of faith is sin]]. If someone does something while believing it is wrong, they sin, because they are acting against their conscience. Acting in faith means acting with confidence that God approves.

### A Note on the Doxology (14:24–26)

In the World English Bible, the closing doxology of Romans appears at the end of chapter 14 (14:24–26). In many other Bibles, these words appear at the end of chapter 16, as verses 25–27. Ancient manuscripts differ on where this doxology belongs; some place it after chapter 14, others after chapter 16, others in both places, and others after chapter 15. The words themselves are a beautiful hymn of praise: [[v26|to the only wise God, through Jesus Christ, to whom be the glory forever! Amen]]. The doxology praises God, who is able to strengthen believers through the gospel and who has revealed the mystery, kept hidden for ages, that all nations would come to the obedience of faith.

## Important Verses Explained

### Romans 14:1 — Accept the One Who Is Weak in Faith

verse: Romans 14:1

#### Simple Explanation

The church in Rome was made up of believers from different backgrounds. Some were Jewish and had grown up observing the food laws and the Sabbath. Others were Gentiles who had never followed those customs. Disagreements arose about what foods believers could eat and which days they should treat as special.

Paul begins his response with a clear command: [[v1|Now accept one who is weak in faith]]. "Accept" means to welcome, to receive warmly into fellowship. It is the same word used in 15:7, where Paul writes, [[Romans 15:7|Therefore accept one another, even as Christ also accepted you]]. Believers should welcome one another as Christ has welcomed them.

"One who is weak in faith" refers to a believer whose conscience is not fully persuaded of their freedom in Christ in certain matters. In this context, the "weak" were those who felt they had to avoid certain foods or observe special days. Their faith in Christ was real, but they had not yet understood all the implications of their freedom.

[[v1|but not for disputes over opinions]]. Believers should welcome the weak, but not in order to argue with them about their convictions. They should not welcome them only to try to change their minds or criticize their scruples. True welcome accepts people as they are.

This verse is the foundation for the rest of the chapter. Before Paul addresses the issues themselves, he establishes the priority of welcome.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reflects the welcoming heart of Christ. Jesus welcomed people of all kinds: tax collectors, sinners, the sick, the poor, children, and outcasts. His church is called to welcome as He did.

It also shows the importance of distinguishing between essential and non-essential matters. The gospel itself, salvation by grace through faith in Christ, is essential. But many practices, preferences, and opinions are matters where believers may differ. Wise believers learn to recognize the difference. A well-known saying, often attributed to early Christian writers, expresses it: "In essentials, unity; in non-essentials, liberty; in all things, charity."

Finally, the verse shows that unity does not require uniformity. Believers do not need to agree on every question in order to be one in Christ. The church can include people with different convictions on disputable matters, united by their common faith and love.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to welcome fellow Christians who see things differently. Believers today may disagree about matters such as styles of worship, alcohol, holidays, entertainment, diet, schooling choices, or other questions where Scripture does not give a clear command. Believers can welcome one another without requiring agreement on every point.

It also warns believers against using fellowship as an opportunity to argue. Some believers welcome others only to correct their opinions. Paul calls for genuine acceptance that does not quarrel over disputable matters.

Finally, the verse encourages humility. Believers who consider themselves "strong" may be tempted to look down on those with stricter consciences. Believers who are more cautious may be tempted to judge those who feel free. Both are called to welcome one another in love.

### Romans 14:7–8 — We Are the Lord's

verse: Romans 14:7-8

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been explaining that believers who disagree about food and special days are all trying to honor the Lord. One person eats to the Lord and gives thanks; another abstains to the Lord and gives thanks. Both are living for Christ.

Now Paul states the principle behind this: [[v7|For none of us lives to himself, and none dies to himself]]. No believer lives only for themselves. Their lives are not their own. Everything they do, including their choices about food and days, is lived in relationship to the Lord.

[[v8|For if we live, we live to the Lord. Or if we die, we die to the Lord]]. Both life and death belong to the Lord. Believers live to honor Him, and when they die, they die belonging to Him.

[[v8|If therefore we live or die, we are the Lord’s]]. This is the heart of the matter. Believers belong to Christ. Whether they are strong or weak in conscience, whether they eat or abstain, whether they live or die, they are His.

In the next verse, Paul explains why: [[v9|For to this end Christ died, rose, and lived again, that he might be Lord of both the dead and the living]]. Christ's death and resurrection established His lordship over all.

Because every believer belongs to the Lord, no believer has the right to judge another believer's service. Each answers to Christ.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the Christian life is centered on Christ's lordship. Believers do not belong to themselves. Paul writes elsewhere, [[1 Corinthians 6:19|You are not your own]], [[1 Corinthians 6:20|for you were bought with a price]].

They also bring deep comfort about death. For believers, death is not the end of their belonging to Christ. They die "to the Lord" and remain His. Paul writes, [[Philippians 1:21|For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain]].

Finally, these verses show that Christ's lordship unites believers. Despite their differences, all believers belong to the same Lord. This shared belonging is the foundation for mutual acceptance.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to live every part of life for the Lord. Eating, working, resting, relating, and choosing can all be done in honor of Christ. Believers can ask in each situation how they can live "to the Lord."

They also bring peace in the face of death. Believers who fear death can remember that whether they live or die, they belong to the Lord. Death cannot take them from His care.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to respect one another's convictions. Since every believer belongs to the Lord and lives for Him, believers can trust that others who differ from them are also seeking to honor Christ.

### Romans 14:12–13 — Each Will Give Account; Do Not Cause Stumbling

verse: Romans 14:12-13

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been urging believers not to judge or despise one another over disputable matters. He had reminded them that each believer belongs to the Lord. Then he pointed to the future: [[v10|For we will all stand before the judgment seat of Christ]]. Every believer will stand before Christ. Paul quoted Isaiah 45:23: every knee will bow and every tongue will confess to God.

[[v12|So then each one of us will give account of himself to God]]. Each believer will give an account "of himself," not of others. Believers are not responsible for judging other believers' service; they are responsible for their own.

This does not mean believers should never correct one another about clear sin; elsewhere Paul commands loving correction (Galatians 6:1). But in disputable matters, each believer is accountable to God for their own convictions and choices.

[[v13|Therefore let’s not judge one another any more]]. Since God is the judge, believers should stop judging one another over these matters.

[[v13|but judge this rather, that no man put a stumbling block in his brother’s way, or an occasion for falling]]. Paul uses a play on words. Instead of "judging" others, believers should "judge" or decide to avoid causing others to stumble. A "stumbling block" is something that causes another person to trip and fall, in this case, to sin against their conscience or to fall away from faith.

This verse shifts the focus from the weak to the strong. Strong believers should be careful not to use their freedom in ways that harm weaker believers.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that accountability to God should shape how believers treat one another. Knowing that each person will stand before Christ humbles believers and frees them from the burden of judging others.

They also show that love limits freedom. Paul writes elsewhere, [[1 Corinthians 8:9|But be careful that by no means does this liberty of yours become a stumbling block to the weak]]. True freedom is not insisting on one's rights but using freedom in love.

Finally, these verses show that the spiritual welfare of others matters. Believers are not isolated individuals. Their choices affect fellow believers. Love considers how one's actions will affect others' faith.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to focus on their own accountability to God. Rather than evaluating others' choices in disputable matters, believers can examine their own hearts and lives.

They also call believers to consider how their freedom affects others. A believer may be free to do something, but if it would cause a fellow believer to stumble, love may call them to set it aside, at least in that person's presence.

Finally, these verses remind believers that they will stand before Christ. This truth can motivate them to live faithfully, to treat others with grace, and to leave judgment in God's hands.

### Romans 14:17–19 — Righteousness, Peace, and Joy in the Holy Spirit

verse: Romans 14:17-19

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been urging strong believers not to use their freedom in ways that harm weaker believers. He had said that if a believer's eating grieves a brother, that believer is no longer walking in love. Now he puts the issue in perspective.

[[v17|for God’s Kingdom is not eating and drinking]]. The kingdom of God, God's rule in the lives of His people, is not mainly about food and drink. These are secondary matters. Believers should not let them become the center of their faith or the cause of division.

[[v17|but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit]]. The kingdom is about "righteousness," right relationship with God and right living; "peace," harmony with God and with one another; and "joy," the deep gladness the Spirit gives. All of these come from the Holy Spirit. These are the things that truly matter.

[[v18|For he who serves Christ in these things is acceptable to God and approved by men]]. Those who serve Christ by pursuing righteousness, peace, and joy please God and earn the respect of others.

[[v19|So then, let’s follow after things which make for peace, and things by which we may build one another up]]. Believers are called to actively pursue peace and mutual upbuilding. "Build up" means to strengthen, like constructing a building. Believers should seek what strengthens one another's faith rather than what tears it down.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the true priorities of God's kingdom. Religious people have often focused on outward practices, such as food, days, and rituals, while neglecting the weightier matters. Jesus criticized those who tithed spices but neglected [[Matthew 23:23|the weightier matters of the law: justice, mercy, and faith]].

They also show the work of the Holy Spirit in the community. Righteousness, peace, and joy are the fruit of the Spirit. When the Spirit is at work, the church is marked by these qualities.

Finally, these verses show the importance of building up the church. Paul repeatedly emphasizes edification (1 Corinthians 14:12; Ephesians 4:12, 29). Believers' words and actions should strengthen others, not weaken them.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses help believers keep perspective. Many conflicts in churches are over secondary matters. Believers can ask whether a disagreement truly concerns the gospel, or whether it is about preferences. The kingdom is about righteousness, peace, and joy, not about secondary matters.

They also encourage believers to pursue peace. Peace does not happen automatically; it requires effort. Believers can take steps to resolve conflicts, overlook minor offenses, and seek harmony.

Finally, these verses call believers to build one another up. Believers can encourage, support, and strengthen fellow believers through their words and actions, asking whether their choices will help or hinder others' faith.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Romans 14 calls believers to welcome one another across differences of conscience. In matters where Scripture does not give clear commands, sincere believers may disagree. Believers are called to accept one another without arguing over opinions, neither looking down on those with stricter consciences nor judging those who feel free.

The chapter also reminds believers that they belong to the Lord in life and in death. Each believer lives for Christ and will give an account to Him. This truth frees believers from judging one another and calls them to focus on their own faithfulness. It also brings comfort: in life and in death, believers are the Lord's.

Romans 14 teaches that love limits freedom. Strong believers may be free to do certain things, but if their freedom would cause a weaker believer to stumble, love calls them to set it aside. Believers can consider how their choices affect others and avoid placing stumbling blocks in their path.

Finally, the chapter keeps believers focused on what matters most. The kingdom of God is not about food and drink but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit. Believers can pursue what makes for peace and builds others up, refusing to let secondary matters divide the body of Christ, while still standing firm on the clear teachings of the gospel.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for welcoming me into Your family. Help me to welcome others as You have welcomed me, especially those who see things differently than I do. Keep me from looking down on those whose consciences are stricter than mine, and from judging those who feel free where I do not. Thank You that in life and in death I belong to You. Remind me that I will give an account to You for my own life, and free me from the burden of judging others. Teach me to use my freedom in love, never placing a stumbling block in a brother's or sister's way. Help me to remember that Your kingdom is righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit. Make me a person who pursues peace and builds others up. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
