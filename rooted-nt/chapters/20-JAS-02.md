# James 2
theme: Faith Without Favoritism, Faith That Works
main: James warns believers not to show favoritism, contrasting the warm welcome often given to a rich man in fine clothes with the dismissive treatment of a poor man in shabby clothes. Such partiality makes them judges with evil motives, especially since God has chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of His kingdom, while the rich were often the ones oppressing them. If they keep the royal law, "love your neighbor as yourself," they do well, but favoritism is sin, and breaking one part of the law makes a person guilty of breaking it all. They should speak and act as those who will be judged by the law that gives freedom, for judgment without mercy will be shown to those who have shown no mercy, but mercy triumphs over judgment. Then James asks what good it is if someone claims to have faith but has no deeds. Wishing a hungry, poorly clothed brother or sister well without meeting their needs is useless; faith without works is dead. Even demons believe there is one God and shudder. Abraham's faith was shown complete when he offered Isaac, and Rahab's faith was shown when she protected the spies. As the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead.

## Chapter Background and Story

James 2 contains two closely connected sections. The first (vv1–13) addresses favoritism, especially the tendency to honor the rich and dishonor the poor in the Christian gathering. The second (vv14–26) addresses the relationship between faith and works, insisting that genuine faith always shows itself in action.

Both sections apply the principle of James 1:22: be doers of the word, not hearers only. Faith in "our glorious Lord Jesus Christ" (v1) must shape how believers treat others, especially the poor, and must produce real deeds of love.

The second section has been the subject of much discussion because it seems at first to contradict Paul's teaching that a person is justified by faith apart from works (Romans 3:28; Galatians 2:16). The commentary below explains how James and Paul fit together. Paul is answering people who thought they could earn God's acceptance through religious works. James is answering people who thought that mere belief, without any change of life, was enough. Both agree that true, saving faith is a living trust in Christ that produces a changed life.

### Do Not Show Favoritism (2:1–4)

The warning against favoritism is studied below.

### God's Choice of the Poor (2:5–7)

[[v5|Listen, my beloved brothers. Didn’t God choose those who are poor in this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the Kingdom which he promised to those who love him?]] God often chooses those the world overlooks. Jesus said, [[Luke 6:20|Blessed are you who are poor, for God's Kingdom is yours]]. Paul noted that not many of the Corinthian believers were wise, powerful, or of noble birth (1 Corinthians 1:26–29). This does not mean that poverty earns salvation or that the rich cannot be saved, but that God's grace often finds a warmer welcome among those who know their need.

[[v6|But you have dishonored the poor man. Don’t the rich oppress you and personally drag you before the courts?]] [[v7|Don’t they blaspheme the honorable name by which you are called?]] James points out the irony. The very people the believers were flattering, the wealthy, were often the ones exploiting them, taking them to court, and mocking the name of Christ. "The honorable name by which you are called" may refer to the name of Christ invoked over believers at baptism.

### The Royal Law (2:8–13)

[[v8|However, if you fulfill the royal law according to the Scripture, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself,” you do well]]. James quotes Leviticus 19:18. He calls it "the royal law," perhaps because it is the supreme law, the law of the King, or because Jesus, the King, called it one of the two greatest commandments (Mark 12:31).

[[v9|But if you show partiality, you commit sin, being convicted by the law as transgressors]]. Favoritism is not a small matter. It violates the command to love one's neighbor. Leviticus 19:15, just a few verses before the command to love one's neighbor, forbids showing partiality to the poor or favoring the great.

[[v10|For whoever keeps the whole law, and yet stumbles in one point, he has become guilty of all]]. [[v11|For he who said, “Do not commit adultery,” also said, “Do not commit murder.” Now if you do not commit adultery but do murder, you have become a transgressor of the law]]. God's law is a unity, because it comes from one Lawgiver. Breaking any part of it is breaking God's law. People cannot excuse favoritism by pointing to other commandments they keep.

[[v12|So speak and so do as men who are to be judged by the law of freedom]]. "The law of freedom" (see James 1:25) is God's law as fulfilled in Christ and written on believers' hearts. Believers will be evaluated by it. The principle of mercy is studied below.

### Faith Without Works Is Dead (2:14–20)

James's challenge about faith that does nothing is studied below. [[v18|Yes, a man will say, “You have faith, and I have works.” Show me your faith without works, and I will show you my faith by my works]]. James imagines someone objecting that faith and works are separate gifts: some have one, some the other. James answers that faith cannot be seen without works. Works are the visible evidence of invisible faith.

[[v19|You believe that God is one. You do well. The demons also believe—and shudder]]. Belief that there is one God was the heart of Jewish faith (Deuteronomy 6:4). But mere intellectual agreement is not saving faith. Even demons know that God exists, and they tremble in fear. Their belief does not lead to love, trust, or obedience.

[[v20|But do you want to know, vain man, that faith apart from works is dead?]] "Vain" means empty or foolish. Faith that produces nothing is useless.

### Abraham and Rahab (2:21–26)

The examples of Abraham and Rahab are studied below. [[v25|In the same way, wasn’t Rahab the prostitute also justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out another way?]] Rahab, a Canaanite woman with a sinful past, showed her faith in Israel's God by protecting the Israelite spies at great risk to herself (Joshua 2). James pairs her with Abraham, the revered patriarch. Faith that acts is found in the most unlikely people. Hebrews 11:31 also commends Rahab's faith.

[[v26|For as the body apart from the spirit is dead, even so faith apart from works is dead]]. A body without breath is a corpse. Faith without works is equally lifeless. Works are the sign of life in faith.

## Important Verses Explained

### James 2:1–4 — Do Not Show Favoritism

verse: James 2:1-4

#### Simple Explanation

"My brothers, don’t hold the faith of our glorious Lord Jesus Christ with partiality." Believers follow "our glorious Lord Jesus Christ." His glory outshines all human status. To show favoritism based on wealth or appearance is inconsistent with faith in Him.

James then describes a scene. "For if a man with a gold ring, in fine clothing, comes into your synagogue." The word "synagogue" here means "assembly" or "gathering." Jewish believers may have used this word for their church gatherings. A wealthy visitor arrives, wearing gold rings and expensive clothes. "And a poor man in filthy clothing also comes in." A poor visitor arrives in shabby, dirty clothes.

Verse 3: "and you pay special attention to him who wears the fine clothing and say, 'Sit here in a good place;' and you tell the poor man, 'Stand there,' or 'Sit by my footstool.'" The rich man is given a seat of honor; the poor man is told to stand or to sit on the floor.

Verse 4: "haven't you shown partiality among yourselves, and become judges with evil thoughts?" By treating people differently based on their wealth, believers are acting as judges with corrupt motives, evaluating people by worldly standards.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Favoritism contradicts the gospel. God does not show partiality (Deuteronomy 10:17; Acts 10:34; Romans 2:11). Jesus welcomed the poor, the sick, and the outcast. He Himself became poor for our sake (2 Corinthians 8:9). A church that honors the rich and dishonors the poor reflects the world's values, not God's.

The phrase "our glorious Lord Jesus Christ" is significant. In the presence of Christ's glory, all human status fades. The richest person and the poorest person stand equally in need of His grace. Gold rings and fine clothing mean nothing compared with His glory.

Favoritism can take many forms beyond wealth: race, education, social status, appearance, age, nationality, or any characteristic that leads people to treat others unequally. All of these are contrary to the gospel.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Churches can examine whether they show favoritism, giving more attention, honor, or influence to those who are wealthy, attractive, educated, or socially prominent. Every person who walks through the door should receive the same warm welcome.

Individuals can also examine their own attitudes. Do they pay more attention to people who can benefit them? Do they overlook those who seem unimportant? Following Christ means treating everyone with dignity and respect.

This passage also challenges believers to notice those who are often ignored: the poor, the lonely, those with disabilities, newcomers, and those who do not fit in. Welcoming them reflects the heart of God.

### James 2:13 — Mercy Triumphs over Judgment

verse: James 2:13

#### Simple Explanation

"For judgment is without mercy to him who has shown no mercy." Those who show no mercy to others will receive no mercy in judgment. The way people treat others reveals their hearts and affects how they will be judged.

"Mercy triumphs over judgment." Mercy wins. God's mercy is greater than His judgment for those who show mercy. When believers show mercy, it reflects the mercy they have received, and that mercy will prevail when they stand before God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse echoes Jesus' teaching. He said, [[Matthew 5:7|Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy]]. In the parable of the unforgiving servant, a man forgiven a huge debt refused to forgive a small one, and he faced judgment as a result (Matthew 18:21–35). Jesus taught His disciples to pray, [[Matthew 6:12|Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors]].

This does not mean that people earn God's mercy by being merciful. Mercy is always a gift. But those who have truly received God's mercy will show mercy to others. A merciless heart reveals that a person has not truly grasped God's grace.

"Mercy triumphs over judgment" is one of the most hopeful phrases in Scripture. It reveals God's heart. He delights in mercy (Micah 7:18). The cross is the ultimate place where mercy triumphed over judgment, as Christ bore the judgment so that mercy could be extended to sinners.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can ask whether they are quick to show mercy or quick to judge. Do they give others the same grace God has given them?

Showing mercy might mean forgiving someone who has hurt them, helping someone in need, overlooking a minor offense, or responding to failure with compassion rather than condemnation. Mercy does not mean ignoring harm or excusing abuse; protecting people and seeking justice can be acts of mercy too. But mercy shapes the heart with which believers respond.

This verse also brings comfort. Believers who know they have fallen short can trust that God's mercy triumphs over judgment. His mercy is greater than their sin.

### James 2:14–17 — Faith Without Works Is Dead

verse: James 2:14-17

#### Simple Explanation

"What good is it, my brothers, if a man says he has faith, but has no works? Can faith save him?" Notice the word "says." James is speaking of someone who claims to have faith. The question is whether that kind of faith, faith that produces no works, can save. The implied answer is no.

James gives an illustration. Verse 15: "And if a brother or sister is naked and in lack of daily food." A fellow believer lacks basic clothing and food.

Verse 16: "and one of you tells them, 'Go in peace. Be warmed and filled;' yet you didn’t give them the things the body needs, what good is it?" Saying kind words without offering practical help is empty. "Go in peace" was a common blessing, but in this case it is hollow.

Verse 17: "Even so faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself." Just as words of blessing without action are useless, faith without works is dead. It has no life in it.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

James is not saying that works earn salvation. He is saying that genuine faith always produces works. Faith that does not change how a person lives is not living faith; it is dead.

How does James relate to Paul? Paul teaches that people are justified by faith, not by works of the law (Romans 3:28; Galatians 2:16). He is addressing those who tried to earn God's favor through religious observance. James teaches that faith without works is dead. He is addressing those who claimed to have faith but whose lives showed no evidence of it. Paul also insists that faith produces good works. He speaks of [[Galatians 5:6|faith working through love]] and says believers are created in Christ Jesus [[Ephesians 2:10|for good works]]. James and Paul are not contradicting each other; they are addressing different errors and agree on the same truth: people are saved by faith alone, but the faith that saves is never alone.

Christian traditions have expressed this relationship in different ways. Some emphasize that justification is by faith alone, with good works as its necessary fruit. Others emphasize that faith and works together characterize the life of the justified. All agree that a faith that never acts in love is not the living faith Scripture describes.

James's example is pointed. Believers who see a brother or sister in need and do nothing reveal that their faith is not genuine. John says the same: [[1 John 3:17|But whoever has the world's goods and sees his brother in need, then closes his heart of compassion against him, how does God's love remain in him?]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can ask whether their faith is visible in action. Does their faith lead them to care for those in need, to serve others, to obey God's word?

This passage especially challenges believers to respond to practical needs. Kind words and prayers are good, but when someone lacks food, clothing, or shelter, love requires action. Churches and individuals can look for ways to meet real needs in their communities.

At the same time, believers should not be crushed by this passage, as if they must prove their salvation through constant activity. Works are the fruit of faith, not its root. As faith grows, good works naturally follow.

### James 2:21–24 — Abraham's Faith Made Complete

verse: James 2:21-24

#### Simple Explanation

James uses Abraham as an example. "Wasn't Abraham our father justified by works, in that he offered up Isaac his son on the altar?" When God tested Abraham by asking him to offer Isaac, Abraham obeyed (Genesis 22). His action demonstrated his faith.

Verse 22: "You see that faith worked with his works, and by works faith was perfected." Abraham's faith and works cooperated. His faith produced his actions, and his actions completed, or brought to maturity, his faith.

Verse 23: "So the Scripture was fulfilled which says, 'Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him as righteousness,' and he was called the friend of God." James quotes Genesis 15:6, which describes God counting Abraham as righteous because he believed. That happened many years before the offering of Isaac. When Abraham offered Isaac, he showed that his earlier faith was genuine, and the Scripture was "fulfilled," confirmed and displayed. Abraham was called "the friend of God" (2 Chronicles 20:7; Isaiah 41:8).

Verse 24: "You see then that by works a man is justified, and not only by faith." This is the verse that seems to contradict Paul. But James uses "justified" in a different sense. For Paul, "justified" means declared righteous before God, which happens by faith. For James, in this context, "justified" means shown to be righteous, vindicated, demonstrated as genuine. Abraham was declared righteous by faith (Genesis 15:6), and his righteousness was shown by his works (Genesis 22). The faith that is "only" faith, mere belief without action, is the dead faith James has been describing.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul and James both use Abraham as an example, but they focus on different moments in his life. Paul focuses on Genesis 15, where Abraham believed and was counted righteous before he had done any works and before he was circumcised (Romans 4:1–12). This shows that justification is by faith. James focuses on Genesis 22, where Abraham's faith was proven by his obedience. This shows that genuine faith produces works.

Both are true. Abraham was made right with God by faith. And his faith was the kind that obeyed God at great cost. Hebrews 11:17–19 also describes Abraham's offering of Isaac as an act of faith.

The phrase "faith was perfected" means faith reached its goal, its full expression. Faith is not complete until it acts. Like a seed that grows into a tree, faith grows into obedience.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can see their acts of obedience not as ways to earn God's favor but as expressions of their trust in Him. Each step of obedience shows that their faith is real and helps it grow.

This passage also encourages believers who face difficult acts of obedience. Abraham's test was agonizing, but his faith carried him through. God does not ask believers to do anything He will not give them strength to do.

Finally, believers can take comfort that, like Abraham, they can be called friends of God. Jesus said to His disciples, [[John 15:15|I have called you friends]]. Faith that trusts and obeys brings believers into friendship with God.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

James 2 shows that faith in "our glorious Lord Jesus Christ" must shape how believers treat others. Favoritism, honoring the rich and dishonoring the poor, contradicts the gospel. God has chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of His kingdom. The royal law, "love your neighbor as yourself," calls believers to treat everyone with equal dignity, regardless of wealth, status, appearance, or background.

James reminds believers that God's law is a unity and that favoritism is a real sin. Those who show no mercy will receive judgment without mercy, but mercy triumphs over judgment. Believers who have received God's mercy are called to extend it to others.

The second half of the chapter teaches that genuine faith always produces works. Faith that claims to believe but does nothing for a brother or sister in need is dead. Even demons believe there is one God, but their belief does not save them. True faith acts, as Abraham's did when he offered Isaac and as Rahab's did when she protected the spies.

James and Paul agree: people are made right with God by faith, not by earning it through works; and the faith that saves is a living faith that shows itself in love and obedience. Believers can let their faith come alive in practical ways, welcoming all people, showing mercy, meeting real needs, and obeying God, knowing that such works are the natural fruit of a heart that trusts Him.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are glorious, and in Your presence all human status fades. Forgive me for the times I have shown favoritism, honoring some and overlooking others. Help me to see every person as You see them and to welcome the poor, the overlooked, and the outsider with the same love I would show anyone else. Teach me to keep the royal law, loving my neighbor as myself. Thank You that mercy triumphs over judgment. Make me merciful, as You have been merciful to me. Let my faith be alive, not dead. Give me eyes to see the needs around me and a heart to meet them, not only with words but with action. Like Abraham, help me to trust You enough to obey, even when it is costly. Make my faith complete in loving works, for Your glory. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
