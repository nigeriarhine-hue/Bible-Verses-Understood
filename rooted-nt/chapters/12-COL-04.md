# Colossians 4
theme: Devoted to Prayer and Gracious in Speech
main: Paul completes his household teaching by calling masters to treat their servants justly and fairly, remembering that they too have a Master in heaven. He urges the Colossians to devote themselves to prayer with watchfulness and thanksgiving, asking God to open doors for the gospel. They are to act wisely toward outsiders, making the most of every opportunity, and to let their speech always be gracious and seasoned with salt. Paul closes with greetings from a remarkable group of coworkers, including Tychicus, Onesimus, Aristarchus, Mark, Luke, and Epaphras, who wrestles in prayer for the Colossians to stand mature and fully assured in God's will. He sends greetings to Laodicea, gives a charge to Archippus, and signs the letter in his own hand, asking them to remember his chains.

## Chapter Background and Story

The final chapter of Colossians moves from instructions about relationships inside the Christian household to relationships with the wider world, and then to personal greetings that reveal the network of believers who worked alongside Paul.

The list of names at the end is easy to skip, but it is full of stories. Several of these people appear elsewhere in the New Testament. Onesimus is the runaway slave whose story is told in the letter to Philemon, written at the same time. Mark is the young man who once abandoned Paul and Barnabas but was later restored to ministry. Luke is the physician who wrote the Gospel of Luke and Acts. Demas would later desert Paul (2 Timothy 4:10). These names remind readers that the gospel spread through real people with real histories, strengths, and failures.

### Tychicus and Onesimus (4:7–9)

[[v7|All my affairs will be made known to you by Tychicus, the beloved brother, faithful servant, and fellow bondservant in the Lord]]. Tychicus also carried the letter to the Ephesians (Ephesians 6:21–22). [[v8|I am sending him to you for this very purpose, that he may know your circumstances and comfort your hearts]].

[[v9|together with Onesimus, the faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you. They will make known to you everything that is going on here]]. Onesimus was a slave who had run away from his master, Philemon, a member of the Colossian church. While away, he met Paul and became a believer. Now Paul was sending him back, carrying the letter to Philemon in which Paul asked Philemon to receive him [[Philemon 1:16|no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother]]. Notice how Paul describes Onesimus here: not as a runaway, but as "the faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you." The gospel had transformed both his life and his standing.

### Greetings from Paul's Companions (4:10–14)

[[v10|Aristarchus, my fellow prisoner, greets you, and Mark the cousin of Barnabas (concerning whom you received instructions, “if he comes to you, receive him”)]]. Aristarchus was from Thessalonica and had traveled with Paul through dangerous times, including the riot in Ephesus and the voyage to Rome (Acts 19:29; 27:2). Mark had once left Paul and Barnabas partway through their first missionary journey (Acts 13:13). Paul later refused to take him on the second journey, which led to a sharp disagreement with Barnabas (Acts 15:37–39). Now Mark is with Paul again and commended to the churches. Later, Paul would write that Mark was [[2 Timothy 4:11|useful to me for service]]. Mark's story is one of failure followed by restoration.

[[v11|and Jesus who is called Justus. These are my only fellow workers for God’s Kingdom who are of the circumcision, men who have been a comfort to me]]. "Jesus" was a common Jewish name (the Greek form of Joshua). These three were Jewish believers who worked with Paul. Their loyalty was a great comfort to him.

Epaphras's prayer ministry is noted in verses 12–13 and reflected in the study of prayer below. [[v13|For I testify about him that he has great zeal for you, and for those in Laodicea, and for those in Hierapolis]]. These three cities were close neighbors in the Lycus valley.

[[v14|Luke the beloved physician and Demas greet you]]. This verse tells us that Luke was a doctor. Since he is not among those "of the circumcision" (v11), he was likely a Gentile, making him the only Gentile author of a New Testament book. Demas is mentioned without comment. Sadly, he would later abandon Paul, [[2 Timothy 4:10|having loved this present world]].

### Greetings and Instructions (4:15–18)

[[v15|Greet the brothers who are in Laodicea, with Nymphas and the assembly that is in his house]]. Early churches met in homes. Some ancient manuscripts read "Nympha" and "her house," indicating a woman who hosted a church, much as Lydia did in Philippi (Acts 16:15, 40). Women who hosted churches in their homes likely played significant roles in their communities.

[[v16|When this letter has been read among you, cause it to be read also in the assembly of the Laodiceans, and that you also read the letter from Laodicea]]. Paul's letters were meant to be read aloud in church gatherings and shared with other churches. "The letter from Laodicea" may be a letter Paul wrote to Laodicea that has not survived, or it may be the letter we know as Ephesians, if that letter circulated among the churches of the region.

[[v17|Tell Archippus, “Take heed to the ministry which you have received in the Lord, that you fulfill it.”]] Archippus is also mentioned in Philemon 1:2, perhaps as a member of Philemon's household. He had received a ministry from the Lord, and Paul publicly encourages him to complete it.

[[v18|I, Paul, write this greeting with my own hand. Remember my chains. Grace be with you. Amen]]. Paul takes the pen from his secretary and signs the letter himself. As he writes, his hand may have been weighed down by the chain. "Remember my chains" is both a request for prayer and a reminder of the cost of the gospel. And his final word, as always, is grace.

## Important Verses Explained

### Colossians 4:1 — Masters, Treat Others Justly

verse: Colossians 4:1

#### Simple Explanation

This verse completes Paul's teaching on households, which began in Colossians 3:18. Having addressed servants, Paul now addresses masters. "Masters, give to your servants that which is just and equal."

"Just" means right and fair. "Equal" can mean fair or equitable, and it may even suggest treating servants as equals in some sense. In the Roman world, slaves had almost no legal rights, and masters could treat them however they wished. Paul places a moral obligation on masters to treat their servants with justice and fairness.

The reason: "knowing that you also have a Master in heaven." Masters were not ultimately in charge. They too were servants of a higher Master, the Lord Jesus Christ. They would answer to Him for how they treated those under their authority. This places masters and servants on the same level before God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows how the gospel begins to undermine unjust structures from within. Paul did not have the power to abolish Roman slavery, but he set principles in motion that would challenge it. If masters must treat slaves justly and fairly, if both are brothers in Christ (Philemon 1:16), if in Christ there is neither slave nor free (Colossians 3:11), then the very foundations of slavery are called into question. Over centuries, Christians drew on these truths in the fight to end slavery, even as others tragically misused Scripture to defend it.

The principle behind this verse applies to all relationships of power. Those who have authority, whether employers, managers, government officials, parents, teachers, or church leaders, are accountable to God for how they use it. God sees, and He judges impartially (Colossians 3:25). Authority is a stewardship, not a license for exploitation.

This verse also reflects the character of God, who is just and who defends the oppressed. The Old Testament repeatedly commands fair treatment of workers, such as paying wages promptly (Deuteronomy 24:14–15), and the prophets condemned those who exploited laborers (Jeremiah 22:13). James warns that the wages withheld from workers cry out to God (James 5:4).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Employers, supervisors, and anyone with authority over others can ask whether they are treating people "justly and fairly." This includes fair wages, safe working conditions, honest communication, respect, and freedom from abuse or harassment.

This verse also offers hope to those who are treated unjustly. God sees, and every person with power answers to a Master in heaven. Believers who face unfair treatment at work may pursue justice through appropriate means, trusting that God cares about their situation.

Finally, this verse reminds all believers that whatever authority they hold, they hold it under Christ. Remembering that they too have a Master can keep them humble, fair, and kind.

### Colossians 4:2–4 — Continue Steadfastly in Prayer

verse: Colossians 4:2-4

#### Simple Explanation

"Continue steadfastly in prayer." The word means to persist, to be devoted to, to hold fast. Prayer is not an occasional activity but a constant commitment.

"Watching in it with thanksgiving." "Watching" means being alert and attentive, like a guard on duty. Jesus told His disciples to watch and pray (Mark 14:38). Believers are to pray with their eyes open, aware of spiritual realities and of the needs around them. And their prayers are to be filled with thanksgiving, as thankfulness runs throughout Colossians.

Verse 3: "praying together for us also, that God may open to us a door for the word, to speak the mystery of Christ, for which I am also in bonds." Paul asks for prayer, but not for release from prison. He asks for open doors to share the gospel. "The mystery of Christ" is the gospel now revealed, that Christ dwells in His people, including Gentiles (Colossians 1:27). Paul was in chains because he proclaimed it.

Verse 4: "that I may reveal it as I ought to speak." Paul asks for clarity, to be able to explain the gospel well.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul's request shows his priorities. Though in chains, his greatest concern was the spread of the gospel. He believed that God opens doors for the message, and that prayer is part of how those doors open. Elsewhere he speaks of "a great and effective door" opened for him (1 Corinthians 16:9) and of a door opened by the Lord (2 Corinthians 2:12).

The combination of persistence, watchfulness, and thanksgiving forms a healthy pattern for prayer. Persistence keeps believers praying even when answers are slow. Watchfulness keeps them spiritually alert. Thanksgiving keeps them aware of God's goodness and guards against discouragement.

Paul's humility is also evident. He, the great apostle, asks the Colossians to pray for him. Every believer, no matter how mature or gifted, needs the prayers of others. Prayer binds believers together in a shared mission.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to make prayer a regular, devoted part of life, not only in times of crisis. Setting aside time each day for prayer, praying throughout the day, and joining with others in prayer all help build a steadfast prayer life.

Believers can also pray specifically for open doors for the gospel: for opportunities to share their faith with friends, neighbors, and coworkers, and for missionaries and church leaders around the world. They can pray for clarity and boldness for those who teach and preach.

Thanksgiving can transform prayer. Beginning or ending prayer by thanking God for specific blessings keeps the heart grateful and hopeful.

### Colossians 4:5–6 — Wisdom and Gracious Speech Toward Outsiders

verse: Colossians 4:5-6

#### Simple Explanation

Paul turns to how believers should relate to people outside the church. "Walk in wisdom toward those who are outside." Believers should live wisely in front of those who do not share their faith, aware that their conduct reflects on Christ.

"Redeeming the time." As in Ephesians 5:16, this means making the most of every opportunity. Believers should be alert to chances to show kindness, demonstrate integrity, and share their faith.

Verse 6: "Let your speech always be with grace, seasoned with salt." "With grace" means gracious, kind, pleasant, and filled with God's grace. "Seasoned with salt" means flavorful, interesting, and wholesome. In the ancient world, salt preserved food and enhanced its taste. Believers' words should be engaging and beneficial, not bland, harsh, or corrupt.

"That you may know how you ought to answer each one." Every person is different, with different questions, needs, and backgrounds. Gracious, thoughtful speech allows believers to respond appropriately to each individual.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses describe a winsome, thoughtful witness. Paul does not call believers to aggressive or argumentative evangelism, but to wise living and gracious speech. First Peter 3:15–16 gives similar counsel: always be ready to give an answer to anyone who asks about the hope within, but do so with gentleness and respect.

Jesus modeled this kind of engagement. He spoke differently to different people: gently to the woman at the well (John 4), boldly to the religious leaders, compassionately to the grieving, and thoughtfully to seekers like Nicodemus (John 3). He knew [[v6|how you ought to answer each one]].

The image of salt also connects with Jesus' teaching that His followers are [[Matthew 5:13|the salt of the earth]]. Their presence and words should preserve what is good and add the flavor of God's kingdom to the world around them.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers interact with people outside the church every day: at work, at school, in their neighborhoods, and online. These verses invite them to consider how their conduct and words reflect Christ. Are they honest, kind, and trustworthy? Do their words build up or tear down?

Online conversations especially can become harsh and divisive. Gracious speech means engaging respectfully even with those who disagree, listening well, and speaking truth with kindness.

Believers can also prepare to share their faith thoughtfully. That might mean learning to explain the gospel clearly, listening carefully to others' questions, and responding with humility rather than having a rehearsed speech for everyone. Wisdom and grace open doors that arguments often close.

### Colossians 4:12 — Wrestling in Prayer

verse: Colossians 4:12

#### Simple Explanation

Paul sends greetings from Epaphras, the founder of the Colossian church. "Epaphras, who is one of you, a servant of Christ." Epaphras was from Colossae. Paul calls him a servant, or slave, of Christ, a title Paul usually used for himself and Timothy.

"Always striving for you in his prayers." The word for "striving" comes from athletic contests and suggests intense effort, like a wrestler in a match. It is related to the English word "agonize." Epaphras was not praying casually. He prayed with deep effort and earnestness.

What did he pray for? "That you may stand perfect and complete in all the will of God." "Perfect" means mature. "Complete" means fully assured, convinced. Epaphras prayed that the Colossians would stand firm, grow to maturity, and be fully confident in God's will, not swayed by false teaching.

Verse 13 adds that Epaphras had great zeal and concern not only for the Colossians but also for the believers in Laodicea and Hierapolis.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Epaphras is a model of pastoral prayer. Separated from his church by distance and perhaps by imprisonment (he is called Paul's "fellow prisoner" in Philemon 1:23), he could not be present with them. But he could pray. And he prayed with intensity, persistence, and love.

His prayer echoes Paul's prayers in this letter: that the Colossians would be filled with the knowledge of God's will (Colossians 1:9), grow to maturity in Christ (1:28), and be established in the faith (2:7). Epaphras shared Paul's goal for the church.

This verse shows that prayer is real work. Jesus Himself prayed with such intensity in Gethsemane that His sweat became like drops of blood (Luke 22:44). Prayer can be costly. It requires time, focus, and love. But it is one of the most powerful ways to serve others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many believers cannot be physically present with those they love: children who have moved away, friends in distant places, missionaries overseas, or believers in persecuted churches. Epaphras's example shows that prayer bridges that distance. Believers can wrestle in prayer for those they love, asking God to help them stand firm and grow mature.

This verse also challenges believers to deepen their prayer lives. Prayer does not always feel easy or natural. Sometimes it requires effort and perseverance. Setting aside time, using Scripture as a guide, and praying for specific people and needs can help.

Finally, Epaphras's prayers for the believers in Laodicea and Hierapolis show a heart that reached beyond his own congregation. Believers can pray not only for their own church but for other churches and believers in their region and around the world.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Colossians 4 brings the letter to a close with practical wisdom and a glimpse into the community that surrounded Paul. It begins by completing the household teaching: those with authority must treat others justly and fairly, remembering that they too answer to a Master in heaven. This principle applies to every relationship of power today, from the workplace to the home to the church. Authority is a stewardship, and God sees how it is used.

The chapter then calls believers to a life of steadfast prayer, marked by watchfulness and thanksgiving. Paul, though in chains, asked not for release but for open doors to share the gospel and for clarity to speak it well. Believers can pray the same way for themselves and for others who carry the gospel.

Paul also teaches believers how to relate to those outside the church: with wisdom in their conduct, making the most of every opportunity, and with speech that is gracious and seasoned with salt. A thoughtful, kind, engaging witness opens hearts more effectively than harsh argument.

Finally, the list of names at the end reminds believers that the gospel advances through ordinary people. Onesimus, a runaway slave, became a beloved brother. Mark, who once failed, was restored to useful service. Luke, a doctor, became a faithful companion and writer. Epaphras wrestled in prayer for his church. Demas, sadly, would later fall away. Each name is a reminder that God works through imperfect people, that failure need not be final, and that faithfulness to the end matters. Through it all, the final word is grace.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord, You are the Master over all. Help me to treat everyone under my care or authority with justice and fairness, remembering that I answer to You. Make me devoted to prayer, watchful and thankful. Open doors for the gospel in my life and in the lives of those who serve You around the world, and give clarity and boldness to all who proclaim Your word. Help me to walk wisely toward those who do not yet know You, making the most of every opportunity. Let my speech always be gracious and seasoned with salt, so that I may know how to answer each person. Thank You for the faithful believers who have gone before me and for the way You restore those who have failed. Teach me to wrestle in prayer for others, that they may stand mature and fully assured in Your will. Let Your grace be with me always. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
