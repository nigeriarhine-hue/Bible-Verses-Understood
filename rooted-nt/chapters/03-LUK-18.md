# Luke 18
theme: Persistent Prayer and Humble Hearts
main: Jesus tells a parable about a widow who keeps pleading with an unjust judge, teaching His followers always to pray and not give up, because God will bring justice to His people. Through the parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector, He shows that God accepts the humble who plead for mercy, not the proud who trust in their own righteousness. He welcomes little children, calls a rich ruler to give up his wealth, predicts His death again, and gives sight to a blind man who cries out for mercy.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 18 continues Jesus' journey to Jerusalem and brings it near its goal. By the end of the chapter, Jesus is approaching Jericho, the last major town before the climb to Jerusalem. The chapter opens with two parables found only in Luke, both about prayer and both concerning people's attitudes toward God. It then includes events also recorded in Matthew and Mark: Jesus blessing children, the rich ruler, the third prediction of His death, and the healing of a blind man. (These are studied in depth at Mark 10:15, Mark 10:21, Matthew 19:26, Matthew 20:28, and Mark 10:50.)

The chapter follows Jesus' teaching at the end of Luke 17 about the sudden coming of the Son of Man. His followers would face a long wait, often in difficult circumstances, and they would need persistent prayer and humble faith.

### The Persistent Widow and the Unjust Judge (18:1–8)

Luke explains the purpose of this parable before Jesus tells it: [[v1|that they must always pray and not give up]]. This is studied below.

In a certain town there was a judge who [[v2|didn’t fear God and didn’t respect man]]. In the Old Testament, judges were commanded to judge fairly, to fear God, and to protect the vulnerable (Deuteronomy 16:18–20; 2 Chronicles 19:6–7). This judge did none of that. He was corrupt and callous.

A widow in that town kept coming to him, saying, [[v3|Defend me from my adversary!]] Widows were among the most vulnerable people in ancient society. With no husband to support or defend them, they were easily exploited. The law of Moses repeatedly commanded care and justice for widows (Exodus 22:22–24). This widow had no money for bribes and no powerful connections. All she had was persistence.

For a while the judge refused. But eventually he said to himself, [[v4|Though I neither fear God nor respect man]], [[v5|yet because this widow bothers me, I will defend her, or else she will wear me out by her continual coming]]. The phrase "wear me out" can literally mean "give me a black eye," a humorous exaggeration suggesting the judge felt worn down, as if beaten, by her constant appeals.

Jesus then drew the lesson, studied below. If even an unjust judge would grant justice to a persistent widow, how much more will God, who is perfectly just and loving, respond to His people who cry out to Him? Then Jesus asked a probing question: [[v8|Nevertheless, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on the earth?]]

### The Pharisee and the Tax Collector (18:9–14)

Jesus told another parable [[v9|to certain people who were convinced of their own righteousness, and who despised all others]]. Two men went up to the temple to pray. People often went to the temple at the times of the morning and afternoon sacrifices to pray. One was a Pharisee, a respected religious man known for careful observance of the law. The other was a tax collector, despised as a collaborator with Rome and often dishonest.

The Pharisee stood and prayed [[v11|by himself]], which may mean he prayed silently about himself, or stood apart from others. His prayer began with thanks to God but quickly turned to self-praise: he was not like other people, robbers, evildoers, adulterers, or even this tax collector. He listed his religious achievements: [[v12|I fast twice a week. I give tithes of all that I get]]. The law required only one annual fast, on the Day of Atonement, but devout Pharisees fasted twice a week. The law required tithing certain produce, but he tithed everything. He went beyond what was required.

Yet his prayer contained no request, no confession, and no real need for God. He was comparing himself to others and congratulating himself.

The tax collector stood at a distance. He would not even look up to heaven, but beat his chest, a sign of deep grief and repentance, and prayed, [[v13|God, be merciful to me, a sinner!]] This prayer is studied below. Jesus declared that this man, not the Pharisee, went home [[v14|justified]], declared righteous before God. [[v14|for everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but he who humbles himself will be exalted]].

### Jesus Blesses the Children (18:15–17)

People were bringing even their babies to Jesus for Him to touch them. Luke uses a word for infants. The disciples rebuked the people, thinking Jesus should not be bothered. But Jesus called the children to Him and said, [[v16|Allow the little children to come to me, and don’t hinder them, for God’s Kingdom belongs to such as these]]. Whoever does not receive God's kingdom like a little child will never enter it. Placed right after the parable of the tax collector, this scene reinforces the lesson: the kingdom belongs to the humble and dependent, not the self-sufficient. (See Mark 10:15.)

### The Rich Ruler (18:18–30)

A certain ruler asked Jesus, [[v18|Good Teacher, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?]] Jesus questioned why he called Him good, since only God is good, and listed several commandments. The ruler said he had kept them all since his youth. Jesus said, [[v22|You still lack one thing. Sell all that you have and distribute it to the poor. Then you will have treasure in heaven; then come, follow me]]. The man became very sad, because he was very rich.

The contrast with the previous scenes is clear. The tax collector came with nothing but a plea for mercy. Little children came with nothing to offer. The rich ruler came with his record of obedience and his wealth, and he could not let go of them. Jesus said how hard it is for the rich to enter God's kingdom, harder than for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle. When the listeners asked who could be saved, Jesus answered, [[v27|The things which are impossible with men are possible with God]]. He promised that anyone who has left home or family for God's kingdom will receive much more in this age and eternal life in the age to come. (See Matthew 19:21–26 and Mark 10:21.)

### Third Prediction of the Passion (18:31–34)

Jesus took the Twelve aside and told them that they were going up to Jerusalem, where everything written by the prophets about the Son of Man would be fulfilled. He would be handed over to the Gentiles, mocked, insulted, spit on, flogged, and killed, and on the third day He would rise again. Luke emphasizes the disciples' lack of understanding three times in one verse: [[v34|They understood none of these things. This saying was hidden from them, and they didn’t understand the things that were said]]. Only after the resurrection would Jesus open their minds to understand the Scriptures (Luke 24:45).

### The Blind Man Near Jericho (18:35–43)

As Jesus approached Jericho, a blind man was sitting by the road begging. Hearing the crowd, he asked what was happening and was told that Jesus of Nazareth was passing by. He called out, [[v38|Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!]] People told him to be quiet, but he shouted all the more. Jesus stopped and had him brought near, and asked, [[v41|What do you want me to do?]] He answered, [[v41|Lord, that I may see again]]. Jesus said, [[v42|Receive your sight. Your faith has healed you]]. Immediately he could see, and he followed Jesus, praising God, and all the people praised God too.

This man's prayer, "have mercy on me," echoes the tax collector's prayer, "be merciful to me." Both men came to God with nothing but their need, and both were received. The blind beggar saw who Jesus was, the Son of David, more clearly than the disciples, who did not understand what Jesus had just told them. (Mark's account, which names him Bartimaeus, is studied at Mark 10:50.)

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 18:1 — Always Pray and Do Not Give Up

verse: Luke 18:1

#### Simple Explanation

This verse introduces the parable of the persistent widow, and unusually, Luke tells readers the parable's purpose before it begins: [[v1|He also spoke a parable to them that they must always pray and not give up]].

The context is important. At the end of Luke 17, Jesus had been speaking about the coming of the Son of Man. He told His disciples that there would be days when they would long to see the Son of Man but would not see Him, and that His coming would be sudden. Between Jesus' first coming and His return, His followers would live in a world where injustice continued and where they would often suffer. They would be tempted to lose hope and stop praying.

[[v1|that they must always pray]]. "Always" does not mean that believers must be speaking prayers every moment, but that prayer should be a constant and persistent part of life, especially in times of difficulty. Paul expresses a similar idea: [[1 Thessalonians 5:17|pray without ceasing]].

[[v1|and not give up]]. The phrase means not to lose heart, not to become discouraged, not to grow weary. When prayers seem unanswered and justice seems delayed, it is tempting to stop praying. Jesus warns against this.

The parable that follows illustrates the point. A widow kept coming to an unjust judge asking for justice, and he finally gave in because of her persistence. If persistence works even with an unjust judge, how much more should believers persist in prayer to a just and loving God?

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that persistent prayer is an expression of faith. When believers keep praying, they demonstrate that they still trust God, even when they do not see answers. Giving up on prayer often means giving up hope that God will act.

It also shows that Jesus understands the difficulty of prayer. He knew His followers would face times when prayer seemed fruitless and they would be tempted to quit. He did not ignore this struggle; He addressed it directly with encouragement.

Finally, the verse connects prayer with the hope of Christ's return. Believers pray in a world that is not yet fully set right. They pray, as Jesus taught, "Your kingdom come." Persistent prayer keeps their hope alive until He returns.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers who feel discouraged in prayer. Perhaps they have prayed for a loved one's salvation, for healing, for justice, or for a change in circumstances, and nothing seems to happen. Jesus' words invite them to keep praying and not lose heart.

It also encourages believers to build habits of regular prayer that sustain them through difficult seasons. Prayer is not only for emergencies; it is an ongoing relationship with God. Setting aside regular times for prayer and bringing needs to God throughout the day can help believers pray "always."

Finally, this verse reminds believers that persistent prayer is not about manipulating God. God is not reluctant like the unjust judge. Persistence reflects faith, deepens dependence, and keeps believers connected to God while they wait for His answers.

### Luke 18:7–8 — Will God Not Bring Justice to His Chosen Ones?

verse: Luke 18:7-8

#### Simple Explanation

These verses are Jesus' application of the parable of the persistent widow. In the story, a judge who did not fear God or respect people refused to help a widow who kept asking for justice against her adversary. Eventually, he gave in, not because he cared about justice, but because she kept bothering him. Jesus said, [[v6|Listen to what the unrighteous judge says]]. Then He drew the lesson.

[[v7|Won’t God avenge his chosen ones who are crying out to him day and night]]. The argument moves from lesser to greater. If even an unjust judge would eventually give justice to a persistent widow he did not care about, how much more will God, who is perfectly just and who loves His people, bring justice to His chosen ones? God is not like the unjust judge; He is the opposite. The word "avenge" here means to vindicate or bring justice, to set things right for those who have been wronged.

"His chosen ones" refers to God's people, those He has called to belong to Him. They are not strangers to God, as the widow was to the judge. They are His beloved children.

[[v7|and yet he exercises patience with them?]] This phrase is difficult to translate. It may mean, "Will He delay long over them?", a question expecting the answer "no." Or it may refer to God's patience, His willingness to wait so that more people may repent (2 Peter 3:9).

[[v8|I tell you that he will avenge them quickly]]. God will act decisively. "Quickly" may mean that when God acts, He acts suddenly, or that from God's perspective, the wait is short, even when it feels long to His suffering people.

Then Jesus asked a sobering question: [[v8|Nevertheless, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on the earth?]] The real issue is not whether God will be faithful, but whether His people will remain faithful in prayer and trust while they wait.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal God's commitment to justice. God sees the oppression and suffering of His people, and He will set things right. Throughout Scripture, God is described as the defender of the widow, the orphan, and the oppressed (Psalm 68:5; 146:7–9). The cry for justice is not ignored.

They also acknowledge the reality of waiting. God's people cry out "day and night," which implies a long and painful wait. Revelation pictures martyrs crying out, [[Revelation 6:10|How long, Master, the holy and true, until you judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?]] God's timing often differs from ours, but His justice is certain.

Finally, Jesus' question about faith turns the focus to believers' responsibility. God will be faithful; the question is whether His people will keep trusting and praying until He comes. Persistent prayer is how faith endures through the long wait.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses offer comfort to those who suffer injustice. Whether facing persecution, mistreatment, abuse, or systemic injustice, believers can bring their cries to God, knowing that He hears and will act. They do not need to take revenge into their own hands; they can trust God's justice (Romans 12:19). This trust is compatible with seeking justice through appropriate channels, such as reporting wrongdoing and seeking help.

They also encourage believers to keep praying for justice in the world. Believers can pray for persecuted Christians, oppressed people, victims of violence, and those who have no one to defend them, trusting that God cares about these things.

Finally, Jesus' question invites self-examination: will He find faith in me? Believers can ask God to strengthen their faith so that they remain faithful in prayer and trust until Christ returns.

### Luke 18:13–14 — [[Luke 18:13|God, Be Merciful to Me, a Sinner]]

verse: Luke 18:13-14

#### Simple Explanation

These verses are the climax of the parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector. Jesus told this parable to people who trusted in their own righteousness and looked down on others. Two men went to the temple to pray. The Pharisee stood and prayed about himself, thanking God that he was not like other people, especially the tax collector, and listing his religious achievements: fasting twice a week and tithing everything he received.

[[v13|But the tax collector, standing far away]]. He stood at a distance, perhaps in the outer court, feeling unworthy to come close to God's presence. Tax collectors were despised as traitors and cheats, and this man seems to have known that he deserved that reputation.

[[v13|wouldn’t even lift up his eyes to heaven]]. People often prayed with eyes and hands lifted toward heaven. He could not bring himself to look up, overwhelmed by shame.

[[v13|but beat his chest]]. This was a gesture of intense grief and repentance, usually associated with mourning. The heart was considered the source of sin, and beating the chest expressed deep sorrow over one's own heart.

[[v13|saying, ‘God, be merciful to me, a sinner!’]] His prayer was short and simple. He did not list good deeds or compare himself with others. He confessed that he was a sinner and asked for mercy. The word for "be merciful" is related to the word for atonement; it means "be propitiated toward me" or "let your wrath be turned away from me." He was asking God to forgive him on the basis of mercy, possibly thinking of the sacrifice being offered in the temple at that hour. In Greek, the phrase is literally "the sinner," as if he thought of himself as the worst of all.

Then Jesus gave His verdict: [[v14|I tell you, this man went down to his house justified rather than the other]]. "Justified" means declared righteous before God, accepted by Him. The tax collector, who confessed his sin and asked for mercy, was accepted. The Pharisee, who trusted in his own righteousness, was not.

Jesus concluded with the principle: [[v14|for everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, but he who humbles himself will be exalted]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal the heart of the gospel: people are made right with God not by their own righteousness but by God's mercy. The Pharisee's religious achievements did not justify him, because he trusted in them rather than in God. The tax collector had nothing to offer except his need, and God justified him. Paul later explains this truth: [[Romans 3:24|being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus]].

They also expose the danger of self-righteousness and contempt for others. The Pharisee was not wrong to fast or tithe. His problem was that he trusted in these things and looked down on others. Pride blinds people to their own need for mercy.

Finally, the tax collector's prayer has been treasured throughout Christian history. In Eastern Orthodox tradition, it became the basis for the "Jesus Prayer": "Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner." It expresses the posture of every person who comes to God: humble, honest, and dependent on mercy.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to examine how they approach God. Do they come trusting in their good deeds, church attendance, or moral record? Or do they come humbly, acknowledging their need for mercy? God accepts those who come to Him honestly and humbly.

They also warn against comparing oneself to others. It is easy to feel spiritually superior to people who struggle with obvious sins. Jesus' parable shows that such comparisons are dangerous. Every person stands before God in need of mercy.

Finally, these verses offer hope to those who feel unworthy to approach God. Like the tax collector, they can come with nothing but their need and pray, "God, be merciful to me, a sinner." Jesus promises that such prayers are heard and that those who pray them go home justified.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 18 is about how people approach God. The parable of the persistent widow teaches believers to pray always and not give up, especially when facing injustice and long delays. God is not like the unjust judge; He is a loving Father who will bring justice to His people. Believers can bring their cries to Him persistently, trusting His character and timing, while asking whether He will find faith in them when He comes.

The parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector teaches that God accepts the humble, not the self-righteous. Believers can come to God not on the basis of their achievements but on the basis of His mercy. They can guard against comparing themselves to others and looking down on those who struggle, remembering that they too need God's mercy every day.

The welcome of little children reinforces this lesson. God's kingdom belongs to those who receive it like children, with dependence and trust. The rich ruler shows the opposite: someone who could not let go of his wealth and his record of obedience. Believers can examine what they are clinging to and trust that what is impossible for people is possible with God.

Finally, the blind man near Jericho shows the right response to Jesus: crying out for mercy, persisting despite opposition, recognizing Jesus as the Son of David, and following Him with praise. Believers can approach Jesus the same way, with honest need, persistent faith, and grateful worship.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, teach me to pray always and not to lose heart. When I am waiting for answers and justice seems delayed, help me to keep crying out to You, trusting that You hear and that You will act. When You return, may You find faith in me.

I come to You not with my achievements, but with my need. God, be merciful to me, a sinner. Keep me from pride and from looking down on others. Help me to receive Your kingdom like a little child, with trust and dependence. Show me anything I am clinging to more than You, and give me grace to let it go. Like the blind man near Jericho, I cry out for Your mercy. Open my eyes, and help me to follow You with praise. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
