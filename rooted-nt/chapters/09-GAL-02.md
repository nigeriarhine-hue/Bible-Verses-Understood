# Galatians 2
theme: Justified by Faith, Crucified with Christ
main: Paul describes his visit to Jerusalem, where the leading apostles, James, Peter, and John, recognized his gospel to the Gentiles, refused to require that the Greek believer Titus be circumcised, and gave him the right hand of fellowship. He then recounts how he publicly confronted Peter at Antioch when Peter withdrew from eating with Gentile believers out of fear. From that confrontation, Paul states the heart of the gospel: a person is justified not by the works of the Law but through faith in Jesus Christ. He has been crucified with Christ, and Christ now lives in him, for if righteousness came through the Law, Christ died for nothing.

## Chapter Background and Story

In chapter 1, Paul showed that his gospel came directly from Christ and not from the Jerusalem apostles. But this raised a question: Did the Jerusalem leaders agree with Paul, or were they on the side of the teachers troubling the Galatians? In chapter 2, Paul shows that the leading apostles fully recognized his gospel and his mission to the Gentiles.

He then tells a painful story. At Antioch, a church where Jewish and Gentile believers shared meals together, Peter at first joined freely in table fellowship with Gentiles. But when certain people arrived from Jerusalem, Peter pulled back. Other Jewish believers followed, even Barnabas. Paul saw that this was not a minor matter of social custom. By refusing to eat with Gentile believers, Peter was implying that they were second-class members of God's family unless they adopted Jewish law. That contradicted the gospel.

Paul's account of his words to Peter flows into one of the most important passages in all his letters, a summary of justification by faith and of the believer's union with Christ.

### Recognition in Jerusalem (2:1–10)

[[v1|Then after a period of fourteen years I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, taking Titus also with me]]. The fourteen years are counted either from Paul's conversion or from his first visit to Jerusalem. Scholars debate whether this visit is the famine relief visit of Acts 11:27–30 or the Jerusalem Council of Acts 15. Either way, the issue at stake was the same: must Gentile believers keep the Law of Moses?

[[v2|I went up by revelation, and I laid before them the Good News which I preach among the Gentiles, but privately before those who were respected, for fear that I might be running, or had run, in vain]]. Paul did not go for permission; he went because God directed him. But he wanted the unity of the church to be clear. If the Jerusalem leaders rejected his gospel, the church would be split into two, and his work among the Gentiles could be undermined.

[[v3|But not even Titus, who was with me, being a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised]]. Titus was a test case. He was a Gentile believer, and the Jerusalem leaders did not require him to be circumcised. This was strong evidence that Paul's gospel was accepted.

[[v4|This was because of the false brothers secretly brought in, who stole in to spy out our liberty which we have in Christ Jesus, that they might bring us into bondage]], [[v5|to whom we gave no place in the way of subjection, not for an hour, that the truth of the Good News might continue with you]]. Some people pretending to be believers had pushed for Titus to be circumcised. Paul refused to yield, not out of stubbornness, but to protect the freedom of the gospel for Gentiles like the Galatians.

[[v6|But from those who were reputed to be important—whatever they were, it makes no difference to me; God doesn’t show partiality to man—they, I say, who were respected imparted nothing to me]]. Paul respected the Jerusalem leaders, but he knew that God does not judge by status. The leaders added nothing to his gospel.

Instead, they recognized that God was working through Paul among the Gentiles just as He was working through Peter among the Jews (vv7–8). [[v9|and when they perceived the grace that was given to me, James and Cephas and John, those who were reputed to be pillars, gave to Barnabas and me the right hand of fellowship, that we should go to the Gentiles, and they to the circumcision]]. "Cephas" is the Aramaic form of Peter's name. The right hand of fellowship was a sign of partnership and agreement. The leaders divided the mission field, not the gospel. [[v10|They only asked us to remember the poor—which very thing I was also zealous to do]]. This concern for the poor led to the collection Paul gathered for Jerusalem (2 Corinthians 8–9).

### Justified by Faith (2:15–21)

Paul's words to Peter turn into a summary of the gospel. [[v15|“We, being Jews by nature and not Gentile sinners]]. Jewish people often spoke of Gentiles as "sinners" because they did not have or keep the Law. Paul begins from that common viewpoint and then shows that even Jewish believers had to be justified the same way as Gentiles: through faith in Christ. This central teaching is studied below.

[[v17|But if while we sought to be justified in Christ, we ourselves also were found sinners, is Christ a servant of sin? Certainly not!]] Paul's opponents might argue that abandoning the Law to eat with Gentiles made Jewish believers "sinners" and that Christ was therefore promoting sin. Paul rejects this completely. [[v18|For if I build up again those things which I destroyed, I prove myself a law-breaker]]. The real wrongdoing would be to rebuild the Law as a way of being right with God after having torn it down as that way. That was exactly what Peter was doing at Antioch.

[[v19|For I through the law died to the law, that I might live to God]]. The Law itself, by exposing sin and pronouncing its sentence of death, led Paul to Christ, who bore that sentence. In union with Christ's death, Paul died to the Law as a means of righteousness, so that he could live for God. His union with Christ and his conclusion about grace are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Galatians 2:11–14 — Walking Uprightly According to the Truth of the Gospel

verse: Galatians 2:11-14

#### Simple Explanation

Antioch, in Syria, was home to the first large church with many Gentile members (Acts 11:19–26). Jewish and Gentile believers there ate together, including, most likely, the Lord's Supper. For Jews who followed strict interpretations of the food laws, eating with Gentiles was a serious issue, because Gentile food might not be prepared according to the Law and because close table fellowship with Gentiles was widely avoided.

When Peter came to Antioch, he ate freely with Gentile believers. This was consistent with what God had shown him in his vision and at the house of Cornelius (Acts 10:28). But when "some people came from James," Peter "drew back and separated himself, fearing those who were of the circumcision." These visitors may have pressured Jewish believers to keep separate from Gentiles. Peter's fear of their disapproval led him to change his behavior.

The effect spread. "The rest of the Jews joined him in his hypocrisy, so that even Barnabas was carried away." Barnabas, Paul's partner in the Gentile mission, was swept along. The church was being divided into two groups at the table.

Paul saw that this was not merely about food. It was about "the truth of the Good News." By withdrawing, Peter was sending a message: Gentile believers were not fully acceptable unless they lived like Jews. So Paul confronted him publicly: "If you, being a Jew, live as the Gentiles do, and not as the Jews do, why do you compel the Gentiles to live as the Jews do?"

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This story shows that the gospel has social consequences. If all believers are justified by faith in Christ alone, then they all belong equally to God's family and should share the same table. Separating from fellow believers on the basis of ethnicity or religious custom denies the gospel in practice, even if it is affirmed in words.

Paul calls Peter's actions "hypocrisy," which literally means play-acting. Peter knew the truth; he had acted on it. But fear made him act as though he believed something else. This is a sobering reminder that even mature believers and respected leaders can fail when they fear others' disapproval. Peter, who had once denied Jesus out of fear, struggled again with the same weakness.

It is also important to see what happened later. Peter was not destroyed by this confrontation. At the Jerusalem Council, he spoke powerfully for salvation by grace for Gentiles (Acts 15:7–11), and in his own letter he speaks warmly of [[2 Peter 3:15|our beloved brother Paul]]. Honest correction, received humbly, can strengthen relationships rather than end them.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Churches today can still divide along lines of ethnicity, class, culture, or politics, sometimes without noticing. This passage calls believers to examine whether their practices, such as whom they welcome, sit with, eat with, and include, reflect the truth that all believers are one in Christ.

This story also shows the danger of fear of others' opinions. Peter knew what was right but changed his behavior under social pressure. Believers face similar pressures to distance themselves from people others look down on. Faithfulness to the gospel sometimes requires the courage to stay at the table.

Finally, Paul's example shows that loving correction is sometimes necessary, even with respected leaders. When public behavior harms others and contradicts the gospel, it may need to be addressed openly. Such correction should be motivated by concern for the truth and for those being harmed, not by pride.

### Galatians 2:16 — Justified Through Faith in Jesus Christ

verse: Galatians 2:16

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is one of the clearest statements of the gospel in Paul's letters. He uses the word "justified" three times. To be justified means to be declared righteous, to be put right with God, to be accepted by Him as being in the right. It is a legal term, like a judge's verdict, but it also describes the restoration of a relationship.

Paul says that "a man is not justified by the works of the law." "Works of the law" refers to doing what the Law of Moses commands. In the context of Galatians, Paul has especially in view the practices that marked Jews as distinct from Gentiles, such as circumcision, food laws, and Sabbath observance, but the principle applies to any attempt to be right with God through law-keeping.

Instead, a person is justified "through faith in Jesus Christ." Paul emphasizes that even Jewish believers like himself and Peter, who had the Law, "believed in Christ Jesus, that we might be justified by faith in Christ."

He ends with a reason drawn from Psalm 143:2: "because no flesh will be justified by the works of the law." No human being can be made right with God by keeping the Law, because everyone falls short of it (Romans 3:20, 23).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The phrase translated "faith in Jesus Christ" can also be translated "the faithfulness of Jesus Christ." Scholars have debated this for many years. Those who prefer "faithfulness of Christ" see Paul emphasizing Christ's faithful obedience to the Father, even to death, as the basis of justification. Those who prefer "faith in Christ" see Paul emphasizing the believer's trust in Christ. In this verse, Paul clearly speaks of believers trusting in Christ ("we believed in Christ Jesus"), so both ideas are present: believers are justified because of Christ's faithful work, received through their faith in Him.

This verse was at the center of the Protestant Reformation in the sixteenth century, when Martin Luther and others found in it freedom from trying to earn God's favor through religious works. Christians from many traditions agree that salvation is God's gracious gift received through faith, though they have sometimes described the relationship between faith, works, and the sacraments in different ways. In recent decades, some scholars have emphasized that Paul's argument in Galatians concerns not only individual salvation but also whether Gentiles can belong fully to God's people without becoming Jews. Both concerns are present. Justification by faith means that every person, Jew or Gentile, comes to God on the same basis, and therefore all believers belong together.

Faith in this sense is not a work that earns salvation. It is trusting reliance on Christ, receiving what He has done. Genuine faith will produce a changed life (Galatians 5:6), but the changed life is the fruit of justification, not its basis.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people, including many churchgoers, live with a quiet belief that they must earn God's acceptance by being good enough, religious enough, or disciplined enough. This verse offers freedom. God's acceptance comes through faith in Christ, not through a record of achievements. Believers can rest in what Christ has done.

This truth also humbles. If no one is justified by works, then no believer can look down on another as less worthy. Everyone stands before God on the same ground: the grace of God received through faith. That truth should shape how believers treat those of different backgrounds, as Paul's confrontation with Peter shows.

### Galatians 2:20 — Christ Lives in Me

verse: Galatians 2:20

#### Simple Explanation

This is one of the most personal and beloved verses in Paul's letters. "I have been crucified with Christ." When Christ died, Paul died with Him. This is not a physical death but a spiritual union. Paul's old life, the life of trying to be right with God by his own efforts and living for himself, came to an end at the cross.

"And it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me." The center of Paul's life has changed. He still has his own personality, but the driving force of his life is now the risen Christ living in him by the Holy Spirit.

"That life which I now live in the flesh, I live by faith in the Son of God." Paul still lives in a human body, with its limitations and struggles. But he lives that life by trusting in Christ day by day.

"Who loved me and gave himself up for me." Paul makes it deeply personal. Christ did not only love the world in general; He loved Paul specifically and gave Himself for him. The great love of God becomes intensely personal at the cross.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul develops this idea of union with Christ in many places. In Romans 6:3–11, he explains that believers were baptized into Christ's death and raised to walk in newness of life. In Colossians 3:3, he says, [[Colossians 3:3|For you died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God]]. The Christian life is not merely imitation of Christ from a distance; it is participation in His life.

In the context of Galatians, this verse explains why returning to the Law makes no sense. The person who has died with Christ has died to the Law as a means of righteousness (v19). Their new life is not lived by law-keeping but by faith in the Son of God and by the power of Christ living in them.

The phrase "who loved me and gave himself up for me" is the only place in Paul's letters where he speaks so directly of Christ's love for him in the singular. It reveals the personal heart behind Paul's theology. Everything he taught flowed from the wonder of being loved by Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse changes how believers understand their identity. They are not simply people trying to be better. They are people who have died with Christ and in whom Christ now lives. Their old self does not have the final word. This truth can bring hope to those who feel trapped by their past or their failures.

It also shapes daily living. Each day can be lived "by faith in the Son of God," trusting Him for strength, guidance, and grace in ordinary tasks and struggles. The Christian life is not lived by willpower alone but by reliance on Christ who lives within.

Finally, this verse invites every believer to receive Christ's love personally. It is one thing to know that God loves the world; it is another to say, "He loved me and gave Himself for me." That personal assurance can steady the heart in seasons of doubt, loneliness, or shame.

### Galatians 2:21 — If Righteousness Came Through the Law, Christ Died for Nothing

verse: Galatians 2:21

#### Simple Explanation

Paul concludes his argument with a powerful statement. "I don’t reject the grace of God." The teachers in Galatia were, in effect, setting aside God's grace by insisting that the Law must be added to faith in Christ. Paul refuses to do that.

Then he gives the decisive reason: "For if righteousness is through the law, then Christ died for nothing!" If people could be made right with God by keeping the Law, then there would have been no need for Christ to die. His death would have been pointless.

This shows how serious the issue in Galatia was. Adding the Law as a requirement for righteousness did not simply add a few extra rules. It undermined the very reason for the cross.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The cross is the measure of how serious sin is and how great God's grace is. If human effort could solve the problem of sin, God would not have given His Son. The fact that Christ died shows that no other way was sufficient. To trust in anything other than Christ for righteousness is, in effect, to say that His death was unnecessary.

Paul is not saying the Law is bad. In chapter 3 he will explain that the Law had a good purpose: it revealed sin and guided God's people until Christ came. But it was never meant to be the means by which people are made right with God. That role belongs to Christ alone.

This verse also shows that grace and self-justification cannot be combined. A person either receives righteousness as a gift through Christ or tries to earn it through works. Mixing the two turns grace into something else. As Paul wrote in Romans 11:6, if it is by grace, it is no longer of works.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can slip into thinking that their standing with God depends on their performance, such as how much they pray, serve, or avoid sin. Those things are good fruits of faith, but they are not the basis of righteousness. This verse calls believers back to the cross as the only foundation of their acceptance with God.

This verse also encourages gratitude. Christ did not die for nothing. His death accomplished what no human effort ever could. Remembering this can turn anxious striving into grateful obedience, and it honors the immense cost of God's grace.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Galatians 2 shows that the gospel unites people. The leaders in Jerusalem recognized that the same God was at work through Peter among Jews and through Paul among Gentiles. They gave Paul the right hand of fellowship, and they did not require Gentile believers to become Jews. The gospel creates one family from people of every background.

The confrontation at Antioch warns that this unity can be undermined by fear and social pressure, even among respected leaders. When Peter withdrew from Gentile believers, he denied in practice what he believed in principle. Churches today can examine their own practices to see whether they welcome and include all believers equally, and believers can find courage to stand with those others exclude.

At the heart of the chapter is the truth that people are justified through faith in Jesus Christ, not by the works of the Law. No one earns God's acceptance. Everyone receives it as a gift through Christ. This truth brings freedom from anxious striving and humility that rules out looking down on others.

Finally, Paul's words "I have been crucified with Christ, and it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me" describe the inner reality of the Christian life. Believers have died to the old way of self-effort and now live by faith in the Son of God, who loved them and gave Himself for them. Holding on to grace honors the cross. If righteousness came through the Law, Christ would have died for nothing, but He did not die in vain. His death is the source of new life for all who believe.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You that I am made right with God not by my own works but through faith in You. Thank You that You loved me and gave Yourself up for me. I have been crucified with You; let it be no longer I who live, but You who live in me. Teach me to live each day by faith in You. Keep me from setting aside Your grace by trusting in my own efforts. Give me the courage to stand for the truth of the gospel, even when I feel pressure to please others. Help me to welcome and stand with all Your people, whatever their background, and never to treat any of them as second-class members of Your family. Make me humble enough to receive correction and wise enough to offer it in love. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
