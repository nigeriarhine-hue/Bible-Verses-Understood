# Matthew 6
theme: Living Before the Father, Not for the Audience
main: Jesus teaches that giving, prayer, and fasting should be done for God, who sees in secret, rather than to impress other people. He gives His disciples a model prayer, warns that wealth can capture the heart, and calls them to replace anxious striving with trust in their heavenly Father by seeking His kingdom first.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 6 continues the Sermon on the Mount. In chapter 5 Jesus described the character of kingdom people and showed that God's commands reach the heart: anger, lust, dishonesty, revenge, and hatred of enemies. He ended that chapter by saying His disciples should be complete in love, as their heavenly Father is. Chapter 6 turns to another area where the heart is revealed: religious practice and the use of money. The phrase "your Father" appears again and again in this chapter. Jesus wants His followers to live with a constant awareness that they are children of a Father who sees, knows, and cares.

The first half of the chapter (vv. 1–18) addresses three practices that were central to Jewish devotion: giving to the poor, prayer, and fasting. Jesus does not question whether His disciples should do these things; He assumes they will. His concern is why and how they do them. The second half (vv. 19–34) addresses money, possessions, and anxiety, showing how the heart can become divided between God and wealth.

### The Warning About Performing for Others (6:1)

Jesus begins with a general principle: [[v1|Be careful that you don’t do your charitable giving before men, to be seen by them, or else you have no reward from your Father who is in heaven]]. The word translated "charitable giving" in some manuscripts is literally "righteousness," referring to acts of devotion in general. The problem is not being seen; Jesus told His disciples in Matthew 5:16 to let their light shine. The problem is doing good deeds in order to be seen and praised. When that is the goal, the praise of people is the only reward one receives.

### Giving to the Poor (6:2–4)

Jesus describes "hypocrites" who announce their giving with a trumpet in the synagogues and streets. The word "hypocrite" originally referred to an actor in the Greek theater who wore a mask and played a role. Whether Jesus means a literal trumpet or uses the phrase as a vivid image for self-promotion, the point is clear: some people gave in a way designed to win admiration. Jesus says, [[v2|they have received their reward]]. The verb was used in commercial receipts to mean "paid in full." They wanted applause, they got it, and that is all they will get.

Instead, His disciples are not to let their left hand know what their right hand is doing. This is a vivid way of saying that giving should be so unselfconscious that we do not even keep score for ourselves. The Father, who sees in secret, will reward them.

### Prayer (6:5–15)

Jesus then applies the same principle to prayer. Some people liked to pray standing in the synagogues and on street corners so that others would see them. Jewish people often prayed at set times of the day, and someone who arranged to be in a public place at those times could make a show of their devotion. Jesus tells His disciples to go into their [[v6|inner room]], close the door, and pray to the Father in secret. In a typical home, the inner room was often a storeroom, the only room with a door. The point is not that public prayer is wrong (Jesus Himself prayed publicly), but that prayer is conversation with God, not a performance for an audience.

He also warns against babbling [[v7|vain repetitions as the Gentiles do]]. In many pagan religions, worshipers piled up the names of gods and repeated formulas, hoping to secure the gods' attention. Jesus says that the Father already knows what His children need before they ask. Prayer is not a technique for persuading a reluctant deity; it is trusting communication with a Father who cares.

Then Jesus gives a model prayer, often called the Lord's Prayer, which is studied in detail below. After the prayer, He adds a comment on one line of it: if we forgive others, the Father will forgive us; if we refuse to forgive, neither will the Father forgive us. This does not mean that we earn God's forgiveness by forgiving others. It means that a person who has truly received God's mercy cannot hold onto unforgiveness toward others without contradicting the very mercy they claim to have received. Jesus will illustrate this with a parable in Matthew 18:21–35.

### Fasting (6:16–18)

Fasting, going without food for a period of time to devote oneself to God, was a regular part of Jewish piety. The law required fasting on the Day of Atonement, and devout people like the Pharisees fasted twice a week (Luke 18:12). Some made sure others knew it by looking gloomy and neglecting their appearance. Jesus tells His disciples to wash their faces and groom themselves normally so that their fasting is seen only by the Father. Again, the reward comes from God, not from people.

### Treasure in Heaven (6:19–24)

The second half of the chapter turns to wealth. Jesus warns against storing up treasures on earth, where [[v19|moth and rust consume, and where thieves break through and steal]]. In the ancient world, wealth was often kept in fine clothing (vulnerable to moths), metals and grain (which could corrode or be eaten by pests), and stored valuables (which thieves could steal by digging through mud-brick walls). Earthly wealth is insecure. Instead, His disciples should store up treasure in heaven, where it is safe.

He then speaks of the eye as the lamp of the body. If the eye is "sound" (or "single," focused and generous), the whole body is full of light; if the eye is "evil," the body is full of darkness. In Jewish idiom, a "good eye" described a generous person and an "evil eye" described someone stingy or envious (see Proverbs 22:9; 28:22). Jesus is saying that how we look at money and possessions affects our whole life.

Finally, He says no one can serve two masters. A slave could not truly belong to two owners with competing demands. [[v24|You can’t serve both God and Mammon]]. "Mammon" is an Aramaic word for wealth or property. Jesus personifies it as a rival master that demands loyalty. The issue is not owning things, but who or what rules the heart.

### Anxiety and Trust (6:25–34)

Because no one can serve both God and money, Jesus says, [[v25|don’t be anxious for your life]]. Many of His listeners were poor farmers and laborers for whom food and clothing were real daily concerns. Jesus does not dismiss these needs. He points to the birds, which do not sow or reap yet are fed by the Father, and to the wildflowers, which are more beautifully clothed than Solomon in all his royal splendor, though they bloom for only a short time and are then used as fuel for ovens. If God cares for birds and flowers, He will certainly care for His children.

Jesus gently rebukes His listeners as people of [[v30|little faith]] and points out that worry accomplishes nothing; it cannot add a single moment to a person's life. Anxious striving after food, drink, and clothing is what [[v32|the Gentiles seek after]], meaning people who do not know God as their Father. Disciples know that their Father knows what they need. So instead of being ruled by anxiety, they are to seek first God's kingdom and His righteousness, and these needs will be provided. The chapter ends with practical wisdom: do not worry about tomorrow, because each day has enough trouble of its own.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 6:9–13 — The Prayer Jesus Taught

verse: Matthew 6:9-13

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus gives this prayer in the middle of His teaching about prayer. He has just warned against praying to be seen by others and against heaping up empty words as though God could be manipulated by long speeches. Now He says, [[v9|Pray like this]]. The prayer is a model showing what true prayer includes, though Christians have also prayed these exact words together from the earliest days of the church. Luke records a shorter version, given when a disciple asked Jesus to teach them to pray (Luke 11:1–4).

The prayer begins, "Our Father in heaven." Jews sometimes called God "Father" in prayer, but Jesus' constant use of this name, and His invitation to His disciples to share in it, was distinctive. "Our" reminds disciples that they pray as part of a family. "In heaven" reminds them that the Father is also the sovereign Lord above all. The first request, [[v9|may your name be kept holy]], asks that God's name, meaning His character and reputation, be honored and treated as holy by everyone.

The next two requests continue that focus: [[v10|Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven]]. These ask God to establish His rule fully on earth, so that the world is set right and His will is done here as completely as it is in heaven. The prayer begins with God's glory and purposes before turning to personal needs.

Then come three requests for daily needs. "Give us today our daily bread" asks for the provision needed for each day, reminding disciples to depend on God one day at a time, as Israel depended on manna in the wilderness. [[v12|Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors]] asks for forgiveness of sin, which is pictured as a debt owed to God, and acknowledges that those who receive forgiveness forgive others. [[v13|Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one]] asks God to keep His children from being overwhelmed by testing and to rescue them from the devil's power. The closing words of praise, "For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen," are not found in the oldest manuscripts of Matthew, but they were used in early Christian worship and echo 1 Chronicles 29:11.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The structure of this prayer teaches believers what matters most. It begins with God: His name, His kingdom, His will. Only then does it turn to human needs. This order reshapes the way people pray. Prayer is not primarily a way to get what we want but a way of aligning our lives with God's purposes. Yet the prayer also shows that God cares about the most basic needs: food, forgiveness, and protection.

The prayer is deeply communal. Every request uses "us" and "our." Even when praying alone in a closed room, disciples pray as members of God's family, concerned for the needs of others as well as their own.

The prayer also holds together the present and the future. Asking for God's kingdom to come looks toward the day when Christ returns and all evil is removed. Asking for daily bread and forgiveness today recognizes that we live now, in a world not yet made whole, dependent on God's grace each day. The prayer gives believers both hope for the future and help for the present.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This prayer offers a pattern that can shape daily prayer. Believers can begin by honoring God and asking that His name be respected in their own lives, families, and communities. They can pray for His will to be done in specific situations, including in their own choices. Then they can bring their needs, confess their sins, and ask for protection, all with confidence that the Father hears.

The prayer also challenges believers to examine their relationships. Praying "as we also forgive our debtors" while holding onto bitterness is a contradiction. If there is someone we refuse to forgive, this prayer brings that to light and invites us to release it to God. Forgiveness does not mean pretending wrongdoing did not happen or necessarily restoring trust immediately, but it does mean giving up the desire for revenge and leaving judgment to God.

Finally, the request for daily bread encourages contentment and trust. Rather than demanding security for years ahead, believers can ask God for what they need today and trust Him again tomorrow. And the request for deliverance from temptation encourages humility: believers do not presume on their own strength but ask God to guard them.

### Matthew 6:21 — Where Your Treasure Is

verse: Matthew 6:21

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes Jesus' short teaching about treasures in verses 19–21. He has told His disciples not to store up treasures on earth, where moths, rust, and thieves can destroy or take them, but to store up treasures in heaven, where nothing can damage or steal them. Now He gives the reason: [[v21|for where your treasure is, there your heart will be also]].

In biblical thought, the "heart" is not merely the seat of emotions but the center of a person's thoughts, desires, choices, and loyalties. Jesus is saying that what we value most will capture our hearts. Our attention, affection, and energy naturally follow what we treasure.

Jesus' listeners understood earthly treasure: fine clothes, stored grain, precious metals, and land. Many of them had very little, while some, like the religious leaders, were comfortable. "Treasure in heaven" refers to what has lasting value before God, such as faithfulness, generosity, acts of mercy, and devotion to God's kingdom. Elsewhere Jesus speaks of selling possessions and giving to the poor as a way of storing treasure in heaven (Matthew 19:21; Luke 12:33).

This verse connects the private devotion described earlier in the chapter with the use of money. Just as the hypocrites wanted the reward of human praise, people can set their hearts on earthly wealth. In both cases, the question is the same: what are we really living for?

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the close connection between money and spiritual life. Jesus speaks about wealth often because it so easily becomes a rival to God. Paul writes that [[1 Timothy 6:10|the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil]], and warns that those who are eager to be rich fall into many temptations. Possessions are not evil in themselves, but they can easily claim the heart.

The verse also teaches that the heart follows investment. We often think we must first feel love for something before we give to it, but Jesus suggests that where we place our treasure shapes what we love. Giving generously to God's purposes draws the heart toward those purposes. Hoarding draws the heart toward possessions.

Treasure in heaven also speaks of hope. Earthly wealth is temporary; it cannot be taken beyond death. Treasure in heaven lasts forever. Jesus calls His followers to live with eternity in view, valuing what God values and investing in what will endure.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to look honestly at where their time, money, and attention go. Bank statements, calendars, and daily thoughts often reveal what we truly treasure. If most of our energy is spent pursuing financial security, comfort, or possessions, our hearts will follow.

It also calls believers to practice generosity as a spiritual discipline. Giving to the church, supporting those in need, and contributing to work that advances God's kingdom are ways of storing treasure in heaven. Generosity loosens the grip of money on the heart and directs affection toward God's purposes. This does not mean neglecting responsible care for family or necessities; Scripture also commends providing for one's household (1 Timothy 5:8). It means keeping possessions in their proper place.

Finally, this verse offers freedom. Those who place their treasure in heaven do not have to live in fear of losing everything. Economic changes, theft, or loss can still hurt, but they cannot touch the treasure that matters most.

### Matthew 6:33 — Seek First His Kingdom

verse: Matthew 6:33

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the climax of Jesus' teaching about anxiety in verses 25–34. He has told His disciples not to worry about food, drink, and clothing. He has pointed to the birds and wildflowers as evidence of God's care, and He has reminded them that worry cannot add a moment to their lives. He has also said that anxious pursuit of these things is characteristic of [[v32|the Gentiles]], meaning people who do not know God as Father, whereas His disciples have a Father who knows what they need.

Now Jesus tells them what to do instead of worrying: [[v33|But seek first God’s Kingdom and his righteousness]]. To "seek" means to pursue something as a priority, to make it the goal of one's efforts. "First" means first in importance, not merely first in a sequence. God's kingdom is His rule and saving reign through Jesus. "His righteousness" refers to the way of life that reflects God's will, which Jesus has been describing throughout the Sermon on the Mount.

Then comes a promise: [[v33|and all these things will be given to you as well]]. "All these things" refers specifically to the necessities Jesus has been discussing: food, drink, and clothing. Jesus promises that the Father will provide what His children truly need when they make His kingdom their priority.

The final verse of the chapter adds practical wisdom: do not worry about tomorrow, because each day has enough trouble of its own. Disciples are to live faithfully in the present, trusting God with the future.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse addresses the root of anxiety. Worry often comes from making our own security the center of life. When we are ultimately responsible for providing everything we need, anxiety is natural. Jesus offers a different center: God's kingdom. When God's purposes take first place, our needs are placed in His hands, and He has promised to care for His children.

This promise must be understood carefully. Jesus is not promising wealth or luxury, nor is He saying that believers will never experience hardship or need. Christians in the New Testament sometimes went hungry and suffered poverty (2 Corinthians 11:27; Philippians 4:12). The promise is that God will provide what His children truly need to live and to do His will, and that nothing can separate them from His care. Paul expresses the same confidence: [[Philippians 4:19|My God will supply every need of yours according to his riches in glory in Christ Jesus]].

The verse also teaches that God's kingdom and righteousness belong together. Seeking the kingdom does not only mean religious activity; it means pursuing a life that reflects God's justice, mercy, and holiness in every area, including work, money, and relationships.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to examine their priorities. Many decisions about work, finances, time, and relationships reveal what we are seeking first. Seeking God's kingdom first might mean choosing honesty when dishonesty would be more profitable, giving generously even when finances are tight, or making time for worship and service when other activities compete for attention.

It also offers a practical response to anxiety. When worries arise about money, health, or the future, believers can turn them into prayer, bringing their needs to the Father who knows them. Then they can focus on what God has given them to do today, rather than trying to control tomorrow. Responsible planning is not forbidden; Scripture praises wise preparation (Proverbs 6:6–8). What Jesus forbids is anxious striving that forgets God.

Finally, this verse invites trust in God's character. The Father who feeds birds and clothes flowers knows and loves His children. Believers can rest in His care, even when circumstances are uncertain, and find freedom to live generously and faithfully rather than fearfully.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 6 confronts one of the most subtle temptations in spiritual life: doing the right things for the wrong reasons. Giving, praying, and fasting are good, but they can become ways of building a reputation or feeling superior. Jesus calls His followers to live before an audience of One. In practice, this might mean giving quietly without seeking recognition, praying honestly in private rather than only in public, and practicing spiritual disciplines without advertising them. It also means examining motives regularly and asking God to purify them.

The chapter also teaches believers how to pray. The Lord's Prayer provides a framework that keeps God's glory central while bringing real needs before Him. It reminds believers to depend on God daily, to confess sin honestly, to forgive others, and to ask for protection from temptation and evil. Using this pattern regularly can deepen prayer and keep it from becoming either self-centered or mechanical.

Jesus' teaching about money is especially relevant in cultures shaped by consumerism and financial pressure. He warns that wealth can capture the heart and become a rival master. Believers can respond by practicing generosity, holding possessions loosely, and evaluating major financial decisions in light of God's kingdom. The question is not only "Can I afford this?" but "Does this reflect what I treasure most?"

Finally, the chapter speaks directly to anxiety. Jesus does not shame people for their worries, but He invites them to trust their Father. He reminds them of God's care for creation, the uselessness of worry, and the priority of God's kingdom. Anxiety may sometimes require practical help, including counsel or medical care, and seeking such help is consistent with trusting God. But the deepest answer to fear is knowing the Father's love and choosing, day by day, to seek His kingdom first and leave tomorrow in His hands.

## Chapter Prayer

Father in heaven, thank You that You see what is done in secret and that I can live my life before You rather than for the approval of others. Forgive me for the times I have given, prayed, or served to be noticed. Purify my motives so that I seek Your reward and not human praise.

Teach me to pray as Jesus taught: to honor Your name, to long for Your kingdom, to seek Your will, to depend on You for daily bread, to confess my sins and forgive those who have wronged me, and to ask You to keep me from evil. Guard my heart from the love of money, and help me to store up treasure in heaven through generosity and faithfulness. When I am anxious about tomorrow, remind me that You know what I need. Help me to seek Your kingdom and righteousness first, and to trust You with everything else. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
