# Luke 11
theme: Teach Us to Pray: Bold Asking and True Blessedness
main: When His disciples ask Him to teach them to pray, Jesus gives them a model prayer and a parable about a friend at midnight, encouraging bold, persistent asking of a Father who gives the Holy Spirit. He answers critics who claim He works by Satan's power, says that true blessedness belongs to those who hear and keep God's word, warns a generation that demands signs, and rebukes religious leaders whose outward correctness hides neglect of justice and love for God.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 11 continues Jesus' journey toward Jerusalem. The previous chapter ended with Mary sitting at Jesus' feet, listening to His word, and Jesus saying this was the one thing needed (Luke 10:42). Luke 11 begins with prayer, another way of being in God's presence. The chapter then moves to conflict, as Jesus faces accusations and confronts the hypocrisy of religious leaders.

Some of the teaching in this chapter also appears in Matthew, often in different settings. Jesus likely taught similar themes on multiple occasions. (The Lord's Prayer is studied at Matthew 6:9–13; asking, seeking, and knocking at Matthew 7:7; the Beelzebul controversy and blasphemy against the Spirit at Matthew 12:31–32; and the woes on the Pharisees at Matthew 23:23.) This chapter focuses on Luke's distinctive emphases.

### Teach Us to Pray (11:1–4)

Luke sets the scene: Jesus [[v1|finished praying in a certain place]]. Throughout Luke's Gospel, Jesus prays at important moments. His disciples had watched Him pray, and one of them asked, [[v1|Lord, teach us to pray, just as John also taught his disciples]]. Religious teachers in that time often gave their followers distinctive prayers that expressed their community's identity.

Jesus gave them a prayer that is shorter than the version in Matthew 6:9–13. It begins, [[v2|Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy. May your Kingdom come]]. (Many manuscripts read simply "Father," the intimate address Jesus Himself used.) The disciples were to ask God for daily bread, for forgiveness of sins as they forgive everyone indebted to them, and for protection from temptation. Luke's version says, [[v4|Forgive us our sins, for we ourselves also forgive everyone who is indebted to us]], connecting God's forgiveness of sins with the believer's forgiveness of others' debts.

### The Friend at Midnight (11:5–8)

Jesus then told a parable found only in Luke. Imagine going to a friend at midnight and asking to borrow three loaves of bread because a traveler has arrived and you have nothing to offer him. Hospitality was a sacred duty in that culture; failing to feed a guest would bring shame on the whole household and village. Travelers often journeyed in the evening to avoid the heat, so a late arrival was not unusual. Families typically baked bread daily, so having none left at midnight was a real problem.

The friend inside answers, [[v7|Don’t bother me. The door is now shut, and my children are with me in bed. I can’t get up and give it to you]]. In a typical one-room peasant home, the whole family slept together on mats on a raised platform. Getting up meant waking everyone. Yet Jesus said that the friend would get up and give what was needed, not because of friendship, but because of the asker's [[v8|persistence]]. This is studied below.

### Ask, Seek, Knock (11:9–13)

Jesus applied the parable: [[v9|keep asking, and it will be given you. Keep seeking, and you will find. Keep knocking, and it will be opened to you]]. The WEB's wording "keep asking" captures the ongoing sense of the Greek verbs. Everyone who asks receives, seeks finds, and knocks has the door opened.

Then He reasoned from human fathers. What father would give his son a snake when he asks for a fish, or a scorpion when he asks for an egg? Even sinful parents know how to give good gifts. Then Jesus concluded with a promise unique to Luke's wording, studied below: the heavenly Father will give the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him.

### Jesus and Beelzebul (11:14–28)

Jesus cast out a demon that made a man mute. When the demon left, the man spoke, and the crowds were amazed. But some said, [[v15|He casts out demons by Beelzebul, the prince of the demons]]. Others tested Him by asking for a sign from heaven.

Jesus answered that a kingdom divided against itself falls. If Satan were fighting himself, his kingdom could not stand. He added, [[v20|But if I by God’s finger cast out demons, then God’s Kingdom has come to you]]. The phrase "finger of God" recalls Exodus 8:19, where Pharaoh's magicians, unable to copy Moses' miracle, admitted, "This is God's finger." Jesus' power came directly from God, and it showed that God's kingdom had arrived.

He told of a strong man, fully armed, guarding his house; but when someone stronger attacks and overpowers him, the stronger one takes away his armor and divides the spoils. Jesus is the stronger one who has come to defeat Satan. He then said, [[v23|He who is not with me is against me]], and warned that an unclean spirit who leaves a person may return with seven others worse than itself if the "house" remains empty. Being cleaned up is not enough; a person must be filled with God's presence.

As Jesus said these things, a woman in the crowd called out, [[v27|Blessed is the womb that bore you, and the breasts which nursed you!]] Jesus replied, [[v28|On the contrary, blessed are those who hear the word of God, and keep it]]. This exchange, found only in Luke, is studied below.

### The Sign of Jonah and the Lamp of the Body (11:29–36)

As crowds grew, Jesus called His generation evil because it sought a sign. The only sign given would be the sign of Jonah. Just as Jonah was a sign to the people of Nineveh, who repented at his preaching, the Son of Man would be a sign to this generation. The Queen of the South and the people of Nineveh would condemn this generation at the judgment, because they responded to lesser messengers, while [[v31|one greater than Solomon is here]] and [[v32|one greater than Jonah is here]].

Jesus then said that no one lights a lamp and hides it. The eye is the lamp of the body. When the eye is healthy, the whole body is full of light; when it is bad, the body is full of darkness. He warned, [[v35|Therefore see whether the light that is in you isn’t darkness]]. The problem was not a lack of light from Jesus but the spiritual condition of those who refused to see it.

### Woes Against Pharisees and Lawyers (11:37–54)

A Pharisee invited Jesus to dinner. Jesus went in and reclined at the table without first performing the ritual washing before the meal, and the Pharisee was surprised. Jesus said, [[v39|Now you Pharisees cleanse the outside of the cup and of the platter, but your inward part is full of extortion and wickedness]]. God made both the outside and the inside. Then He gave a surprising instruction: [[v41|But give for gifts to the needy those things which are within, and behold, all things will be clean to you]]. Generosity from the heart, rather than ritual washing, makes a person clean.

Jesus pronounced three woes on the Pharisees: for tithing small herbs while neglecting [[v42|justice and God’s love]]; for loving the best seats and public greetings; and for being like unmarked graves that people walk over without knowing, becoming unclean (Numbers 19:16). The Pharisees appeared clean but secretly spread defilement.

One of the experts in the law said, [[v45|Teacher, in saying this you insult us also]]. Jesus pronounced three woes on the lawyers: for loading people with burdens they did not help carry; for building tombs for the prophets their ancestors killed, showing that they shared their ancestors' attitude; and for taking away [[v52|the key of knowledge]]. They had the Scriptures, which pointed to God's kingdom, but they neither entered it themselves nor allowed others to enter.

When Jesus left, the scribes and Pharisees became fiercely hostile and began questioning Him aggressively, [[v54|lying in wait for him, and seeking to catch him in something he might say]]. Opposition was growing as Jesus journeyed toward Jerusalem.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 11:8 — Bold Persistence in Prayer

verse: Luke 11:8

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes a parable Jesus told right after teaching His disciples the Lord's Prayer. In the story, a man has an unexpected guest arrive at midnight and has no bread to offer. In that culture, hospitality was a serious obligation; failing to provide food for a guest would bring shame. So the man goes to a friend's house and asks to borrow three loaves.

The friend inside does not want to help. His door is locked, his children are asleep with him, and getting up would disturb the whole family. He says, "Don't bother me."

Then Jesus says, [[v8|I tell you, although he will not rise and give it to him because he is his friend, yet because of his persistence, he will get up and give him as many as he needs]].

The word translated "persistence" literally means "shamelessness" or "boldness." It describes the man's willingness to keep asking without embarrassment, even at an inconvenient time. Some interpreters understand the word to refer to the sleeping friend's desire to avoid shame. In a small village, refusing to help a neighbor with a guest would bring dishonor on him too. Either way, the result is that the friend gets up and gives him [[v8|as many as he needs]], not just the minimum.

The parable uses a "how much more" argument. If a reluctant friend will help because of bold persistence, how much more will God, who is not reluctant at all, respond to His children's prayers? Jesus is not saying God is like the sleepy friend. He is saying God is far better.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that believers can pray boldly and persistently. God is not irritated by His children's requests. He invites them to ask, seek, and knock. The man in the parable asked for bread for someone else, a guest. This suggests that prayer for others' needs is especially welcome to God.

It also shows that God's response is generous. The friend gave "as many as he needs." God does not give grudgingly or minimally. He gives abundantly according to His wisdom.

The parable must be read alongside Jesus' teaching that God is a loving Father who knows what His children need (Luke 12:30). Persistence in prayer does not change God's mind as if He were reluctant; rather, it expresses faith, deepens dependence, and shapes the one who prays. Luke later records a similar parable about a persistent widow, told [[Luke 18:1|that they must always pray, and not give up]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to keep praying when answers seem delayed. Many believers give up praying for a need after a short time. Jesus invites them to keep asking with bold confidence that God hears.

It also encourages believers to pray for others. The man in the parable asked for bread to feed a guest. Believers can bring the needs of friends, family, neighbors, and even strangers to God, persistently and boldly.

Finally, this verse invites believers to approach God without shame. Some people hesitate to pray because they feel unworthy or worry that they are bothering God. Jesus' parable encourages bold, "shameless" prayer, rooted in confidence in God's goodness.

### Luke 11:13 — The Father Gives the Holy Spirit

verse: Luke 11:13

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes Jesus' teaching on prayer that began with the disciples' request, "Lord, teach us to pray." Jesus had given them a model prayer, told the parable of the friend at midnight, and encouraged them to keep asking, seeking, and knocking.

Then He used an illustration from family life. Which father, if his son asked for a fish, would give him a snake? Or if he asked for an egg, would give him a scorpion? A snake might resemble a certain type of fish, and a scorpion curled up might look somewhat like an egg. No loving father would trick his child with something harmful.

Then comes this verse: [[v13|If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him?]]

"Being evil" is a reminder that all humans are sinful, yet even sinful parents care for their children. The phrase "how much more" contrasts imperfect human parents with the perfect heavenly Father. If imperfect parents give good gifts, God will give far better gifts.

Matthew's parallel says that the Father will give "good things" to those who ask (Matthew 7:11). Luke specifies the greatest good gift: [[v13|the Holy Spirit]]. The Holy Spirit is God's own presence, dwelling in His people, empowering them, guiding them, and transforming them. Luke emphasizes the Holy Spirit throughout his Gospel and especially in Acts, where the Spirit is poured out on believers at Pentecost and throughout the early church.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that God's greatest gift to His children is Himself, through His Holy Spirit. Prayer is not merely a way to get things from God; it is a way to receive more of God. When believers pray, the Father gives them His presence and power.

It also reveals God's character as a loving Father. He is not stingy or deceptive. He does not trick His children or give them harmful things. Even when He does not give exactly what they ask for, He gives what is good. The best gift is the Spirit, who helps believers in every circumstance.

In Luke's second volume, Acts, this promise is fulfilled in remarkable ways. The disciples prayed, and the Spirit was poured out (Acts 1:14; 2:1–4). Believers prayed, and the place was shaken, and they were filled with the Spirit (Acts 4:31). Prayer and the gift of the Spirit go together.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to ask God for the Holy Spirit. Believers who have trusted Christ already have the Spirit dwelling in them (Romans 8:9). Yet the New Testament also speaks of being filled with the Spirit repeatedly (Ephesians 5:18). Believers can ask God to fill them, guide them, strengthen them, and work through them by His Spirit.

It also encourages believers to trust God's goodness in prayer. When God's answers differ from their requests, they can trust that He is giving good gifts, not harmful ones. Their heavenly Father loves them and knows what they need.

Finally, this verse invites believers to seek God Himself, not only His gifts. Prayer is about relationship. The greatest answer to prayer is more of God's presence in one's life.

### Luke 11:28 — Blessed Are Those Who Hear and Keep God's Word

verse: Luke 11:28

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records a brief exchange found only in Luke's Gospel. Jesus had just been teaching about His victory over Satan and warning about the danger of an empty house that evil spirits can reoccupy. As He was speaking, [[v27|a certain woman out of the multitude lifted up her voice and said to him, “Blessed is the womb that bore you, and the breasts which nursed you!”]]

The woman was praising Jesus by praising His mother. In that culture, a woman's honor was often tied to her children. Saying that the mother of such a son was blessed was a way of honoring the son. It was a sincere compliment, and it echoed what Elizabeth had said to Mary (Luke 1:42) and what Mary herself had sung, [[Luke 1:48|from now on, all generations will call me blessed]].

Jesus responded, [[v28|On the contrary, blessed are those who hear the word of God, and keep it]]. The word translated "on the contrary" can also mean "rather" or "even more." Jesus was not denying that His mother was blessed. Instead, He redirected the focus. The greatest blessing is not physical relationship to Jesus but hearing and obeying God's word.

Interestingly, Mary herself is the model of this blessing in Luke's Gospel. She heard God's word through the angel and responded, [[Luke 1:38|Let it be done to me according to your word]]. She treasured God's works in her heart (Luke 2:19, 51). Elizabeth said, [[Luke 1:45|Blessed is she who believed]]. Mary was blessed not only because she gave birth to Jesus but because she heard God's word and kept it.

This saying echoes Luke 8:21, where Jesus said His mother and brothers are those who hear God's word and do it.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that true blessedness is available to everyone. Physical closeness to Jesus, being His mother or brother, was a privilege only a few could have. But hearing and obeying God's word is open to all. Anyone can receive the blessing Jesus describes.

It also shows that hearing alone is not enough. Jesus says blessed are those who hear the word and "keep" it, meaning guard it, obey it, and live by it. This connects with the parable of the sower, where the good soil hears the word and holds it fast (Luke 8:15), and with the parable of the builders, where the wise person hears and does Jesus' words (Luke 6:47–48).

Finally, the verse places God's word at the center of the life of faith. Relationship with Jesus is expressed through attentive listening and faithful obedience to what God has said.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to pursue the blessing Jesus describes. Believers can make hearing God's word a regular part of their lives through Scripture reading, preaching, and reflection. They can also commit to keeping it, applying it to their decisions and relationships.

It also corrects any tendency to rely on religious connections or heritage. Being raised in a Christian family, belonging to a church, or having religious experiences are not substitutes for hearing and obeying God's word personally.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that blessing is found in obedience. When they keep God's word, even when it is difficult, they experience the blessedness Jesus promised: a life rooted in God's truth and filled with His presence.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 11 begins with prayer and ends with conflict, and both speak to the life of faith. The disciples' request, "Lord, teach us to pray," is one every believer can make. Jesus gave a simple pattern: address God as Father, seek His glory and kingdom, ask for daily needs, receive and extend forgiveness, and ask for protection from temptation. Believers can use this pattern to shape their prayers.

The parable of the friend at midnight and Jesus' teaching on asking, seeking, and knocking encourage bold, persistent prayer. God is not reluctant; He is a loving Father who gives good gifts, especially the gift of the Holy Spirit. Believers can pray with confidence, especially for the needs of others, and seek God Himself above all His gifts.

The controversy over Beelzebul reminds believers that Jesus is stronger than Satan, and that neutrality toward Jesus is impossible. Being cleaned up without being filled with God's presence leaves a person vulnerable. Believers can invite Christ to fill every part of their lives.

Jesus' response to the woman in the crowd reminds believers that true blessedness comes from hearing and keeping God's word. Finally, the woes against the Pharisees and lawyers warn against religion that focuses on outward appearance while neglecting justice, love for God, and generosity. Believers can examine their hearts, give generously to those in need, and make sure they are helping rather than hindering others from entering God's kingdom.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, as Your disciples asked, I ask You: teach me to pray. Help me to call God my Father, to seek His glory and His kingdom, to depend on Him for daily bread, to receive His forgiveness and forgive others, and to ask for His protection from temptation. Give me boldness and persistence in prayer, especially for the needs of others.

Thank You that my heavenly Father gives good gifts and the greatest gift of all, the Holy Spirit. Fill me with Your Spirit, and keep my life from being an empty house. Thank You that You are stronger than every power of evil. Make me one of those who hear the word of God and keep it. Cleanse me from the inside, free me from hypocrisy, and teach me to practice justice, love for God, and generosity. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
