# Matthew 20
theme: Grace That Overturns Ranking, and the Servant King
main: Through the parable of the vineyard workers, Jesus teaches that God's kingdom runs on generous grace, not on earning and comparison. On the road to Jerusalem He predicts His death a third time, then answers a request for places of honor by teaching that greatness means service, because He came to give His life as a ransom for many. Leaving Jericho, He stops for two blind men whom the crowd tries to silence and gives them sight.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 20 continues directly from chapter 19. Peter had just asked what the disciples would receive for leaving everything to follow Jesus, and Jesus promised a great reward, adding, [[Matthew 19:30|But many will be last who are first, and first who are last]]. The parable that opens chapter 20 explains that saying, and it ends with the same words reversed (v. 16). The whole chapter is about how the kingdom of heaven turns human ideas of status, merit, and greatness upside down.

### The Parable of the Workers in the Vineyard (20:1–16)

Jesus told of a landowner who went out early in the morning to hire workers for his vineyard. In Palestine, day laborers gathered in the marketplace hoping to be hired. They were among the poorest people in society, often landless, depending on daily work to feed their families. The landowner agreed to pay them a denarius for the day, the standard wage for a day's labor and roughly what a family needed to live for a day.

He went out again at the third hour (about 9 a.m.), the sixth (noon), and the ninth (3 p.m.), hiring more workers and promising to pay what was right. Finally, at the eleventh hour (about 5 p.m.), with only an hour of daylight left, he found others still standing around. When he asked why they had been idle all day, they replied, [[v7|Because no one has hired us]]. These were likely the least desirable workers, perhaps older, weaker, or less skilled. He sent them into the vineyard too.

At evening, the law required that day laborers be paid before sunset (Deuteronomy 24:14–15). The owner told his manager to pay the workers, starting with the last ones hired. Those who had worked only one hour received a full denarius. When those hired first came forward, they expected more. But they also received one denarius. They grumbled against the owner: [[v12|These last have spent one hour, and you have made them equal to us who have borne the burden of the day and the scorching heat!]]

The owner replied to one of them, calling him [[v13|Friend]], that he had done him no wrong; they had agreed on a denarius. He was free to be generous with what belonged to him. Then he asked, [[v15|Or is your eye evil, because I am good?]] Jesus concluded, [[v16|So the last will be first, and the first last]]. (Some manuscripts add "For many are called, but few are chosen," a saying that also appears in Matthew 22:14.)

The parable answers Peter's question about reward. The disciples would indeed be rewarded, but they must not think that God's kingdom works like a system of wages based on merit and seniority. God gives generously, and those who come late receive the same grace as those who came early. The parable also had a wider application in Jesus' setting: tax collectors, sinners, and later Gentiles were coming into the kingdom alongside the religious people who had long served God, and some resented it.

### Third Prediction of the Passion (20:17–19)

As Jesus was going up to Jerusalem (the city sits on hills, so travelers always "went up" to it), He took the Twelve aside and told them for the third time what would happen. This prediction is the most detailed. He would be handed over to the chief priests and scribes, condemned to death, handed over to the Gentiles (the Romans) to be mocked, flogged, and crucified, and on the third day He would be raised. For the first time Jesus specified that He would be crucified, the Roman method of execution reserved for slaves, rebels, and the worst criminals.

### A Request for Places of Honor (20:20–28)

Immediately after this solemn prediction, the mother of James and John (the sons of Zebedee) came to Jesus with her sons, knelt, and asked a favor. Some have suggested from comparing Matthew 27:56 with Mark 15:40 and John 19:25 that she may have been Salome, possibly the sister of Jesus' mother, which would make James and John Jesus' cousins, though this is uncertain. She asked that her two sons sit at Jesus' right and left in His kingdom, the positions of highest honor next to a king. Mark's account has James and John ask directly (Mark 10:35–37), showing that the request was theirs as well.

Jesus answered, [[v22|You don’t know what you are asking. Are you able to drink the cup that I am about to drink?]] The "cup" was an Old Testament image for suffering and especially for God's judgment (Isaiah 51:17; Jeremiah 25:15). Jesus was about to drink that cup on the cross. They confidently said, "We are able." Jesus told them they would indeed drink His cup. James was later executed by Herod Agrippa I (Acts 12:2), the first of the apostles to be martyred; John suffered persecution and exile (Revelation 1:9). But the places at His right and left were not His to give; they belonged to those for whom the Father had prepared them. Ironically, the next time Matthew mentions people on Jesus' right and left, they are two criminals crucified beside Him (Matthew 27:38).

The other ten disciples were indignant with the two brothers, probably because they wanted those positions themselves. Jesus called them together and contrasted the kingdom with the world's systems of power. Gentile rulers [[v25|lord it over them]], and their great ones exercise authority over others. [[v26|It shall not be so among you]]. Whoever wants to be great must be a servant, and whoever wants to be first must be a slave. A "bondservant" or slave had the lowest position in society, with no rights of his own. Jesus grounded this teaching in His own mission: the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many.

### Two Blind Men Near Jericho (20:29–34)

As Jesus and His disciples left Jericho, about fifteen miles northeast of Jerusalem, a large crowd followed. Pilgrims traveling to Jerusalem for Passover often passed through Jericho. Two blind men sitting by the road heard that Jesus was passing and cried out, [[v30|Lord, have mercy on us, you son of David!]] (Mark and Luke mention one man, Bartimaeus; Matthew, as he sometimes does, mentions two.) By calling Jesus "Son of David," they recognized Him as the Messiah, the heir of David's throne.

The crowd rebuked them and told them to be quiet. Blind beggars were considered a nuisance, and perhaps the crowd thought Jesus had more important things to do on His way to Jerusalem. But the men cried out even louder. Jesus stopped, called them, and asked, [[v32|What do you want me to do for you?]] They answered, [[v33|Lord, that our eyes may be opened]]. Moved with compassion, Jesus touched their eyes. Immediately they could see, and they followed Him.

The contrast with the request of James and John is striking. Jesus asks both groups essentially the same question (compare v. 21). The disciples, who could see physically, asked for power and position. The blind men asked for mercy. Their request was granted, and they followed Jesus on the road to Jerusalem, the road to the cross.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 20:15 — Resenting God's Generosity

verse: Matthew 20:15

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the landowner's reply to the workers who complained at the end of the day. They had worked from early morning, through the heat of the day, and received the denarius they had agreed to. But when they saw that those who had worked only one hour received the same amount, they grumbled. They felt they had been treated unfairly.

The landowner first pointed out that he had done them no wrong; he had paid them exactly what they agreed to (v. 13). Then he asked two questions. [[v15|Isn’t it lawful for me to do what I want to with what I own?]] The money was his, and he had the right to be generous with it. Paying the latecomers a full day's wage did not take anything away from those who worked longer.

The second question went deeper: [[v15|Or is your eye evil, because I am good?]] In Jewish idiom, an "evil eye" meant envy or stinginess, a resentful attitude toward someone else's good fortune (see Matthew 6:23). The landowner was exposing the real problem. The workers were not upset because they had been cheated, but because someone else had received generosity they felt was undeserved. Their envy had turned the owner's goodness into a cause for complaint.

Jesus' listeners would have understood the workers' complaint; it seems natural. That is why the parable is so powerful. It shows how human ideas of fairness, based on what people deserve, can clash with God's generosity, based on grace.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This parable reveals that God's kingdom is based on grace, not on merit. No one earns eternal life by working long and hard enough. Those who come to God late in life receive the same gift as those who have served Him for decades. The thief on the cross, who turned to Jesus in his last hours, received the promise of paradise (Luke 23:43).

The verse also exposes a danger for those who have followed God for a long time: resenting God's grace to others. Jesus tells a similar story in the parable of the prodigal son, where the older brother is angry when his father welcomes the younger son home with a celebration (Luke 15:25–32). Religious people can begin to think they deserve more than others because of their faithfulness, and then resent God's kindness to sinners.

Finally, the verse shows that envy is a spiritual problem. It distorts how we see God and others. Instead of rejoicing in God's goodness, envy complains about it. The cure for envy is remembering that we, too, have received grace we did not deserve.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine whether they ever resent God's generosity to others. This might show up in feeling bitter when someone who has lived a careless life comes to faith and is welcomed, when a new believer receives attention or opportunities, or when others are blessed in ways we feel we deserved more. Recognizing envy and confessing it is the first step toward freedom.

It also calls believers to rejoice in God's grace wherever it appears. When someone turns to God late in life, or when a person with a troubled past is restored, believers can celebrate rather than compare. Every person in God's kingdom is there by grace.

Finally, this verse encourages humility about service. Long years of faithfulness are good and will be rewarded, but they do not make anyone more deserving of God's love than others. Believers can serve with gratitude rather than with a sense of entitlement, remembering that the privilege of working in God's vineyard is itself a gift.

### Matthew 20:28 — The Son of Man Came to Serve

verse: Matthew 20:28

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to all twelve disciples after a conflict about status. James and John, along with their mother, had asked for the places of highest honor in Jesus' kingdom. The other ten disciples were angry with them, likely because they wanted those places too. Jesus had just told them for the third time that He was going to Jerusalem to be condemned, mocked, flogged, and crucified. Yet His disciples were arguing about who would be greatest.

Jesus called them together and explained that His kingdom works differently from worldly power. Among the nations, rulers dominate and use authority over others. Among His followers, greatness means serving, and being first means becoming a slave. Then Jesus gave the reason, pointing to Himself: [[v28|even as the Son of Man came not to be served, but to serve]].

"Son of Man" is the title from Daniel 7, where one like a son of man receives authority, glory, and a kingdom, and all peoples serve Him. Jesus is that glorious figure, and yet He came not to be served but to serve. The one who deserves the worship of all nations takes the role of a servant.

He adds, [[v28|and to give his life as a ransom for many]]. A ransom was a price paid to set someone free, such as a slave or a prisoner of war. Jesus is saying that His death will be the price that sets people free. "For many" echoes Isaiah 53:11–12, where the Suffering Servant bears the sins of many. The word "for" can mean "in place of," suggesting that Jesus' death is substitutionary: He dies in place of those He saves.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is one of the clearest statements in the Gospels about why Jesus came and why He died. His entire life was service, and His death was the ultimate act of service, giving His life to set others free. Christians have understood this ransom in various ways: as freedom from sin's power, from the penalty of guilt, and from death and the devil. The New Testament presents all these aspects, and Christians emphasize them differently. The central truth is that Jesus' death accomplished what humans could not do for themselves: their liberation and reconciliation with God.

This verse also shows that Jesus' example and His saving work belong together. He is the model for His followers' service, but He is more than a model. His service as a ransom is unique; no one else can give their life for the sins of many. Believers serve others in response to His saving service for them.

Finally, the verse redefines greatness. In God's kingdom, the greatest one of all, the Son of Man, is the one who serves most completely. Greatness is measured not by how many people serve us but by how fully we give ourselves to serve others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to adopt a servant mindset in every area of life. In families, it means putting others' needs before one's own. In churches, it means leaders who serve rather than dominate, and members who look for ways to help rather than seek recognition. In workplaces, it means working with integrity and caring for colleagues and customers, not merely seeking advancement.

It also challenges believers' views of leadership. Those who hold authority, whether parents, managers, pastors, or public officials, are called to use it for the good of others, not for personal gain. Jesus' words, "It shall not be so among you," rule out abusive, self-serving uses of power among His people.

Finally, this verse invites gratitude. Jesus gave His life as a ransom for many. Believers can receive this gift with thankfulness, and let that gratitude become the motivation for serving others. Service flows best not from duty or a desire for reward, but from knowing how much one has been served by Christ.

### Matthew 20:32 — [[Matthew 20:32|What Do You Want Me to Do for You?]]

verse: Matthew 20:32

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes in the final scene of the chapter. Jesus and His disciples were leaving Jericho with a large crowd, heading toward Jerusalem for Passover. Two blind men sitting by the road heard that Jesus was passing by and began shouting, [[v30|Lord, have mercy on us, you son of David!]] Calling Him "Son of David" showed that they recognized Him as the Messiah.

The crowd told them to be quiet. Perhaps they thought the blind beggars were embarrassing or unimportant, or that Jesus, on His way to Jerusalem, should not be bothered. But the men cried out even louder.

Then [[v32|Jesus stood still]]. Jesus was on His way to the most important events of His life: His death and resurrection. Yet He stopped for two blind beggars whom the crowd wanted to ignore. He called them and asked, "What do you want me to do for you?" Jesus surely knew what they needed, but He invited them to express their request. This gave them dignity and allowed them to state their faith.

They answered, [[v33|Lord, that our eyes may be opened]]. Matthew tells us Jesus was [[v34|moved with compassion]], touched their eyes, and immediately they could see. Then they followed Him, joining His journey toward Jerusalem.

The question in this verse echoes the question Jesus asked the mother of James and John in verse 21. She asked for power and position for her sons. The blind men asked for mercy and sight. Their request was granted, and they became followers.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus is never too busy for people in need. Even on His way to the cross, He stopped for those others overlooked. This reveals God's attentiveness to individuals, especially the poor and powerless. The crowd's attempt to silence the blind men stands in contrast to Jesus' welcome.

The story also illustrates the kind of prayer Jesus honors. The blind men's cry for mercy reflects the attitude of the poor in spirit, those who know they cannot help themselves. Their persistence, crying out louder despite opposition, recalls the Canaanite woman in Matthew 15. Jesus responds to such humble, persistent faith.

Spiritually, the healing of blindness often symbolizes the opening of eyes to see who Jesus is. The blind men could "see" that Jesus was the Son of David even before their physical sight was restored, while many with physical sight, including the religious leaders, remained spiritually blind. After receiving sight, the men followed Jesus, showing the response that true spiritual sight produces.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to bring their requests to Jesus honestly. He asks, "What do you want me to do for you?" Believers can answer specifically in prayer, naming their needs, hopes, and struggles. God already knows, but He welcomes His children's requests.

It also encourages persistence when others try to silence our cries for help. Sometimes people, circumstances, or even our own doubts tell us to stop praying or that our needs are not important. The blind men's example encourages believers to keep calling out to Jesus.

Finally, this verse challenges believers to stop for people in need. Like the crowd, it is easy to see certain people as interruptions. Jesus' example calls His followers to notice those who are overlooked, to listen to them, to ask what they need, and to respond with compassion.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 20 challenges the way people naturally think about fairness, status, and greatness. The parable of the vineyard workers teaches that God's kingdom is based on generous grace, not on earning and comparison. Believers can let go of the habit of measuring themselves against others and resenting God's kindness to people they think are less deserving. Everyone in God's kingdom is there by grace, and that is cause for celebration, not envy.

The request of James and John shows how easily even devoted followers can seek status and recognition. Jesus' answer redefines greatness as service. This applies in every sphere of life: family, church, work, and community. Believers can look for ways to serve others, especially in unnoticed ways, and can resist the desire for titles, positions, and praise. Those with authority are called to use it for the good of others, never to dominate.

At the center of the chapter is Jesus' statement that He came to serve and to give His life as a ransom for many. This is both the foundation of salvation and the model for Christian life. Believers can receive His sacrifice with gratitude and allow it to shape how they treat others.

Finally, the healing of the blind men shows Jesus' compassion for the overlooked. He stopped for those others tried to silence. Believers can bring their needs to Jesus with humble persistence, trusting His compassion. They can also follow His example by noticing and responding to people in need, even when it is inconvenient. Like the blind men who followed Jesus after receiving sight, those who have experienced His mercy are called to follow Him on the road He walked.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, thank You for the generous grace of Your kingdom, where those who come late are welcomed as fully as those who came early. Forgive me for the times I have compared myself with others or resented Your kindness to them. Help me to rejoice whenever anyone receives Your mercy, remembering that I too have received what I did not deserve.

Lord Jesus, thank You for coming not to be served but to serve, and for giving Your life as a ransom for many, including me. Free me from seeking status and recognition. Teach me to serve others gladly, and to use any influence I have for their good. Thank You for stopping for the blind men when others wanted them silenced. Help me to bring my needs to You honestly and persistently, to notice people others overlook, and to follow You wherever You lead. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
