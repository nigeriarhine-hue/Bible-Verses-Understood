# Acts 13
theme: Sent by the Spirit: The First Missionary Journey and the Good News of Justification
main: While the church in Antioch worships and fasts, the Holy Spirit calls Barnabas and Saul to a special work, and the church sends them out. On Cyprus they meet a Roman governor who believes despite the opposition of a sorcerer. In Pisidian Antioch, Paul preaches in the synagogue, tracing Israel's history to Jesus, the promised Son of David whom God raised from the dead, and announcing that through Him everyone who believes is forgiven and justified. When many Jews reject the message, Paul and Barnabas turn to the Gentiles, who rejoice and believe, and though the missionaries are driven out, the disciples are filled with joy and the Holy Spirit.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 13 marks the beginning of the second half of the book. The focus shifts from Jerusalem and Peter to Antioch and Paul, and from the church's spread in Judea and Samaria to its deliberate mission to the wider Gentile world. This chapter begins what is usually called Paul's first missionary journey, which took place around A.D. 46–48 and covered the island of Cyprus and the region of Galatia in central Asia Minor (modern Turkey).

In this chapter, Luke begins calling Saul by his Roman name, Paul (13:9). Many Jews of the time had both a Hebrew name and a Greek or Latin name. As Saul began his mission to the Gentile world, he used the name that would be more familiar to them.

### The Spirit Sends Barnabas and Saul (13:1–3)

The church in Antioch had a remarkably diverse group of leaders: [[v1|Barnabas, Simeon who was called Niger, Lucius of Cyrene, Manaen the foster brother of Herod the tetrarch, and Saul]]. Barnabas was a Levite from Cyprus. Simeon's nickname "Niger" means "black," suggesting he may have come from Africa. Lucius was from Cyrene in North Africa. Manaen had been raised in the court of Herod Antipas, the ruler who had executed John the Baptist. Saul was a former Pharisee from Tarsus. Their diversity reflected the multicultural church in Antioch. Their calling is studied below.

### Cyprus: The Proconsul Believes (13:4–12)

[[v4|So, being sent out by the Holy Spirit]], Barnabas and Saul sailed to Cyprus, Barnabas's home island, taking John Mark as their helper. They preached in the synagogues of Salamis and traveled across the island to Paphos, the Roman capital.

There they met [[v6|a certain sorcerer, a false prophet, a Jew whose name was Bar Jesus]], also called Elymas. He was an advisor to the proconsul (the Roman governor), [[v7|Sergius Paulus, a man of understanding]], who wanted to hear the word of God. But Elymas opposed them, trying to turn the proconsul from the faith.

Then [[v9|Saul, who is also called Paul, filled with the Holy Spirit]], looked at Elymas and rebuked him sharply: [[v10|You son of the devil, full of all deceit and all cunning, you enemy of all righteousness, will you not cease to pervert the right ways of the Lord?]] There is irony in this: the man called "Bar Jesus," meaning "son of Joshua" or "son of salvation," was in fact acting as a son of the devil. Paul declared that the hand of the Lord was against him, and he would be blind for a time. [[v11|Immediately a mist and darkness fell on him. He went around seeking someone to lead him by the hand]]. Paul himself had once been struck blind and led by the hand (9:8). The temporary blindness may have been meant, as in Paul's case, to lead Elymas to repentance.

[[v12|Then the proconsul, when he saw what was done, believed, being astonished at the teaching of the Lord]]. A high Roman official came to faith.

### Perga: John Mark Leaves (13:13)

Paul and his companions sailed from Paphos to Perga in Pamphylia, on the southern coast of Asia Minor. There [[v13|John departed from them and returned to Jerusalem]]. Luke does not say why. Some suggest he was homesick, afraid of the dangerous journey inland, unhappy that Paul was taking the lead from his cousin Barnabas (Colossians 4:10), or uneasy about the mission to Gentiles. Whatever the reason, Paul saw it as desertion, and it would later cause a sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:37–39). Yet later, Paul and Mark were reconciled, and Paul wrote, [[2 Timothy 4:11|Take Mark, and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for service]].

### Paul's Sermon in Pisidian Antioch (13:14–41)

From Perga, Paul and Barnabas traveled about one hundred miles inland, over the Taurus Mountains, to Antioch in Pisidia (a different city from Antioch in Syria). On the Sabbath, they went to the synagogue. After the reading from the Law and the Prophets, the synagogue leaders invited them to speak. This was a common custom, and visiting teachers were often invited to give a word of encouragement.

Paul's sermon, the first of his recorded sermons, was addressed to [[v16|Men of Israel, and you who fear God]], both Jews and Gentile God-fearers. He reviewed Israel's history: God chose their ancestors, made them great in Egypt, led them out with mighty power, endured them in the wilderness for forty years, gave them the land of Canaan, provided judges, and then gave them kings. The first king, Saul, was removed, and God raised up David. God's testimony about David is studied below.

[[v23|From this man’s offspring, God has brought salvation to Israel according to his promise]]. The promised descendant of David is Jesus. John the Baptist had prepared the way, saying he was not worthy to untie the sandals of the One coming after him.

Paul then explained that the people of Jerusalem and their rulers did not recognize Jesus or understand the prophets read every Sabbath, and so they fulfilled the prophecies by condemning Him. Though they found no grounds for death, they asked Pilate to execute Him. [[v30|But God raised him from the dead]]. Many witnesses saw Him.

Paul supported the resurrection with three Old Testament passages. Psalm 2:7: [[v33|You are my Son. Today I have become your father]], which Paul understood to be fulfilled in Jesus' resurrection, when God publicly declared Him to be the Son of God with power (Romans 1:4). Isaiah 55:3: [[v34|I will give you the holy and sure blessings of David]]. And Psalm 16:10: [[v35|You will not allow your Holy One to see decay]]. Like Peter at Pentecost (Acts 2:29–31), Paul pointed out that David died and his body decayed, but Jesus, whom God raised, did not.

Paul then announced the heart of the gospel, studied below. He closed with a warning from Habakkuk 1:5 not to scoff at God's work.

### Jews and Gentiles Respond (13:42–52)

As they left, the people begged them to speak again the next Sabbath. Many Jews and devout converts to Judaism followed Paul and Barnabas, who [[v43|urged them to continue in the grace of God]].

The next Sabbath, [[v44|almost the whole city was gathered together to hear the word of God]]. But when some of the Jewish leaders saw the crowds, [[v45|they were filled with jealousy]] and contradicted Paul, speaking abusively. Paul and Barnabas answered boldly: [[v46|It was necessary that God’s word should be spoken to you first. Since indeed you thrust it from yourselves, and judge yourselves unworthy of eternal life, behold, we turn to the Gentiles]]. Paul's pattern throughout his ministry was to go first to the synagogue, because the gospel was the fulfillment of God's promises to Israel (Romans 1:16). When Jewish audiences rejected it, he turned to the Gentiles. Their response is studied below.

The Jewish leaders stirred up prominent women and leading men of the city to persecute Paul and Barnabas, and they were driven out. [[v51|But they shook off the dust of their feet against them]], as Jesus had instructed His disciples to do when a town rejected them (Luke 9:5). They went on to Iconium. Yet the chapter ends on a note of joy: [[v52|The disciples were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit]]. The new believers left behind were not discouraged.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 13:2–3 — Separate Barnabas and Saul for Me

verse: Acts 13:2-3

#### Simple Explanation

The church in Antioch had grown into a large and diverse community. Its leaders included prophets and teachers from different backgrounds. One day, [[v2|As they served the Lord and fasted]]. The word "served" here is often used of worship and priestly service. The leaders were devoting themselves to worship and prayer, and they were fasting, setting aside food to focus on God.

In this setting of worship, [[v2|the Holy Spirit said, “Separate Barnabas and Saul for me, for the work to which I have called them.”]] Luke does not say how the Spirit spoke. It may have been through one of the prophets. The message was clear: God had a special task for Barnabas and Saul.

Notice the words "for me." Barnabas and Saul were being set apart for God's purposes. And notice "to which I have called them." God had already called them; the church was recognizing and confirming that call. Saul had been told at his conversion that he would carry Christ's name to the Gentiles (9:15).

[[v3|Then, when they had fasted and prayed and laid their hands on them, they sent them away]]. The church responded with more fasting and prayer. Then they laid their hands on Barnabas and Saul, a sign of blessing, commissioning, and identification. The church sent them out.

It is worth noting that the church sent two of its best leaders. Barnabas and Saul had been teaching the church for over a year. Sending them was a sacrifice. But the church in Antioch was willing to give up its best for the sake of God's mission.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that mission begins with God. The Holy Spirit took the initiative, calling Barnabas and Saul. Mission is not merely a human strategy; it is God's work, carried out through people He calls and sends. Jesus had said, [[John 20:21|As the Father has sent me, even so I send you]].

They also show the role of the church in confirming and sending. The Spirit called, and the church responded by praying, laying on hands, and sending. Individual calling and community confirmation work together. Paul later asked, [[Romans 10:15|And how will they preach unless they are sent?]]

Finally, these verses show the importance of worship, prayer, and fasting in discerning God's will. The church heard the Spirit's voice as they worshiped and fasted. Seeking God's guidance involves drawing near to Him in devotion.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage churches to be places of worship, prayer, and fasting, where believers seek God's direction together. Churches can set aside time to listen to God, especially when making important decisions.

They also encourage churches to be generous in sending. Antioch sent its best leaders. Churches today can support missionaries and new works, even when it means giving up gifted people or resources. A sending church shares in the fruit of the mission.

Finally, these verses invite each believer to be open to God's calling. God calls people to many different kinds of service, at home and abroad. Believers can seek God's guidance through prayer and the counsel of the church, ready to go where He leads.

### Acts 13:22 — A Man After My Heart

verse: Acts 13:22

#### Simple Explanation

In his sermon in the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch, Paul reviewed Israel's history to show how it led to Jesus. He described how God chose Israel's ancestors, brought them out of Egypt, gave them the land, and provided judges. Then the people asked for a king, and God gave them Saul. But Saul disobeyed God, and God removed him.

[[v22|When he had removed him, he raised up David to be their king]]. God chose David, the youngest son of Jesse, a shepherd boy from Bethlehem, to be king.

Then Paul quoted God's testimony about David: [[v22|I have found David the son of Jesse, a man after my heart, who will do all my will]]. These words combine phrases from 1 Samuel 13:14, Psalm 89:20, and Isaiah 44:28. "A man after my heart" means a man whose heart was aligned with God's heart, someone who loved God and sought to do His will.

David was not perfect. He committed serious sins, including adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of her husband Uriah (2 Samuel 11). But when confronted, David repented deeply (Psalm 51). His heart was turned toward God. Unlike Saul, who made excuses, David humbly confessed and sought God's mercy.

Paul mentioned David because God had promised that the Messiah would come from David's family. The next verse says, [[v23|From this man’s offspring, God has brought salvation to Israel according to his promise]]. Jesus is the Son of David, the King who fulfills all God's promises to David.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows what God values in a person: a heart turned toward Him. When Samuel was looking for the next king, God told him that He does not look at outward appearance but at the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). David was chosen not because of his size, age, or status, but because of his heart.

It also shows that God's purposes move forward through imperfect people. David's failures were real and serious, yet God kept His promises to him. This reveals God's grace and faithfulness.

Finally, the verse points to Jesus, the true King after God's own heart. David's heart was devoted to God, but imperfectly. Jesus perfectly did the Father's will in everything. He is the Son of David who reigns forever, fulfilling God's promise that David's throne would endure (2 Samuel 7:12–16).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to seek a heart after God's heart. This means loving God, desiring to do His will, and responding to Him with humility and repentance when they fail. God is more concerned with the heart than with outward achievements.

It also encourages believers who have failed. David sinned greatly, yet his repentance was genuine, and God forgave him. Believers who have fallen can return to God with honest confession, trusting His mercy.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that Jesus is the King after God's own heart. Believers can look to Him as their perfect example and their faithful King, the One in whom all God's promises are fulfilled.

### Acts 13:38–39 — Justified by Him

verse: Acts 13:38-39

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had shown his listeners in the synagogue that Jesus was the promised descendant of David, that He had been condemned and crucified, and that God had raised Him from the dead. Now Paul came to the heart of his message: what this means for them.

[[v38|Be it known to you therefore, brothers, that through this man is proclaimed to you remission of sins]]. "Remission" means forgiveness. Through Jesus, forgiveness is offered. Paul emphasized "this man," Jesus, as the one through whom forgiveness comes.

[[v39|and by him everyone who believes is justified from all things, from which you could not be justified by the law of Moses]]. "Justified" is a legal term meaning "declared righteous" or "acquitted." A person who is justified is declared not guilty before God and accepted as righteous. This is the first time in Acts that this key word, central to Paul's letters, appears.

Notice three important points. First, justification is "by him," through Jesus. Second, it is for "everyone who believes." It is received by faith, not earned. Third, it covers "all things," everything from which the Law of Moses could not free people. The Law could reveal sin and point to the need for forgiveness, but it could not make people fully right with God. Jesus does what the Law could not do.

These words were spoken in a synagogue, to Jews and God-fearers who honored the Law. Paul was not saying the Law was bad; he was saying that it could not justify. Only faith in Jesus brings complete forgiveness and right standing with God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses summarize the gospel that Paul would explain in detail in his letters to the Romans and Galatians. Paul writes, [[Romans 3:28|We maintain therefore that a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law]]. Justification is a gift of God's grace received through faith in Christ.

They also show that justification is complete. Believers are justified "from all things." There is no sin too great to be covered by Christ's work. Paul writes, [[Romans 8:1|There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus]].

Finally, these verses show the relationship between the Law and the gospel. The Law is good and holy, but it cannot save. It shows people their sin and their need for a Savior. The gospel announces that the Savior has come, and that through faith in Him, people are declared righteous.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses give believers assurance. Many people struggle with guilt and wonder whether they are truly accepted by God. Paul's message is clear: everyone who believes in Jesus is justified from all things. Believers can rest in this complete forgiveness.

They also caution against trying to earn God's acceptance. Some believers try to make themselves acceptable to God through rule-keeping, religious activity, or good works. These things have their place as expressions of love for God, but they cannot justify. Only faith in Christ does that.

Finally, these verses invite everyone to believe. Justification is offered to "everyone who believes." No one is excluded. Believers can share this good news with confidence, knowing that it is for all.

### Acts 13:47–48 — A Light for the Gentiles

verse: Acts 13:47-48

#### Simple Explanation

Paul and Barnabas had preached in the synagogue in Pisidian Antioch, and the following Sabbath almost the whole city came to hear them. But some of the Jewish leaders became jealous and opposed them. Paul and Barnabas declared that since these leaders had rejected the message, they would turn to the Gentiles.

They explained their decision by quoting Scripture: [[v47|For so has the Lord commanded us, saying, ‘I have set you as a light for the Gentiles, that you should bring salvation to the uttermost parts of the earth.’]] This quotation comes from Isaiah 49:6, which describes the mission of God's Servant. The Servant would not only restore Israel but also bring God's light to the nations. Paul and Barnabas saw their mission to the Gentiles as part of the Servant's mission, which Jesus fulfilled and now carried out through His followers.

[[v48|As the Gentiles heard this, they were glad and glorified the word of God]]. The Gentiles rejoiced when they heard that the gospel was for them too. They had been on the outside of Israel's covenant, but now they were invited in.

[[v48|As many as were appointed to eternal life believed]]. Many Gentiles believed. Luke describes them as those "appointed to eternal life."

[[v49|The Lord’s word was spread abroad throughout all the region]]. The gospel spread throughout the surrounding area.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the mission to the Gentiles was rooted in God's original plan. From the beginning, God intended to bless all nations through Abraham's offspring (Genesis 12:3). The prophets foresaw a day when God's light would reach the ends of the earth. The church's mission to the Gentiles was not a new idea but the fulfillment of Scripture.

The phrase "as many as were appointed to eternal life believed" has been understood in different ways. Many Christians, especially in the Reformed tradition, see it as teaching that God's sovereign choice lies behind faith: those whom God had appointed believed. Others, especially in the Wesleyan and Arminian traditions, emphasize that the Greek word can mean "disposed" or "ordered" toward eternal life, describing those whose hearts were open to God's call, and they stress human responsibility to respond. Both perspectives affirm that salvation is God's gracious work and that people are called to believe. Notably, the previous verse described the opposing leaders as judging themselves unworthy of eternal life (13:46), highlighting human responsibility alongside God's sovereignty.

Finally, the verses show the joy of the gospel. The Gentiles were glad and glorified God. The good news is truly good news that brings joy to those who receive it.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses remind believers that they are called to be a light to the nations. The mission given to the Servant and carried out by Paul continues through the church today. Believers can support and participate in sharing the gospel with people of every nation, both near and far.

They also encourage believers when some reject the message. Paul and Barnabas faced opposition, but they did not give up. They turned to others who were open. When some people reject the gospel, believers can continue to share it with others whose hearts God may be preparing.

Finally, these verses invite believers to rejoice in the gospel. Those who were once far from God have been brought near. Believers can join the Gentiles of Antioch in being glad and glorifying God's word.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 13 shows that God's mission begins with God. The Holy Spirit called Barnabas and Saul, and the church in Antioch responded by praying, fasting, and sending them. Churches today can follow this example, seeking God's direction in worship and prayer, recognizing those He calls, and generously sending and supporting them.

The chapter also shows that the gospel meets opposition. Elymas tried to turn the proconsul away from the faith, and jealous leaders in Pisidian Antioch stirred up persecution. Yet in both places, God's word prevailed. Believers can face opposition with courage, trusting that God is at work even when others resist.

Acts 13 presents the heart of the gospel in Paul's first recorded sermon: Jesus, the Son of David, was crucified and raised, and through Him everyone who believes is forgiven and justified from all things. This message is for everyone, Jews and Gentiles alike. Believers can rest in the assurance of their justification and share this good news with confidence.

Finally, the chapter shows the gospel going to the nations as a light to the Gentiles. Even when Paul and Barnabas were driven out, the new believers were filled with joy and the Holy Spirit. Believers today can carry that light wherever they go, finding joy in the gospel and sharing it with all people.

## Chapter Prayer

Holy Spirit, thank You for calling and sending Your servants into the world. Make Your church a people who worship, fast, and pray, listening for Your voice. Show me where You are calling me to serve, and give me a willing heart to go. Thank You, Father, for David, a man after Your heart, and for Jesus, the Son of David, who fulfills all Your promises. Thank You that through Jesus my sins are forgiven and I am justified from all things, not by my own works but by faith in Him. Help me to rest in this grace and to share it with others. Make me a light to those around me, and fill me with joy and with the Holy Spirit, even when I face opposition. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
