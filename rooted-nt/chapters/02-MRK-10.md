# Mark 10
theme: On the Road to Jerusalem: Receiving the Kingdom Like a Child
main: As Jesus travels toward Jerusalem, He teaches about marriage, welcomes children, and lovingly calls a rich man to give up his wealth and follow Him. He warns that riches make entering the kingdom hard, promises that no sacrifice for Him will be lost, predicts His death for the third time, and corrects His disciples' desire for status by declaring that He came to serve and give His life as a ransom. The chapter ends with blind Bartimaeus throwing off his cloak, receiving his sight, and following Jesus on the road.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 10 continues Jesus' journey south from Galilee toward Jerusalem, where He will be crucified. Mark often describes Jesus and His disciples as being "on the way" (vv. 17, 32, 46, 52). The phrase is literal, describing their travel, but it also carries a deeper meaning: following Jesus means walking with Him on the way to the cross. The early Christians were sometimes called followers of [[Acts 9:2|the Way]].

This chapter parallels Matthew 19–20, but Mark adds vivid personal details, such as Jesus' indignation at the disciples, His love for the rich man, and the name and actions of the blind beggar Bartimaeus. The chapter is also framed by two encounters with Jesus' question, "What do you want me to do for you?" (vv. 36, 51), asked first to James and John, who want power, and then to Bartimaeus, who wants sight.

### Teaching About Divorce (10:1–12)

Jesus left Galilee and went into Judea and the region east of the Jordan River. Crowds gathered again, and He taught them. Some Pharisees came to test Him with a question: [[v2|Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife?]] This region was ruled by Herod Antipas, who had divorced his wife to marry Herodias and had executed John the Baptist for criticizing that marriage. The question may have been a trap.

Jesus asked what Moses commanded. They answered that Moses permitted a man to write a certificate of divorce and send his wife away (Deuteronomy 24:1–4). Jesus explained that Moses wrote this command [[v5|For your hardness of heart]]. The law regulated divorce in a sinful world, but it did not represent God's original design. Jesus pointed back to creation: God made humanity male and female, and a man leaves his parents and is joined to his wife, and the two become one flesh. Therefore, [[v9|What therefore God has joined together, let no man separate]].

Later, in the house, the disciples asked Him about this again. Jesus said that whoever divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery against her, and a woman who divorces her husband and marries another commits adultery. Mark's version mentions a woman divorcing her husband, which was possible under Roman law but rarely under Jewish law; Mark's Gentile readers in Rome would have needed this application. Notice also that Jesus said a man who divorces commits adultery "against her," treating the wife as a person who could be wronged, at a time when many viewed adultery mainly as an offense against a husband. Mark does not include the exception for sexual immorality found in Matthew. (Christian traditions differ on how to apply these teachings, including in cases of abandonment and abuse; see the discussion at Matthew 19:6. Jesus' teaching protects the vulnerable and must not be used to keep anyone in danger.)

### Jesus Blesses the Children (10:13–16)

People were bringing little children to Jesus so that He would touch them. The disciples rebuked them, perhaps thinking Jesus was too important or busy for children. Mark alone tells us that Jesus [[v14|was moved with indignation]] at the disciples. He said, [[v14|Allow the little children to come to me! Don’t forbid them, for God’s Kingdom belongs to such as these]]. Then He added a principle studied below. Mark also tells us that Jesus took the children in His arms, laid His hands on them, and blessed them. His tenderness toward children contrasts with His disciples' dismissiveness.

### The Rich Man (10:17–27)

As Jesus set out on His way, a man ran up, knelt before Him, and asked, [[v17|Good Teacher, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?]] Matthew calls him young, and Luke calls him a ruler. His running and kneeling showed eagerness and respect.

Jesus asked why he called Him good, since no one is good except God alone. Then He reminded him of the commandments dealing with relationships with others, including one unique to Mark's list: [[v19|Do not defraud]], perhaps a pointed reference to how wealth could be gained unjustly. The man said he had kept all these since his youth.

Then Mark records something the other Gospels omit: [[v21|Jesus looking at him loved him]]. Jesus told him he lacked one thing: he should sell everything, give to the poor, and he would have treasure in heaven; then he should come and follow Jesus. The man's face fell, and he went away sad, because he had great wealth. This verse is studied below.

Jesus looked around and said to His disciples how hard it is for the wealthy to enter God's kingdom. The disciples were amazed, because many people assumed wealth was a sign of God's blessing. Jesus repeated, calling them [[v24|Children]], a tender term, and said that it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter God's kingdom. The disciples were even more astonished: [[v26|Then who can be saved?]] Jesus looked at them and said, [[v27|With men it is impossible, but not with God, for all things are possible with God]]. (These sayings are studied at Matthew 19:21 and 19:26.)

### The Reward of Following Jesus (10:28–31)

Peter said, [[v28|Behold, we have left all and have followed you]]. Jesus promised that no one who had left home, family, or land for His sake and the gospel's would fail to receive a hundred times as much in this present age, including houses, brothers, sisters, mothers, children, and fields, [[v30|with persecutions]], and in the age to come, eternal life.

This promise shows that those who leave family and possessions for Christ gain a new, much larger family: the community of believers who share homes, resources, and love. Mark alone adds "with persecutions," a realistic reminder that following Jesus brings hardship as well as blessing. Mark's first readers in Rome knew this well. Jesus ended with a reversal: [[v31|many who are first will be last, and the last first]].

### The Third Prediction of the Passion (10:32–34)

Mark gives a striking picture: they were on the road going up to Jerusalem, with Jesus walking ahead of them. The disciples were astonished, and those who followed were afraid. Jesus was walking deliberately toward His death, and His followers sensed the danger.

Jesus took the Twelve aside and told them, for the third time, what would happen. This is the most detailed of His predictions: He would be handed over to the chief priests and scribes, condemned to death, handed over to the Gentiles (the Romans), mocked, spat on, flogged, and killed. And on the third day He would rise again. Every detail would be fulfilled in Mark 14–16.

### James and John's Request (10:35–45)

Immediately after this prediction, James and John came to Jesus and said, [[v35|Teacher, we want you to do for us whatever we will ask]]. Jesus asked, [[v36|What do you want me to do for you?]] They asked to sit at His right and left in His glory, the positions of highest honor. (In Matthew's account, their mother made the request on their behalf.)

Jesus told them they did not know what they were asking, and He asked whether they could drink His cup and be baptized with His baptism. The "cup" was an Old Testament image for suffering and God's judgment, and "baptism" here pictures being overwhelmed or immersed in suffering. They confidently said they could. Jesus told them they would indeed share His suffering, but the places at His right and left were not His to give.

The other ten were indignant with James and John. Jesus called them together and contrasted the way of the world, where rulers lord it over others, with the way of His kingdom, where the great are servants and the first are slaves of all. He concluded, [[v45|For the Son of Man also came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many]]. (This verse, central to Mark's Gospel, is studied in depth at Matthew 20:28.)

### Blind Bartimaeus (10:46–52)

They came to Jericho, about fifteen miles northeast of Jerusalem, the last major stop before the climb to the city. As Jesus was leaving with His disciples and a large crowd, a blind beggar named Bartimaeus, the son of Timaeus, was sitting by the road. Mark is the only Gospel writer to give his name, which suggests he may have become known to the early church.

When Bartimaeus heard that Jesus of Nazareth was passing by, he began to shout, [[v47|Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!]] "Son of David" was a title for the Messiah, the heir to David's throne. Bartimaeus, though physically blind, saw who Jesus was. Many people rebuked him and told him to be quiet, but he shouted even louder.

Jesus stopped and said, "Call him." The people now said to Bartimaeus, [[v49|Cheer up! Get up. He is calling you!]] Bartimaeus threw off his cloak, jumped up, and came to Jesus. This is studied below. Jesus asked him, [[v51|What do you want me to do for you?]], the same question He had asked James and John. Bartimaeus answered, [[v51|Rabboni, that I may see again]]. "Rabboni" means "my teacher," a respectful and personal title. Jesus said, [[v52|Go your way. Your faith has made you well]]. Immediately he received his sight [[v52|and followed Jesus on the way]].

The final phrase is significant. Bartimaeus followed Jesus "on the way," the road to Jerusalem and the cross. He became a model disciple: seeing Jesus clearly, asking for mercy rather than status, and following Him.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 10:15 — Receiving the Kingdom Like a Little Child

verse: Mark 10:15

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words after His disciples tried to stop people from bringing children to Him. Parents were bringing their little children so that Jesus would touch them and bless them, a common practice with respected teachers. But the disciples rebuked them. Perhaps they thought Jesus had more important things to do, or that children were not worth His time.

Mark tells us that when Jesus saw this, He was [[v14|moved with indignation]]. This is the only time in the Gospels that Jesus is described as indignant with His own disciples. He told them to let the children come and not to hinder them, [[v14|for God’s Kingdom belongs to such as these]].

Then Jesus added the solemn statement in this verse: [[v15|whoever will not receive God’s Kingdom like a little child, he will in no way enter into it]]. "Receive" means to welcome or accept a gift. The kingdom of God is not earned or achieved; it is received. Jesus says it must be received the way a little child receives.

How does a child receive? In Jesus' culture, children were dependent and had no status or achievements to offer. A child receives gifts simply and gladly, without thinking they must earn them. A child trusts the giver. This is the attitude Jesus calls for: humble dependence, trust, and openness to God's gift.

The next verse adds a beautiful scene: Jesus [[v16|took them in his arms and blessed them, laying his hands on them]]. Jesus showed by His actions that the children were welcome.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that entrance into God's kingdom is by grace, received with humble trust. No one can enter by their own merit, achievement, or status. This connects directly with the next scene, where a rich man asks what he must do to inherit eternal life. He comes thinking of achievement; Jesus has just said the kingdom must be received like a child.

The verse also shows the value Jesus places on children. In a culture that often overlooked them, Jesus welcomed, embraced, and blessed them. He made them examples of how to receive the kingdom. This reveals God's heart for the small, weak, and dependent.

Finally, Jesus' indignation shows that hindering people from coming to Him is a serious matter. The disciples thought they were protecting Jesus' time, but they were blocking the very people who represented the kingdom. Religious people can sometimes become obstacles to others coming to Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to approach God with childlike trust. Rather than trying to earn God's favor or relying on accomplishments, believers can receive His grace as a gift. This means admitting dependence on Him, trusting His goodness, and coming to Him with openness rather than pride.

It also calls believers to value children and help them come to Jesus. Churches and families can make room for children, take their faith seriously, teach them about Jesus, and show them His love. Children are not merely the church of the future; they are welcomed by Jesus now.

Finally, this verse warns against hindering anyone from coming to Christ. Believers can examine whether their attitudes, traditions, or behavior make it harder for others, whether children, newcomers, or people from different backgrounds, to come to Jesus. Jesus wants all to come to Him.

### Mark 10:21 — Jesus Looked at Him and Loved Him

verse: Mark 10:21

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the heart of Jesus' encounter with the rich man. The man had run up to Jesus, knelt, and asked what he must do to inherit eternal life. Jesus reminded him of the commandments about relationships with others, and the man said he had kept them all since his youth. He seems to have been sincere, a man who had tried to live a good life.

Then Mark gives a detail the other Gospels do not include: [[v21|Jesus looking at him loved him]]. Jesus looked carefully at this man, seeing his sincerity and his need, and He felt genuine love for him. Everything Jesus said next was spoken in love.

[[v21|One thing you lack]]. Despite his moral life, the man was missing something essential. Jesus identified it: [[v21|Go, sell whatever you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me, taking up the cross]]. (The last phrase is found in some manuscripts.)

The command had several parts. Selling his possessions would free him from what was holding his heart. Giving to the poor would express love for his neighbor in a costly way. Treasure in heaven would replace earthly treasure. And following Jesus was the ultimate goal. The man's wealth had become an idol that kept him from wholehearted devotion to God.

The man's response was heartbreaking: [[v22|But his face fell at that saying, and he went away sorrowful, for he was one who had great possessions]]. He wanted eternal life, but not at the cost of his wealth.

Matthew's version of this story is studied at Matthew 19:21, focusing on the danger of wealth. Mark's detail about Jesus' love adds another dimension: even His most demanding call comes from love.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus' commands, even the hard ones, come from love. Jesus did not ask the man to give up his wealth to punish him or to make following Him difficult. He asked because He loved him and saw what was keeping him from true life. Jesus' call to give up idols is an invitation to freedom.

It also shows that Jesus sees each person clearly. He knew exactly what this man needed to hear. For another person, the "one thing" might be different: a relationship, a habit, an ambition, or a grudge. Jesus lovingly identifies what stands between each person and wholehearted devotion to God.

Finally, the man's sad departure shows that Jesus does not force anyone to follow Him. He loved the man, but He let him go. Love respects the freedom to choose, even when the choice is tragic. Mark does not tell us whether the man later changed his mind; the story leaves the reader to consider their own response.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to trust that Jesus' commands come from love. When God asks them to give up something, whether a possession, a habit, a relationship, or a plan, they can trust that He is not trying to take away their joy but to give them something better. His demands are expressions of His love.

It also invites believers to ask what "one thing" might be holding them back. Many people live respectable lives but hold onto something that competes with God for their hearts. Believers can ask Jesus to show them what that is and to give them courage to let it go.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to speak hard truths in love. Jesus told the man something difficult, but He did it from genuine love. When believers need to challenge someone, they can follow Jesus' example, looking at the person with love and speaking truth for their good, not to condemn them.

### Mark 10:50 — Throwing Off the Cloak

verse: Mark 10:50

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes Bartimaeus's response when Jesus called him. Bartimaeus was a blind beggar sitting by the road outside Jericho. When he heard that Jesus of Nazareth was passing by, he began shouting, [[v47|Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!]] The crowd told him to be quiet, but he shouted even louder. Jesus stopped and told the people to call him. The same people who had told him to be quiet now said, [[v49|Cheer up! Get up. He is calling you!]]

Bartimaeus responded immediately: [[v50|He, casting away his cloak, sprang up, and came to Jesus]]. Each action is significant.

[[v50|casting away his cloak]]. For a poor beggar, a cloak was one of his most important possessions. It kept him warm at night and served as his blanket. He may also have spread it on the ground to collect coins from passersby. The law of Moses protected a poor person's cloak, requiring that it be returned before sunset if taken as a pledge (Exodus 22:26–27). Throwing it aside showed his eagerness to get to Jesus and his willingness to leave behind what had been his security. A blind man who throws aside his cloak may not be able to find it again.

[[v50|sprang up]]. He did not hesitate or move slowly. He jumped up with excitement and hope.

[[v50|came to Jesus]]. Though blind, he made his way to Jesus, likely guided by the voices of those around him.

Jesus asked him, "What do you want me to do for you?" Bartimaeus answered, "Rabboni, that I may see again." Jesus told him his faith had made him well. Immediately he received his sight and followed Jesus on the way.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse provides a picture of how people respond to Jesus' call. Bartimaeus's actions contrast sharply with the rich man earlier in the chapter. The rich man came to Jesus eagerly but went away sad because he could not let go of his great possessions. Bartimaeus, a poor beggar, threw away his only possession to come to Jesus. The rich man held onto his wealth; the beggar let go of his cloak.

It also shows the contrast with James and John. When Jesus asked them, "What do you want me to do for you?", they asked for positions of power. When He asked Bartimaeus the same question, he asked for mercy and sight. Bartimaeus understood his need and came to Jesus with humility.

Finally, Bartimaeus's story ends with discipleship. He did not simply receive healing and go home; he followed Jesus "on the way," the road to Jerusalem and the cross. His healing led to following. He becomes a model for all believers: recognizing Jesus, crying out for mercy, letting go of what holds us back, and following Him.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to respond to Jesus' call with eagerness. When Jesus calls, through Scripture, through conviction, or through an opportunity to serve, believers can respond without delay, as Bartimaeus did.

It also challenges believers to consider what "cloak" they might need to throw aside to come to Jesus. It may be a source of security, a habit, a way of thinking, or something that seems necessary but is holding them back. Letting go of such things can be frightening, but Jesus offers far more than what we leave behind.

Finally, Bartimaeus's persistence encourages believers to keep calling out to Jesus even when others discourage them. Many people face voices that tell them to be quiet about their faith or their needs. Bartimaeus shouted louder. Believers can keep seeking Jesus and bringing their needs to Him, trusting that He will stop and call them.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 10 is about what it means to follow Jesus "on the way" to Jerusalem and the cross. Jesus' teaching on marriage calls believers to honor God's design for lifelong covenant faithfulness, while His treatment of women as persons who can be wronged reminds them that this teaching protects the vulnerable rather than trapping them in harm.

His welcome of children shows that the kingdom must be received with humble, trusting dependence. Believers can approach God not with achievements but with open hands, and they can make room for children and others who are easily overlooked, never hindering anyone from coming to Jesus.

The rich man's story and Jesus' teaching on wealth confront believers with the power possessions can have over the heart. Jesus looked at the man with love and called him to let go of what held him. Believers can ask what holds their hearts, trust that Jesus' demands come from love, and remember that salvation, though impossible for people, is possible with God. They can also trust Jesus' promise that nothing given up for Him is lost, even though following Him includes persecutions.

James and John's request and the disciples' indignation show how easily even devoted followers seek status. Jesus' answer, that He came to serve and give His life as a ransom, redefines greatness for every believer. Finally, Bartimaeus shows the response Jesus desires: recognizing Him, crying out for mercy, throwing aside whatever holds us back, and following Him on the way. Believers can walk the road of discipleship with the same eagerness and humility.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for welcoming little children into Your arms. Teach me to receive Your kingdom like a child, with humble trust and open hands, and keep me from ever hindering others from coming to You. Thank You for looking at me with love, even when You call me to give up what I hold too tightly. Show me the one thing that stands between me and wholehearted devotion to You, and give me courage to let it go.

Free me from seeking greatness and status. Teach me to serve as You served, You who gave Your life as a ransom for many. Like Bartimaeus, I cry out for Your mercy. Help me to throw aside whatever holds me back, to come to You eagerly, and to follow You on the way, wherever it leads. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
