# Matthew 7
theme: Two Ways, Two Trees, Two Foundations
main: Jesus concludes the Sermon on the Mount by warning against hypocritical judgment, encouraging persistent prayer to a generous Father, and summarizing God's will in the Golden Rule. He then presents a series of choices: the narrow and the wide road, true and false prophets, genuine and empty confession, and building on rock or on sand. Hearing His words is not enough; the wise person puts them into practice.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 7 is the final chapter of the Sermon on the Mount, which began in Matthew 5 when Jesus sat down on a mountainside to teach His disciples while the crowds listened. In chapter 5 He described the character of kingdom people and showed that God's commands reach the heart. In chapter 6 He warned against religious performance and against letting money and anxiety rule the heart. Now He addresses how disciples should treat others and how they should respond to His teaching as a whole.

The chapter moves from relationships (judging others, the Golden Rule) to prayer and then to a series of sharp contrasts. Jewish wisdom teaching often ended with a choice between two ways, as in Psalm 1 and Deuteronomy 30:15–20, where Moses set before Israel life and death, blessing and curse. Jesus ends His sermon the same way, presenting His hearers with a decision.

### Judging Others (7:1–6)

Jesus begins, [[v1|Don’t judge, so that you won’t be judged]]. This famous saying is often misunderstood. Jesus is not forbidding all moral evaluation; in the same chapter He tells His disciples to recognize false prophets by their fruit (vv. 15–20), which requires discernment. What He forbids is the harsh, condemning, self-righteous judgment that looks down on others while ignoring one's own faults. He warns that the standard we use for others will be used for us.

He illustrates with a humorous and exaggerated picture: someone with a beam (a large wooden plank used in building) sticking out of his own eye trying to remove a speck of sawdust from his brother's eye. As a carpenter's son, Jesus may have known this image well. The point is not that we should never help others with their faults. Jesus says, [[v5|First remove the beam out of your own eye, and then you can see clearly to remove the speck out of your brother’s eye]]. Honest self-examination and repentance come first, and they make us both humble and clear-sighted enough to help others gently.

Verse 6 adds a balancing warning: [[v6|Don’t give that which is holy to the dogs, neither throw your pearls before the pigs]]. In Jewish culture, dogs (usually wild scavengers, not pets) and pigs were unclean animals. Holy things, such as food offered to God, were not to be thrown to dogs. Pearls were extremely valuable, but pigs would see them only as inedible and trample them. Jesus seems to be saying that while disciples should not be judgmental, they also need discernment. Some people persistently and violently reject what is holy, and disciples are not required to keep pressing sacred truth on those who will only scorn and attack it. Jesus later tells His disciples to shake the dust off their feet when a town refuses their message (Matthew 10:14).

### Asking, Seeking, Knocking (7:7–11)

Jesus then encourages His disciples to pray: [[v7|Ask, and it will be given you. Seek, and you will find. Knock, and it will be opened for you]]. The verbs in Greek suggest continuing action: keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking. He supports this with an argument from everyday life. No father would give his hungry son a stone that looks like a loaf of bread or a snake instead of a fish. If sinful human parents know how to give good gifts to their children, how much more will the heavenly Father give good things to those who ask Him? (Luke's parallel says the Father will give [[Luke 11:13|the Holy Spirit]], the greatest good gift.)

The placement of this teaching is significant. Jesus has just set out the demanding standards of the kingdom. The disciples might wonder how they could ever live this way. Jesus' answer is prayer: ask the Father for what you need, including the grace to obey.

### The Golden Rule (7:12)

Jesus then gives what has become known as the Golden Rule: [[v12|whatever you desire for men to do to you, you shall also do to them]]. He adds, [[v12|for this is the law and the prophets]], meaning that this principle summarizes the moral teaching of the whole Old Testament. Versions of this principle in negative form ("do not do to others what you would not want done to you") existed in Judaism and other cultures, including a saying attributed to the rabbi Hillel. Jesus' positive form is more demanding: it requires actively doing good, not just avoiding harm.

### The Narrow Gate (7:13–14)

Jesus now begins His concluding warnings. He describes two gates and two roads. The wide gate and broad road lead to destruction, and many travel on it. The narrow gate and restricted road lead to life, and few find it. The image is of a choice between an easy, popular path and a demanding one. Following Jesus is not the default direction of life; it requires a deliberate decision and continuing commitment. The word translated "restricted" can carry the sense of pressure or hardship, hinting that the way of discipleship includes difficulty.

### False Prophets and Their Fruit (7:15–20)

Jesus warns against false prophets who come [[v15|in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly are ravening wolves]]. They appear to be part of God's flock, but their real aim is to harm and exploit it. The Old Testament warned frequently about prophets who spoke falsely in God's name (Jeremiah 23:16; Ezekiel 22:27). Jesus says they can be recognized by their fruit. Just as a thornbush cannot produce grapes, a corrupt person's life and teaching will eventually reveal what is inside. A good tree produces good fruit; a bad tree produces bad fruit. Fruit includes both conduct and the effects of a teacher's message on others.

### Saying "Lord, Lord" (7:21–23)

Jesus then gives one of the most sobering warnings in the Gospels. Not everyone who calls Him "Lord, Lord" will enter the kingdom, but only those who do the will of His Father. On "that day," the day of judgment, many will point to impressive religious activity: prophesying, casting out demons, and doing mighty works in His name. Jesus will say to them, [[v23|I never knew you. Depart from me, you who work iniquity]].

Notice that Jesus presents Himself as the one who will judge on the last day and whose personal relationship with a person determines their destiny. The problem with these people is not that they did no religious works but that they never truly knew Christ, and their lives were marked by lawlessness. Religious activity, even spectacular activity, cannot replace genuine relationship with Jesus and obedience to the Father.

### The Wise and Foolish Builders (7:24–27)

Jesus ends the sermon with a parable. Everyone who hears His words and does them is like a wise man who built his house on rock. When the rain fell, the rivers flooded, and the winds beat against the house, it stood firm. Everyone who hears His words and does not do them is like a foolish man who built on sand. When the storm came, the house collapsed.

In Palestine, dry riverbeds (wadis) could fill suddenly with rushing water during winter rains. A house built on the sandy floor of such a valley might look fine in the dry season, but it would be swept away when the floods came. The two houses might look the same from outside; the difference was only revealed by the storm.

### The Crowd's Reaction (7:28–29)

When Jesus finished, the crowds were astonished, because He taught [[v29|with authority, and not like the scribes]]. The scribes, the experts in the law, usually taught by citing earlier authorities and traditions. Jesus spoke on His own authority: "I tell you." He claimed that obeying His words was the foundation of life and that He would judge the world. The crowds sensed that someone extraordinary was speaking.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 7:1 — Do Not Judge

verse: Matthew 7:1

#### Simple Explanation

This verse begins the final chapter of the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus has just finished teaching about anxiety and trusting God, and now He turns to how His disciples treat others. The command is short and direct, and the next verse explains it: [[v2|For with whatever judgment you judge, you will be judged; and with whatever measure you measure, it will be measured to you]].

The word "judge" in Greek can mean to evaluate, to discern, or to condemn. The context shows that Jesus is warning against a particular kind of judging: a critical, condemning attitude that focuses on other people's faults while ignoring one's own. The picture of the person with a beam in his eye trying to remove a speck from his brother's eye (vv. 3–5) shows that the problem is hypocrisy and self-righteousness.

Jesus is not forbidding all moral discernment. In this same chapter He tells His disciples to beware of false prophets and to recognize them by their fruit (vv. 15–20), and in Matthew 18:15–17 He gives instructions for confronting a brother who sins. The issue is not whether we notice wrong but how we respond to it: with humility, honesty about ourselves, and a desire to help, or with pride, harshness, and condemnation.

His listeners lived in a culture where religious groups often judged one another, and where some looked down on "sinners," tax collectors, and Gentiles. Jesus warns that those who use harsh standards for others will face the same standards themselves before God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse connects with Jesus' teaching on mercy throughout the Sermon. Earlier He said, [[Matthew 5:7|Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy]], and in the Lord's Prayer He taught His disciples to ask forgiveness as they forgive others (Matthew 6:12). The principle is consistent: the way we treat others reveals whether we have truly understood God's mercy toward us.

Ultimately, only God is the righteous judge. Paul writes, [[Romans 14:10|why do you judge your brother?]] and reminds believers that all will stand before God's judgment seat. James adds that there is only one lawgiver and judge (James 4:12). When we condemn others, we take a role that belongs to God alone.

At the same time, Scripture calls believers to discernment, to distinguish truth from error and right from wrong, and to lovingly correct one another (Galatians 6:1). The key is that correction must begin with self-examination and be carried out in humility and gentleness, with the goal of restoration rather than condemnation.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to notice how quickly they criticize others. Before pointing out someone else's faults, they can ask God to reveal their own. Often the faults that irritate us most in others are ones we struggle with ourselves.

It also calls for a different tone when correction is necessary. Confronting wrongdoing is sometimes an act of love, especially when someone is harming themselves or others. But it should be done privately when possible, gently, and with a genuine desire to help, not to shame. Believers should be as eager to extend grace as they would want grace extended to them.

Finally, this verse warns against the habit of speaking negatively about others, whether in conversation, online, or in our thoughts. Assuming the worst about people, labeling them, or writing them off reflects the judgmental spirit Jesus forbids. Choosing to assume the best where possible, and leaving final judgment to God, frees believers from a burden they were never meant to carry.

### Matthew 7:7 — Ask, Seek, and Knock

verse: Matthew 7:7

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words after warning about judging others and about giving holy things to those who scorn them. He now turns to prayer, encouraging His disciples to come to God with confidence. The three verbs, ask, seek, and knock, form a progression of increasing intensity. Asking is making a request; seeking involves effort and searching; knocking is persistent pursuit, like someone standing at a door until it is opened.

In Greek, these commands are in the present tense, suggesting ongoing action: keep on asking, keep on seeking, keep on knocking. Jesus is encouraging persistence in prayer. Luke places a similar saying right after a parable about a man who keeps knocking on his friend's door at midnight until the friend gets up and gives him bread (Luke 11:5–10).

Jesus then explains why prayer is worthwhile. The Father is generous. Using a comparison His listeners would understand immediately, He asks which father would give his son a stone when he asks for bread, or a snake when he asks for fish. In Galilee, bread was a basic food and fish was common from the lake. No loving father would deceive his child with something useless or dangerous. If sinful human parents know how to give good gifts, [[v11|how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him!]]

This teaching comes near the end of a sermon full of demanding commands. It reassures the disciples that they do not have to live the kingdom life by their own strength. They can ask the Father for everything they need.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals God's character as a generous Father. Prayer is not an attempt to wear down a reluctant God; it is the natural expression of children who trust a Father who delights to give good gifts. James writes that [[James 1:17|Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights]].

It is important to read the promise in context. Jesus promises that the Father will give "good things" to those who ask. This does not mean that God will grant every specific request exactly as we ask it. A wise father does not give his children everything they want, especially if it would harm them. Sometimes God's answer is different from our request because He knows what is truly good. Jesus Himself prayed in Gethsemane for the cup to pass from Him, and the Father's answer was not removal but strength to obey (Matthew 26:39).

The greatest good gift the Father gives is Himself: His presence, His Spirit, and His kingdom. Prayer that seeks God Himself, and not only His gifts, will never be disappointed.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to pray with confidence and persistence. When a need remains or a prayer seems unanswered, the call is not to give up but to keep asking, seeking, and knocking, while trusting God's wisdom about the answer. Persistent prayer often deepens our understanding of what we truly need and shapes our hearts toward God's will.

It also invites believers to bring every kind of need to God, including the need for grace to live the life Jesus describes. Struggles with anger, lust, worry, or unforgiveness are exactly the kinds of things to bring to the Father, asking for help.

Finally, this verse invites trust in God's goodness when answers differ from our hopes. Remembering that the Father gives good gifts helps believers interpret disappointments without concluding that God does not care. He may be giving something better, or working in ways we cannot yet see.

### Matthew 7:12 — The Golden Rule

verse: Matthew 7:12

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is often called the Golden Rule, and it serves as a summary of much of the Sermon on the Mount. The word "therefore" links it to what came before. Jesus has just said that the Father gives good gifts to those who ask. Because God is so generous to us, we should treat others with the same generosity.

The principle is simple: think about how you would want others to treat you, and then treat them that way. If we want to be listened to, we should listen. If we want to be forgiven, we should forgive. If we want others to be honest and fair with us, we should be honest and fair with them. If we would want help in a time of need, we should help others in their need.

Jesus adds, [[v12|for this is the law and the prophets]]. This phrase echoes Matthew 5:17, where He said He came to fulfill the law and the prophets. Here He says that this rule sums up their moral teaching. Later, Jesus will say that the whole law and the prophets depend on the two great commandments: love God with all your heart, and love your neighbor as yourself (Matthew 22:37–40). The Golden Rule is a practical way of living out love for neighbor.

Similar sayings existed in Jewish and other ancient traditions, usually in negative form, such as "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor." Jesus' version is positive: it is not enough to avoid harming others; disciples must actively do good to them.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The Golden Rule reflects God's own character. God does not wait for people to earn His kindness; He takes the initiative to do good, sending sun and rain on the just and unjust (Matthew 5:45). When believers treat others as they would like to be treated, they reflect the generous heart of their Father.

The rule also provides a practical guide for countless situations that are not specifically addressed in Scripture. Rather than requiring a detailed rule for every circumstance, Jesus gives a principle that can be applied everywhere: at home, at work, in business, in conflict, and in caring for the needy.

At the same time, the Golden Rule must be understood within the whole of Jesus' teaching. It does not mean giving people whatever they want, or approving of everything they do. Love sometimes requires telling the truth or setting boundaries. The question is what we would truly want if we were in the other person's place and thinking clearly about what is good.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

The Golden Rule invites believers to practice empathy deliberately. Before responding to someone, especially in a difficult situation, they can pause and ask how they would want to be treated if their positions were reversed. This simple question can transform conversations, decisions, and relationships.

It applies in practical ways: speaking respectfully about people who are not present, treating employees and coworkers fairly, paying debts on time, being patient with those who are slow or struggling, and showing hospitality to strangers. It also applies to how we treat people who cannot benefit us, such as the poor, the elderly, children, and those on the margins.

Finally, the Golden Rule challenges passivity. It is not enough to avoid harming others. Jesus calls His disciples to take initiative in doing good, looking for ways to bless others as they would want to be blessed.

### Matthew 7:24 — Building on the Rock

verse: Matthew 7:24

#### Simple Explanation

This verse begins the final parable of the Sermon on the Mount. Jesus has just warned that not everyone who calls Him "Lord" will enter the kingdom, but only those who do the Father's will. Now He gives a picture of the difference between true and false discipleship.

He says that [[v24|Everyone therefore who hears these words of mine and does them]] is like a wise man who built his house on rock. "These words of mine" refers to the whole Sermon on the Mount, including the Beatitudes, the teaching on anger, lust, truthfulness, and love for enemies, the warnings about hypocrisy and money, and the call to trust the Father. Jesus places His own words at the foundation of life, something only God's Word could claim.

The key phrase is "and does them." Both builders in the parable hear Jesus' words. The difference is that one acts on them and the other does not. In Palestine, a sudden winter storm could turn a dry valley into a rushing river. A house built on the sandy ground in such a valley would be destroyed, while a house built on bedrock would stand. The storm reveals which foundation each builder chose.

Jesus' hearers would have understood the storm as a picture of testing, both the trials of life and, especially, the final judgment. The question is whether one's life will stand when it is tested.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This parable teaches that hearing God's Word is not enough; it must be obeyed. James makes the same point: [[James 1:22|But be doers of the word, and not only hearers, deluding your own selves]]. Knowledge of Scripture, attendance at teaching, and agreement with Jesus' words do not by themselves form a foundation. Obedience does.

This does not mean that salvation is earned by good works. The Sermon on the Mount begins with blessings for the poor in spirit, those who know they cannot save themselves. But genuine faith in Jesus always leads to obedience. Those who truly trust Him will seek to do what He says. A life that never changes despite hearing His words reveals that the person has not truly built on Him.

The parable also shows that Jesus Himself is the rock. Throughout Scripture, God is called the rock of His people (Psalm 18:2; Isaiah 26:4). By saying that a life built on His words will stand, Jesus is claiming to be the sure foundation God provides.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to move from hearing to doing. After reading Scripture or hearing it taught, a helpful response is to identify specific ways to obey: someone to forgive, a habit to change, a person to help, a truth to trust. Small acts of obedience, repeated over time, build a foundation that will stand.

It also prepares believers for storms. Hardships, temptations, losses, and crises come to everyone. Those who have built their lives on Jesus' words are not exempt from storms, but they can withstand them. The time to build a strong foundation is before the storm arrives, through consistent faithfulness in ordinary days.

Finally, this parable invites honest self-examination. Two houses may look alike from the outside. Similarly, two people may appear equally religious. The real question is whether Jesus' words are actually shaping how we live. If they are not, the remedy is to come to Him humbly, ask for His help, and begin to put His words into practice.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 7 brings the Sermon on the Mount to a practical and decisive conclusion. Jesus has described a way of life marked by humility, purity, mercy, truthfulness, generosity, and love. Now He asks His listeners to respond, not merely by admiring His teaching, but by living it.

The chapter's teaching on judging others is especially relevant in a culture quick to criticize. Jesus calls His followers to look first at their own faults, to correct others only with humility and gentleness, and to leave final judgment to God. This does not mean abandoning discernment; believers must still recognize false teaching and harmful behavior. But their posture toward others should be marked by mercy rather than condemnation.

The encouragement to ask, seek, and knock reminds believers that the kingdom life is not lived by willpower alone. The Father is generous and ready to give good things, including the strength to obey. Regular, persistent prayer is the way disciples draw on His help. The Golden Rule then gives a practical test for daily life: treat others as you would want to be treated, actively doing good rather than merely avoiding harm.

Finally, Jesus presents a series of choices that call for honest reflection. The narrow road and the wide road, good fruit and bad fruit, genuine obedience and empty words, rock and sand: each contrast asks whether His teaching is truly shaping a person's life. Religious activity, impressive gifts, and correct words are not enough. What matters is knowing Jesus and doing the Father's will. Believers can respond by examining their lives, confessing where they have heard without doing, and taking concrete steps to build on the rock while there is still time.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for teaching with authority and for giving me words on which I can build my whole life. Forgive me for the times I have judged others harshly while ignoring my own faults. Show me the beam in my own eye, and give me humility and gentleness when I need to help someone else.

Thank You that my Father in heaven gives good gifts to those who ask. Teach me to keep asking, seeking, and knocking, and to trust His goodness when His answers are different from what I expected. Help me to treat others as I would want to be treated, and to take the initiative in doing good. Keep me on the narrow road that leads to life, guard me from false teachers, and let my life bear good fruit. Do not let me be someone who only hears Your words; make me someone who does them, so that my house stands firm when the storms come. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
