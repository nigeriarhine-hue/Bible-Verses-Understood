# Acts 8
theme: Scattered Believers Carry the Gospel to Samaria and Beyond
main: After Stephen's death, a fierce persecution led by Saul scatters the believers from Jerusalem, and wherever they go they preach the word. Philip proclaims Christ in Samaria with signs and great joy, and many believe, including a famous sorcerer named Simon. Peter and John come from Jerusalem, pray for the Samaritans to receive the Holy Spirit, and sternly rebuke Simon for trying to buy God's gift. Then the Spirit leads Philip to a desert road where he explains Isaiah's prophecy of the suffering servant to an Ethiopian official, who believes in Jesus, is baptized, and goes on his way rejoicing.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 8 marks a major turning point. Until now, the gospel had been preached mainly in Jerusalem. Jesus had told His disciples that they would be His witnesses "in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the uttermost parts of the earth" (1:8). In this chapter, the gospel moves into Judea and Samaria, and through the Ethiopian official, it begins its journey to the ends of the earth. Strikingly, the means God uses to spread the gospel is persecution.

### Persecution and Scattering (8:1–4)

[[v1|Saul was consenting to his death]]. Saul approved of Stephen's execution. That same day, [[v1|A great persecution arose against the assembly which was in Jerusalem]]. The believers were [[v1|all scattered abroad throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria, except for the apostles]]. The apostles stayed in Jerusalem, perhaps because the persecution focused especially on the Greek-speaking believers associated with Stephen, or because the apostles felt called to remain at the center.

[[v2|Devout men buried Stephen and lamented greatly over him]]. Even in a time of danger, believers honored Stephen and grieved openly. Faith does not erase grief.

Meanwhile, [[v3|Saul ravaged the assembly, entering into every house and dragged both men and women off to prison]]. The word "ravaged" describes a wild animal tearing its prey. Saul later admitted, [[Galatians 1:13|beyond measure I persecuted the assembly of God and ravaged it]]. The scattering of the believers is studied below.

### Philip in Samaria (8:5–13)

Philip, one of the seven chosen to serve the widows (6:5), went to [[v5|the city of Samaria]] and [[v5|proclaimed to them the Christ]]. This was significant. Jews and Samaritans had been divided for centuries (John 4:9). Jesus had shown the way by speaking with a Samaritan woman and telling a parable about a good Samaritan. Now a Greek-speaking Jewish believer brought the gospel to Samaria. The Samaritans, who also expected a coming deliverer, listened eagerly.

The people paid close attention [[v6|when they heard and saw the signs which he did]]. Unclean spirits came out of many people, and many who were paralyzed or lame were healed. [[v8|There was great joy in that city]].

Among them was a man named Simon who had practiced sorcery and amazed the people, [[v9|making himself out to be some great one]]. The people said of him, [[v10|This man is that great power of God]]. Later Christian writers called him "Simon Magus" ("Simon the magician") and described him as a founder of false teachings, though Luke tells us only what happened in Samaria.

When the Samaritans believed Philip's message about God's kingdom and the name of Jesus, [[v12|they were baptized, both men and women]]. Simon himself believed and was baptized, and he followed Philip, amazed at the miracles. Whether Simon's faith was genuine is uncertain; the following verses suggest his heart had not truly changed.

### Peter and John in Samaria (8:14–25)

When the apostles in Jerusalem heard that Samaria had received the word of God, they sent Peter and John. They prayed for the new believers to receive the Holy Spirit, laid hands on them, and the Spirit came. This unusual event is studied below.

Simon saw that the Spirit was given through the laying on of the apostles' hands, and he offered them money: [[v19|Give me also this power, that whomever I lay my hands on may receive the Holy Spirit]]. Peter's stern response is studied below. Simon asked Peter to pray for him. Luke does not tell us what happened to him afterward. His name gave rise to the word "simony," which refers to buying or selling church offices or spiritual privileges.

Peter and John then returned to Jerusalem, preaching in many Samaritan villages along the way. John, who once wanted to call down fire on a Samaritan village (Luke 9:54), now brought them the good news.

### Philip and the Ethiopian Official (8:26–40)

An angel told Philip to go south to the road from Jerusalem to Gaza, [[v26|This is a desert]]. Philip left a successful ministry among crowds to go to a lonely road, and there he met one man.

[[v27|there was a man of Ethiopia, a eunuch of great authority under Candace, queen of the Ethiopians, who was over all her treasure]]. "Ethiopia" in the ancient world referred to the kingdom of Kush, south of Egypt, in what is now Sudan. "Candace" was a title for the queen mother who ruled there, not a personal name. This official was the royal treasurer, a man of high rank. He had traveled about a thousand miles to worship in Jerusalem.

As a eunuch, he would have been excluded from full participation in the temple assembly (Deuteronomy 23:1), and as a foreigner, he could not enter the inner courts. Yet he had made the long journey, and he was reading the scroll of Isaiah on his way home. Isaiah 56:3–5 contains a beautiful promise that God would give eunuchs who hold fast to His covenant "a name better than of sons and of daughters." This man was about to experience its fulfillment.

The Spirit told Philip to go near the chariot. Philip ran and heard the man reading Isaiah aloud, as was customary in the ancient world. The conversation that followed is studied below.

After the official was baptized, [[v39|the Spirit of the Lord caught Philip away, and the eunuch didn’t see him any more, for he went on his way rejoicing]]. Philip appeared at Azotus (ancient Ashdod) and preached in all the towns along the coast until he reached Caesarea, where he apparently settled. Years later, Paul and Luke would stay at his house there (Acts 21:8–9).

Ancient Christian tradition says that the Ethiopian official brought the gospel back to his homeland. Whether or not that is so, his conversion shows the gospel beginning to reach "the uttermost parts of the earth."

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 8:4 — Scattered Abroad, Preaching the Word

verse: Acts 8:4

#### Simple Explanation

After Stephen was killed, a great persecution broke out against the church in Jerusalem. Saul went from house to house, dragging believers off to prison. Many believers fled from Jerusalem to escape the danger, leaving their homes, jobs, and communities behind. They were scattered throughout Judea and Samaria.

Then Luke writes, [[v4|Therefore those who were scattered abroad went around preaching the word]]. The believers did not hide their faith or fall silent. Wherever they went, they shared the message about Jesus.

Notice who was doing the preaching. The apostles had stayed in Jerusalem (8:1). The ones scattered were ordinary believers, men and women who were not apostles or official leaders. Yet they spread the gospel everywhere they went.

The word "scattered" is related to the word for sowing seed. Like a farmer scattering seed across a field, God scattered His people, and the seed of the gospel took root in new places.

Persecution was meant to destroy the church. Instead, it spread the church. What the enemies of the gospel intended for harm, God used for the spread of His word.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows God's sovereign power to bring good out of evil. The persecution was real and painful. Believers lost homes, freedom, and in some cases their lives. Yet God used it to fulfill Jesus' command to be His witnesses in Judea and Samaria. Paul later wrote from prison that his imprisonment had [[Philippians 1:12|turned out rather to the progress of the Good News]].

It also shows that the spread of the gospel is the work of all believers, not only leaders. Ordinary Christians who simply shared what they knew about Jesus carried the gospel far beyond Jerusalem. Throughout history, the church has often grown most through the everyday witness of ordinary believers.

Finally, the verse reveals the resilience of genuine faith. These believers had lost much, yet they did not lose their message. Their faith was not dependent on comfortable circumstances.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers who have been displaced, whether by persecution, war, economic hardship, or other circumstances. Many Christians today are refugees or migrants. Wherever they go, they can carry the gospel with them. God can use their scattering to spread His word.

It also reminds every believer that they are called to share their faith. Sharing Christ is not only the job of pastors or missionaries. Believers can speak about Jesus in their workplaces, neighborhoods, schools, and families.

Finally, the verse invites believers to see difficult circumstances differently. Changes that seem like losses, such as moving to a new place, changing jobs, or being separated from familiar communities, can become opportunities for new witness. God can use unexpected situations to bring the gospel to people who need it.

### Acts 8:14–17 — The Samaritans Receive the Holy Spirit

verse: Acts 8:14-17

#### Simple Explanation

Philip had preached the gospel in Samaria, and many people had believed and been baptized. When the apostles in Jerusalem heard about it, [[v14|they sent Peter and John to them]]. This was an important moment. For centuries, Jews and Samaritans had been divided by hostility. Now Samaritans were believing in the Jewish Messiah.

When Peter and John arrived, they [[v15|prayed for them, that they might receive the Holy Spirit]]. Luke explains why: [[v16|for as yet he had fallen on none of them. They had only been baptized in the name of Christ Jesus]]. This was unusual. Normally in Acts, believers receive the Spirit when they believe (2:38; 10:44).

[[v17|Then they laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit]]. The Spirit came on the Samaritan believers through the prayer and laying on of hands of the apostles. Something visible seems to have happened, since Simon "saw" that the Spirit was given (8:18).

Why the delay? Many interpreters believe God withheld the Spirit until the apostles came so that the Samaritan believers would be clearly united with the Jerusalem church. If the Samaritans had received the Spirit independently, the old division might have continued, with two separate churches. Instead, the apostles from Jerusalem welcomed the Samaritans into the one body of Christ, and the Samaritans received the Spirit through the leaders of the Jewish church.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage shows that the gospel breaks down barriers. Jews and Samaritans had been enemies for centuries. Through Christ, they became one family, sharing the same Spirit. Paul writes that Christ [[Ephesians 2:14|broke down the middle wall of separation]].

Christians have understood this passage in different ways. Some traditions, particularly Pentecostal ones, see it as evidence that receiving the Holy Spirit in fullness can be a distinct experience after conversion. Some sacramental traditions see a connection to confirmation, in which a bishop lays on hands and prays for the Spirit. Many others see the delay as a unique event in the history of the early church, related to the Samaritans' inclusion, rather than a pattern for every believer. All agree that the Holy Spirit is given to all who belong to Christ (Romans 8:9) and that the Spirit unites believers in one body.

Finally, the passage shows the importance of unity and connection in the church. The apostles did not view the Samaritan believers as a separate movement. They came, prayed, and welcomed them.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage challenges believers to reach across divisions. Many communities today are divided by ethnicity, culture, politics, or history. The gospel calls believers to cross these lines with love, as Philip, Peter, and John did. Churches can seek to welcome people from groups that have been divided from them.

It also encourages believers to value connection with the wider church. Local churches are part of the one body of Christ. Partnerships, fellowship, and mutual support across churches reflect the unity the Spirit creates.

Finally, the passage invites believers to pray for the Spirit's work. Peter and John prayed for the Samaritans to receive the Spirit. Believers can pray for one another to be filled with the Spirit and to experience His presence and power.

### Acts 8:20–22 — The Gift of God Cannot Be Bought

verse: Acts 8:20-22

#### Simple Explanation

Simon had been a famous sorcerer in Samaria before he believed and was baptized. When he saw that the Holy Spirit was given through the laying on of the apostles' hands, he offered them money and asked for the same power. As a magician, he may have been used to buying secret techniques from other practitioners. He seemed to view the Holy Spirit as a power he could purchase and use.

Peter's response was severe: [[v20|May your silver perish with you, because you thought you could obtain the gift of God with money!]] The Holy Spirit is a gift from God. Gifts cannot be bought. Simon's offer showed that he completely misunderstood the nature of God's grace.

[[v21|You have neither part nor lot in this matter, for your heart isn’t right before God]]. Peter saw that Simon's heart was not right. He wanted power and prestige, not God Himself.

Yet Peter did not leave Simon without hope: [[v22|Repent therefore of this, your wickedness, and ask God if perhaps the thought of your heart may be forgiven you]]. Peter called Simon to repent and pray for forgiveness. The word "perhaps" does not mean God might refuse a truly repentant heart; it emphasizes the seriousness of Simon's sin and the need for genuine repentance.

Peter added, [[v23|For I see that you are in the poison of bitterness and in the bondage of iniquity]]. Simon was still enslaved by sin.

Simon replied, [[v24|Pray for me to the Lord, that none of the things which you have spoken happen to me]]. His response seems more concerned with avoiding punishment than with a change of heart, but Luke does not tell us the outcome.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage reveals that God's gifts cannot be purchased. Salvation, the Holy Spirit, and spiritual gifts are given freely by God's grace. Isaiah invited people to come and buy [[Isaiah 55:1|without money and without price]]. Any attempt to buy God's favor misunderstands grace.

It also shows the danger of seeking spiritual power for selfish purposes. Simon wanted to use God's power to enhance his own reputation and influence. Throughout history, some people have tried to use religion for profit, fame, or control. God's power is not for sale and cannot be used for personal gain.

Finally, the passage shows that outward participation does not guarantee a changed heart. Simon believed, was baptized, and followed Philip, yet his heart was not right before God. Jesus warned that not everyone who calls Him "Lord" truly knows Him (Matthew 7:21–23). True faith involves a transformed heart.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage warns against treating faith as a means to personal gain. Some people approach God mainly for what they can get: wealth, health, success, or influence. While God cares about people's needs, He is not a tool to be used. Believers are called to seek God for who He is.

It also warns against the commercialization of spiritual things. Churches and ministries must be careful never to suggest that God's blessings can be bought, or that giving money guarantees spiritual power or favor. Generosity is good, but God's grace is free.

Finally, the passage encourages believers to examine their hearts. Simon's outward actions seemed right, but his heart was not. Believers can ask God to search their hearts and to purify their motives, and they can trust that God forgives those who genuinely repent.

### Acts 8:35–36 — Beginning from This Scripture, He Preached Jesus

verse: Acts 8:35-36

#### Simple Explanation

The Ethiopian official was sitting in his chariot reading from the scroll of Isaiah. He was reading Isaiah 53:7–8, which describes someone who was led like a sheep to the slaughter, silent like a lamb before its shearer, humiliated and denied justice, whose life was taken from the earth.

Philip, led by the Spirit, ran up to the chariot and asked, [[v30|Do you understand what you are reading?]] The official answered honestly, [[v31|How can I, unless someone explains it to me?]] He invited Philip to sit with him.

The official asked, [[v34|Who is the prophet talking about? About himself, or about someone else?]] This was a natural question. Jewish interpreters had different views. Some thought Isaiah was speaking about himself, others about the nation of Israel, others about a future figure.

[[v35|Philip opened his mouth, and beginning from this Scripture, preached to him about Jesus]]. Philip explained that the passage described Jesus, who was led to the slaughter, suffered in silence, was unjustly condemned, and died for the sins of others. Isaiah 53 says that the servant was "pierced for our transgressions" and that "the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all."

As they traveled, they came to some water, and the official said, [[v36|Behold, here is water. What is keeping me from being baptized?]] He wanted to respond immediately to the good news. (Verse 37, which contains a confession of faith, appears in some later manuscripts but not in the earliest ones, which is why the World English Bible leaves it blank.) The chariot stopped, and Philip baptized him. The official went on his way rejoicing.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage shows that the Old Testament points to Jesus. Isaiah 53, written centuries before Jesus' birth, describes His suffering and death with remarkable clarity. Jesus Himself taught that all the Scriptures testify about Him (Luke 24:27, 44). Philip's method, beginning with a Scripture passage and showing how it is fulfilled in Jesus, is a model for understanding the Bible.

It also shows God's care for individuals. Philip left a city-wide revival to speak with one man on a desert road. God cared about this one seeker, a foreigner and a eunuch who had been on the edges of Israel's worship. Through Christ, he was fully welcomed into God's people.

Finally, the official's question, "What is keeping me from being baptized?", has a powerful answer: nothing. His nationality, his status as a eunuch, and his distance from Jerusalem could not keep him out. In Christ, the barriers are removed.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage encourages believers to help others understand Scripture. Many people read the Bible but struggle to understand it. Believers can come alongside them, as Philip did, with patience and humility, explaining how the Bible points to Jesus.

It also encourages believers to follow the Spirit's leading, even when it seems unusual. Philip went to a desert road because God told him to. Believers can be open to God's prompting to speak with someone, visit someone, or help someone, trusting that God has prepared the way.

Finally, the passage invites anyone who feels excluded to come to Christ. The Ethiopian official was a foreigner and a eunuch, someone who had been kept at a distance from full participation in worship. In Jesus, he was welcomed completely. No one is too far away, too different, or too marginalized to be included in God's family.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 8 shows that God can use even persecution to spread the gospel. The believers who were scattered from Jerusalem preached the word wherever they went. Believers today who face hardship, displacement, or loss can trust that God is able to use their circumstances for His purposes, and they can carry the message of Jesus wherever they go.

The chapter also shows the gospel crossing barriers. Philip preached to the Samaritans, long despised by Jews, and Peter and John came to welcome them into the one church. Philip then explained the Scriptures to an Ethiopian official who had been on the margins of Israel's worship. Believers today are called to reach across lines of ethnicity, culture, and social status, welcoming all into the family of God.

Acts 8 also warns against trying to use God for selfish purposes. Simon wanted to buy spiritual power, but God's gifts cannot be purchased. Believers can examine their motives, seeking God for who He is rather than for what they can gain, and they can trust that God forgives those who truly repent.

Finally, the chapter shows the joy of the gospel. There was great joy in Samaria, and the Ethiopian official went on his way rejoicing. When people encounter Jesus, their lives are filled with joy. Believers can share this joy by helping others understand the Scriptures and meet the Savior they reveal.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord God, thank You that no persecution or hardship can stop Your word from spreading. When my life is disrupted or I find myself in new places, help me to carry the message of Jesus with me. Thank You for breaking down the walls between Jews and Samaritans, and for welcoming the Ethiopian official into Your family. Show me the barriers I need to cross with Your love. Guard my heart from seeking You only for what I can gain, and purify my motives. Thank You that Your gifts are free and cannot be bought. Lead me by Your Spirit, as You led Philip, to people who are seeking You. Give me wisdom to explain Your word and to point others to Jesus. Fill me with the joy of Your salvation. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
