# Romans 11
theme: God Has Not Rejected His People: The Olive Tree and the Depth of God's Wisdom
main: Paul insists that God has not rejected Israel; he himself is an Israelite, and just as in Elijah's day, there is a faithful remnant chosen by grace. Israel's stumbling has opened the way of salvation to the Gentiles, and Paul hopes this will move his own people to jealousy and faith. Using the image of an olive tree, he warns Gentile believers, grafted in like wild branches, not to boast over the natural branches, because they stand only by faith, and God is able to graft the natural branches back in. Paul reveals a mystery: a partial hardening has come upon Israel until the full number of Gentiles comes in, and so all Israel will be saved, for God's gifts and calling are irrevocable. The chapter ends in a hymn of praise to the unsearchable wisdom of God.

## Chapter Background and Story

Romans 11 completes Paul's discussion of Israel in chapters 9–11. In chapter 9, Paul showed that God's word has not failed, because God's promises work through His sovereign choice. In chapter 10, he showed that Israel is responsible for not believing the gospel. Now he answers the crucial question: Has God rejected His people? His answer is a resounding no.

This chapter is especially important for understanding the relationship between the church and the Jewish people. Paul warns Gentile Christians against arrogance toward Israel, affirms God's ongoing love for the Jewish people, and expresses hope for their future. Sadly, throughout history, many Christians have ignored this teaching and treated Jewish people with contempt and cruelty. Romans 11 stands as a rebuke to all such attitudes.

### A Remnant Chosen by Grace (11:1–10)

[[v1|I ask then, did God reject his people? May it never be!]] Paul offers himself as proof: [[v1|For I also am an Israelite, a descendant of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin]]. If God had rejected Israel, Paul himself would not have been saved. [[v2|God didn’t reject his people, whom he foreknew]].

Paul then points to Elijah. In a time of great unfaithfulness, Elijah complained that he alone was left faithful (1 Kings 19:10). But God answered, [[v4|I have reserved for myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal]]. Elijah was not alone. God had preserved a faithful remnant. Paul's application is studied below.

[[v7|What then? That which Israel seeks for, that he didn’t obtain, but the chosen ones obtained it, and the rest were hardened]]. Paul quotes Deuteronomy 29:4 and Isaiah 29:10 about a "spirit of stupor," and Psalm 69:22–23. These passages describe God's judgment on those who persistently reject Him. Yet Paul will show that this hardening is neither total nor final.

### Israel's Stumbling and the Gentiles' Salvation (11:11–16)

[[v11|I ask then, did they stumble that they might fall? May it never be!]] Israel's stumbling is not permanent. [[v11|But by their fall salvation has come to the Gentiles, to provoke them to jealousy]]. When many Jews rejected the gospel, the apostles turned to the Gentiles (Acts 13:46). God used Israel's unbelief to bring salvation to the nations. And He intends the Gentiles' salvation to make Israel jealous, drawing them back to Him.

[[v12|Now if their fall is the riches of the world, and their loss the riches of the Gentiles, how much more their fullness!]] If Israel's stumbling has brought such blessing, how much greater blessing will come when Israel returns. Paul magnified his ministry to the Gentiles, hoping it would provoke his own people to faith. [[v15|For if the rejection of them is the reconciling of the world, what would their acceptance be, but life from the dead?]] Israel's future acceptance will be like resurrection.

[[v16|If the first fruit is holy, so is the lump. If the root is holy, so are the branches]]. The "first fruit" and "root" probably refer to the patriarchs, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Because the root is holy, the whole tree belongs to God.

### The Olive Tree (11:17–24)

Paul then uses the image of an olive tree, studied below. He warns, [[v20|True; by their unbelief they were broken off, and you stand by your faith. Don’t be conceited, but fear]]. [[v21|for if God didn’t spare the natural branches, neither will he spare you]]. [[v22|See then the goodness and severity of God. Toward those who fell, severity; but toward you, goodness, if you continue in his goodness; otherwise you also will be cut off]]. God's kindness should never be taken for granted. Those who stand by faith must continue in faith.

[[v23|They also, if they don’t continue in their unbelief, will be grafted in, for God is able to graft them in again]]. The door remains open for Israel. [[v24|how much more will these, which are the natural branches, be grafted into their own olive tree?]] It is even more natural for the original branches to be restored.

### The Mystery: All Israel Will Be Saved (11:25–32)

Paul reveals a "mystery," studied below. He then explains God's attitude toward Israel: [[v28|Concerning the Good News, they are enemies for your sake. But concerning the election, they are beloved for the fathers’ sake]]. In relation to the gospel, many Jews were opposing it, which opened the door for the Gentiles. But in relation to God's choice, they remain loved because of His promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. [[v29|For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable]]. God does not take back His promises. This is good news for Israel, and also for all believers: God's faithfulness to His promises is the foundation of their own security.

[[v30|For as you in time past were disobedient to God, but now have obtained mercy by their disobedience]], [[v31|even so these also have now been disobedient, that by the mercy shown to you they may also obtain mercy]]. Gentiles and Jews alike receive mercy. [[v32|For God has bound all to disobedience, that he might have mercy on all]]. God has shown that all people, Jews and Gentiles, are disobedient, so that all might receive mercy rather than earning salvation. This verse does not teach that every individual will be saved regardless of faith, but that God's mercy is offered to all without distinction.

### The Hymn of Praise (11:33–36)

Paul ends this section with a burst of worship, studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Romans 11:5–6 — A Remnant by Grace

verse: Romans 11:5-6

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had asked whether God had rejected His people, Israel. He answered, "May it never be!" He pointed to himself, an Israelite who believed in Jesus. Then he pointed to Elijah's time, when Elijah thought he was the only faithful person left, but God said He had kept seven thousand who had not worshiped Baal.

Now Paul applied this to his own time: [[v5|Even so too at this present time also there is a remnant according to the election of grace]]. A "remnant" is a small part that remains from a larger whole. In the Old Testament, God often preserved a faithful remnant even when most of the nation turned away (Isaiah 10:20–22). In Paul's day, many Jews had believed in Jesus, including the apostles, the thousands at Pentecost, and many others. They were the remnant.

This remnant exists "according to the election of grace." God chose them, and His choice was based on grace, not on their merit.

[[v6|And if by grace, then it is no longer of works; otherwise grace is no longer grace]]. Paul explains what grace means. If salvation were based on works, it would no longer be grace. Grace and works are opposites when it comes to earning salvation. Grace is God's free gift; works are what people earn. If people could earn salvation, grace would not really be grace.

These verses show that God has always been faithful to His people, preserving a remnant by His grace. Even in times of widespread unbelief, God is at work.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show God's faithfulness to His promises. Even when Israel as a whole turned away, God never abandoned His people completely. He always preserved a faithful remnant. This shows that God's purposes cannot be defeated by human unfaithfulness.

They also show that salvation is entirely by grace. Paul writes elsewhere, [[Ephesians 2:9|not of works, that no one would boast]]. Grace cannot be mixed with merit. Either salvation is a free gift, or it is earned. It cannot be both.

Finally, these verses offer encouragement in times when faithfulness seems rare. Elijah felt alone, but he was not. God always has people who belong to Him, even when they are hidden.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers who feel isolated in their faith. Like Elijah, believers may sometimes feel that they are the only ones who remain faithful. God's word to Elijah applies: He has others who belong to Him. Believers are not alone.

They also remind believers that their salvation is a gift of grace. No one can earn it. This truth humbles believers and fills them with gratitude.

Finally, these verses give hope for the future. God is always at work, preserving His people and drawing others to Himself. Believers can trust that God's purposes will prevail, even in times of widespread unbelief.

### Romans 11:17–18 — Don't Boast over the Branches

verse: Romans 11:17-18

#### Simple Explanation

Paul now uses a vivid image from agriculture to describe the relationship between Israel and Gentile believers: an olive tree. Olive trees were common in Israel and were an important source of food, oil, and income. The olive tree was also a symbol of Israel in the Old Testament (Jeremiah 11:16; Hosea 14:6).

[[v17|But if some of the branches were broken off]]. The "branches" are the people of Israel. Some of them were broken off because of unbelief. Notice that Paul says "some," not all.

[[v17|and you, being a wild olive, were grafted in among them]]. Gentile believers are like branches from a wild olive tree, grafted into the cultivated tree. Grafting involves cutting a branch from one tree and inserting it into another so that it grows as part of it. Normally, farmers grafted cultivated branches onto wild trees, not the other way around. Paul acknowledges that what God has done is unusual, "contrary to nature" (11:24).

[[v17|and became partaker with them of the root and of the richness of the olive tree]]. Gentile believers now share in the root and the rich sap of the tree, the blessings God promised to Abraham and his descendants. They have been brought into the people of God.

[[v18|don’t boast over the branches]]. Paul warns Gentile believers not to feel superior to the Jews. They should not think that because some branches were broken off, they are better.

[[v18|But if you boast, remember that it is not you who support the root, but the root supports you]]. The Gentiles depend on Israel, not the other way around. The root, God's covenant with Abraham and the patriarchs, supports the whole tree. Gentile believers owe their faith to God's work through Israel.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the unity of God's people across history. The church is not a separate tree from Israel. Gentile believers are grafted into the same tree, sharing in the same promises. Paul writes, [[Ephesians 2:19|So then you are no longer strangers and foreigners, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and of the household of God]].

They also show the danger of pride. Gentile believers were tempted to look down on Jews who had not believed. Paul warns that they stand only by faith and have no grounds for boasting. Pride is always a danger when people forget that everything they have is a gift.

Finally, these verses show that the Christian faith has Jewish roots. The Scriptures, the promises, the covenants, and the Messiah all came through Israel. Christians should honor this heritage, not despise it.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses call believers to humility. Believers are saved by grace, not by their own merit. They have no grounds to boast over anyone.

They also call believers to reject antisemitism. Throughout history, many Christians have treated Jewish people with contempt, persecution, and violence. Paul's words condemn such attitudes. Christians are called to honor the Jewish people, pray for them, and treat them with love.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to appreciate their spiritual heritage. The Old Testament is not a book for someone else; it is part of believers' own story. They can study it with gratitude, recognizing the root that supports them.

### Romans 11:25–26 — And So All Israel Will Be Saved

verse: Romans 11:25-26

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been explaining that some branches of the olive tree, the people of Israel, had been broken off because of unbelief, and that Gentiles had been grafted in. But he had said that God is able to graft the natural branches back in. Now he reveals something about the future.

[[v25|For I don’t desire you to be ignorant, brothers, of this mystery, so that you won’t be wise in your own conceits]]. A "mystery" in Paul's letters is not something mysterious in the modern sense, but a truth once hidden that God has now revealed. Paul shares this mystery to keep Gentile believers from becoming proud.

[[v25|that a partial hardening has happened to Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in]]. The hardening of Israel is "partial," not complete; many Jews have believed. It is also temporary, "until" a certain point. That point is when "the fullness of the Gentiles has come in," when the full number of Gentiles God intends to save have come to faith.

[[v26|and so all Israel will be saved]]. This is one of the most discussed phrases in Romans. Christians have understood it in several ways. Many believe it refers to a future turning of the Jewish people as a whole to faith in Jesus as the Messiah, perhaps near the time of Christ's return. Others believe "all Israel" refers to all the elect among the Jewish people throughout history, saved one by one as they come to faith. Still others believe it refers to the whole people of God, both Jews and Gentiles, the "Israel of God" (Galatians 6:16). Each view is held by faithful interpreters. Many find the first view most natural in the context, since Paul has been distinguishing Israel from the Gentiles throughout the chapter. All agree that salvation comes only through faith in Jesus, and that God remains faithful to His promises to Israel.

[[v26|Even as it is written, “There will come out of Zion the Deliverer, and he will turn away ungodliness from Jacob.]] Paul quotes Isaiah 59:20–21 and 27:9. The Deliverer, Christ, will remove sin from Jacob, that is, Israel. [[v27|This is my covenant with them, when I will take away their sins.]] God's covenant includes the forgiveness of Israel's sins.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that God has a continuing purpose for the Jewish people. God has not replaced Israel or forgotten His promises. Paul writes, [[Romans 11:29|For the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable]].

They also show that God's plan is bigger than any one moment in history. Israel's partial hardening, the inclusion of the Gentiles, and the future salvation of Israel are all part of God's unfolding purpose. God is working out His plan across centuries.

Finally, these verses show that salvation always comes through Christ. The Deliverer from Zion is the one who takes away sin. Whatever the exact meaning of "all Israel," it is through faith in the Messiah that Israel will be saved, just as the Gentiles are.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to pray for the Jewish people. Paul longed for their salvation, and these verses give hope that God has not forgotten them.

They also call believers to humility. Paul revealed this mystery so that Gentile believers would not be "wise in your own conceits." Believers should not think of themselves as superior to Israel or any other people.

Finally, these verses give believers confidence in God's faithfulness. If God remains faithful to His promises to Israel despite their unbelief, He will also be faithful to His promises to all who belong to Him.

### Romans 11:33–36 — The Depth of God's Wisdom

verse: Romans 11:33-36

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had spent three chapters wrestling with one of the hardest questions of his time: how God's promises to Israel fit with Israel's unbelief and the inclusion of the Gentiles. He had explained God's sovereign choice, Israel's responsibility, the remnant, the olive tree, and the mystery of Israel's future salvation. At the end of all this explanation, Paul does not simply conclude with a summary. He bursts into worship.

[[v33|Oh the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God!]] Paul is overwhelmed by God's greatness. God's riches, wisdom, and knowledge are like an ocean too deep to measure.

[[v33|How unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past tracing out!]] God's decisions and ways are beyond human understanding. People can understand some of what God reveals, but they can never fully grasp all of His ways. The phrase "past tracing out" pictures footprints that cannot be followed.

[[v34|“For who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor?”]] Paul quotes Isaiah 40:13. No one fully knows God's mind, and no one has ever given Him advice. God does not need human counsel.

[[v35|“Or who has first given to him, and it will be repaid to him again?”]] This echoes Job 41:11. No one has ever given God something that would put Him in their debt. Everything people have came from God first. Salvation is grace, not repayment.

[[v36|For of him and through him and to him are all things]]. Everything comes "from" God as its source, exists "through" God as its sustainer, and exists "for" God as its goal. God is the beginning, middle, and end of all things.

[[v36|To him be the glory for ever! Amen.]] The only fitting response is to give God glory forever.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that theology should lead to worship. Paul did not study God's ways merely to win arguments or satisfy curiosity. The more he understood, the more he was moved to praise. True knowledge of God always leads to adoration.

They also show the limits of human understanding. Paul had explained much, but he acknowledged that God's ways are ultimately beyond full comprehension. This is not a failure of faith but an expression of it. Isaiah writes, [[Isaiah 55:9|For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts]].

Finally, these verses show that everything exists for God's glory. This is the ultimate purpose of creation, history, and salvation. The whole plan of God, including His dealings with Israel and the Gentiles, leads to His glory.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to respond to God's truth with worship. When believers study Scripture and learn about God, they can pause to praise Him for His wisdom and greatness.

They also encourage believers to trust God when they do not understand. Many questions about God's ways remain unanswered. Believers can rest in the knowledge that His wisdom is perfect, even when His ways are past finding out.

Finally, these verses call believers to live for God's glory. Since everything is from Him, through Him, and to Him, believers can seek to honor Him in all they do, offering every part of their lives back to Him.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Romans 11 declares that God has not rejected His people. Even in times of widespread unbelief, God preserves a remnant chosen by grace. Believers who feel isolated in their faith can take heart, as Elijah did, that God always has people who belong to Him. And believers can rest in the truth that salvation is entirely by grace.

The chapter also warns Gentile believers against pride. They are like wild branches grafted into God's olive tree, sharing in the rich root of God's promises to Israel. They have no grounds to boast over the natural branches. Believers are called to humility, to gratitude for their spiritual heritage, and to the firm rejection of antisemitism in every form.

Romans 11 gives hope for Israel's future. A partial hardening has come upon Israel until the fullness of the Gentiles comes in, and so all Israel will be saved, because God's gifts and calling are irrevocable. Believers can pray for the Jewish people with hope, trusting God's faithfulness. And they can take comfort that the God who keeps His promises to Israel will keep His promises to them.

Finally, the chapter ends in worship. After wrestling with the deepest questions, Paul praises the depth of God's wisdom and knowledge, His unsearchable judgments, and His untraceable ways. Believers can follow his example, letting their study of God lead to praise, trusting God when they do not understand, and living for the glory of the One from whom, through whom, and to whom are all things.

## Chapter Prayer

Faithful God, thank You that You have not rejected Your people and that Your gifts and calling are irrevocable. Thank You for preserving a remnant by grace in every generation, and for including me in Your family by grace alone. Keep me humble, remembering that I am a branch grafted in, supported by the root You planted. Forgive Your church for every arrogance and cruelty shown toward the Jewish people, and fill me with love for them. I pray that many would come to know Jesus as their Messiah. When I do not understand Your ways, help me to trust Your wisdom. Oh the depth of the riches of Your wisdom and knowledge! From You and through You and to You are all things. To You be the glory forever. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
