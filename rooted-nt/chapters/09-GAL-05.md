# Galatians 5
theme: Freedom in Christ and the Fruit of the Spirit
main: Paul calls the Galatians to stand firm in the freedom Christ has won and not return to slavery under the Law, warning that seeking righteousness through circumcision means falling away from grace. In Christ, what counts is faith working through love. Yet freedom is not a license for selfishness; it is the freedom to serve one another in love, which fulfills the whole Law. Paul describes the conflict between the flesh and the Spirit, lists the works of the flesh, and then describes the fruit the Spirit produces: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Those who live by the Spirit are called to keep in step with the Spirit.

## Chapter Background and Story

Galatians 5 is the turning point of the letter. In chapters 1–4, Paul defended the gospel of grace and argued that believers are justified by faith in Christ, not by the works of the Law. Now he turns to the practical question: What does this freedom look like in daily life?

There were two dangers in Galatia. The first was legalism: the belief that one must keep the Law, especially circumcision, to be right with God. Paul addresses this in 5:1–12. The second danger was the opposite: if believers are not under the Law, might they live however they please? Paul addresses this in 5:13–26. His answer is that Christian freedom is not freedom to indulge selfish desires but freedom to love. The power for this new life is not the Law but the Holy Spirit.

This chapter contains some of the most beloved and practical teaching in Paul's letters, especially the description of the fruit of the Spirit.

### Stand Firm in Freedom (5:1–6)

Paul's opening call to freedom is studied below. He then speaks very directly. [[v2|Behold, I, Paul, tell you that if you receive circumcision, Christ will profit you nothing]]. Paul is not condemning circumcision as a physical act. He himself had Timothy circumcised for practical reasons of ministry (Acts 16:3). His point is that if the Galatians accepted circumcision as necessary for righteousness, they would be trusting in something other than Christ, and Christ would be of no benefit to them.

[[v3|Yes, I testify again to every man who receives circumcision that he is a debtor to do the whole law]]. Circumcision was the entrance into a whole system. Those who relied on it for righteousness would be obligated to keep the entire Law perfectly. [[v4|You are alienated from Christ, you who desire to be justified by the law. You have fallen away from grace]]. To seek righteousness through the Law is to step away from the realm of grace. Christians have discussed what "fallen away from grace" implies for questions of whether salvation can be lost, and they have answered differently. But all agree on Paul's main point: grace and law-righteousness cannot be mixed. One must choose which to rely on.

[[v5|For we through the Spirit, by faith wait for the hope of righteousness]]. Believers wait in hope for the final declaration of their righteousness at Christ's return, and they wait through the Spirit and by faith, not by works.

[[v6|For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision amounts to anything, but faith working through love]]. This is a key verse. Neither being circumcised nor being uncircumcised gives anyone an advantage before God. What matters is faith, and genuine faith shows itself in love. Paul's teaching that we are justified by faith alone never meant that faith stands alone without producing love.

### Who Hindered You? (5:7–12)

[[v7|You were running well! Who interfered with you that you should not obey the truth?]] Paul uses the image of a race. The Galatians had started well, but someone had cut in on them. [[v8|This persuasion is not from him who calls you]]. The new teaching did not come from God. [[v9|A little yeast grows through the whole lump]]. A small amount of false teaching can spread and corrupt a whole community (see 1 Corinthians 5:6).

[[v10|I have confidence toward you in the Lord that you will think no other way. But he who troubles you will bear his judgment, whoever he is]]. Paul trusts that the Galatians will return to the truth. Those who are leading them astray will answer to God.

[[v11|But I, brothers, if I still preach circumcision, why am I still persecuted? Then the stumbling block of the cross has been removed]]. Some apparently claimed that Paul himself preached circumcision in some settings. Paul answers that if he did, he would not be persecuted. The message of the cross is offensive precisely because it says that nothing people can do, not even religious rituals, can earn salvation.

[[v12|I wish that those who disturb you would cut themselves off]]. This is one of Paul's harshest statements. The phrase can mean "castrate themselves," a biting comment directed at those so obsessed with circumcision. Some pagan priests in the region practiced self-castration, and Paul may be suggesting that the false teachers' insistence on cutting the flesh was no better than pagan ritual. His sharp words show how seriously he took their harm to the Galatians. It is a moment of raw frustration and anger at those who were endangering his spiritual children.

### Called to Freedom and Love (5:13–15)

Paul's teaching on the purpose of freedom is studied below. [[v15|But if you bite and devour one another, be careful that you don’t consume one another]]. The conflict in Galatia had become vicious. Paul warns that a community that fights like wild animals will destroy itself.

### Walking by the Spirit (5:16–26)

Paul now describes the inner conflict between the flesh and the Spirit, and the remedy, which are studied below. He then lists the works of the flesh. [[v19|Now the deeds of the flesh are obvious, which are: adultery, sexual immorality, uncleanness, lustfulness]], [[v20|idolatry, sorcery, hatred, strife, jealousies, outbursts of anger, rivalries, divisions, heresies]], [[v21|envy, murders, drunkenness, orgies, and things like these]]. The list includes sexual sins, religious sins, social sins, and sins of excess. Notice how many involve broken relationships: hatred, strife, jealousy, anger, rivalries, divisions, and envy. These were exactly the problems tearing the Galatian church apart. Paul warns that [[v21|those who practice such things will not inherit God’s Kingdom]]. He is describing a settled pattern of life, not an occasional failure that a believer repents of.

The fruit of the Spirit, which stands in contrast to these works, is studied below. [[v24|Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh with its passions and lusts]]. At conversion, believers decisively rejected the old way of life, nailing it to the cross with Christ (see Galatians 2:20). [[v25|If we live by the Spirit, let’s also walk by the Spirit]]. The word "walk" here means to keep in line or keep in step, like soldiers marching in formation. Since the Spirit has given believers life, they are to follow His lead in daily conduct. [[v26|Let’s not become conceited, provoking one another, and envying one another]]. Pride and competition are among the clearest signs of the flesh at work in a church.

## Important Verses Explained

### Galatians 5:1 — Stand Firm in Freedom

verse: Galatians 5:1

#### Simple Explanation

This verse summarizes everything Paul has said so far. "Stand firm therefore in the liberty by which Christ has made us free." The purpose of Christ's work was freedom. He did not set people free so they could be put back into another form of slavery.

Freedom here means freedom from the Law as a means of being right with God, freedom from the curse of the Law, freedom from sin's power, and freedom from the "elemental principles" that once held them captive (Galatians 4:3, 9). Believers have been set free to live as God's children.

"Stand firm" means to hold one's ground, like a soldier refusing to retreat. The Galatians were being pressured to give up their freedom. Paul calls them to resist.

"Don't be entangled again with a yoke of bondage." A yoke was a wooden frame placed on the necks of animals to make them pull loads. In Jewish teaching, the Law was sometimes called a "yoke." Peter described it at the Jerusalem Council as a yoke that neither the fathers nor his generation had been able to bear (Acts 15:10). Paul warns the Galatians not to put that yoke back on their necks as a way to earn righteousness. The word "again" reminds them that before Christ they had already lived under a different kind of bondage, the bondage of idolatry.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Freedom is one of the great themes of the gospel. Jesus announced that He came to proclaim release to the captives (Luke 4:18). He said, [[John 8:36|If therefore the Son makes you free, you will be free indeed]]. Christian freedom is not merely political or social freedom, though it has implications for both. It is the deep, inner freedom of being right with God and released from the condemnation and power of sin.

Jesus also spoke of a yoke, but a very different one. He said, [[Matthew 11:29|Take my yoke upon you and learn from me]], and [[Matthew 11:30|my yoke is easy, and my burden is light]]. Following Christ is not a yoke of bondage but a yoke of rest. His people do not live without direction; they follow Him. But their following comes from love and grace, not from fear and obligation.

Freedom must be guarded. It is possible to lose the joy and experience of freedom by drifting back into legalism, fear, or performance-based religion. That is why Paul says "stand firm."

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many believers live under burdens Christ never placed on them: expectations of perfection, rules invented by people, or constant fear of failing God. This verse invites them to stand in the freedom Christ has given. Their acceptance with God is secure in Christ, and they do not need to earn it.

Standing firm sometimes means resisting pressure from others who want to add requirements to the gospel. It can also mean resisting inner voices that whisper that God's love must be earned. Believers can return again and again to the truth that Christ has made them free.

At the same time, freedom in Christ is not a reason to look down on believers who have different convictions about secondary matters. Paul's concern is about the gospel itself, not about every personal practice. Believers can stand firm in grace while extending grace to others.

### Galatians 5:13–14 — Freedom to Serve in Love

verse: Galatians 5:13-14

#### Simple Explanation

Paul has strongly defended Christian freedom. Now he guards against a misunderstanding. "For you, brothers, were called for freedom." Freedom is God's calling for His people. "Only don't use your freedom as an opportunity for the flesh." The word "opportunity" was used of a military base of operations, a foothold from which to launch an attack. Paul warns that freedom must not become a foothold for selfish desires.

Instead, "through love be servants to one another." This is a beautiful paradox. Believers have been set free from slavery, and now they are called to serve one another, not out of compulsion, but freely and lovingly. True freedom is not freedom from others but freedom for others.

Verse 14 explains: "For the whole law is fulfilled in one word, in this: 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.'" Paul quotes Leviticus 19:18. The Law that the false teachers were so concerned about is fulfilled not by circumcision or calendar observance but by love. When believers love their neighbors, they accomplish what the Law was aiming at all along.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Jesus taught that the greatest commandments are to love God with all one's heart and to love one's neighbor as oneself (Mark 12:29–31). Paul says something similar in Romans 13:8–10: love is the fulfillment of the Law. The Law's commands against adultery, murder, theft, and coveting are all ways of describing what love does and does not do.

This shows that Christian freedom is not lawlessness. Believers are free from the Law as a means of righteousness, but they are not free from the call to love. In fact, the Spirit empowers them to love in a way the Law alone could never produce. As Paul says in Romans 8:4, the righteous requirement of the Law is fulfilled in those who walk according to the Spirit.

The example of Christ shapes this teaching. Jesus, though free and Lord of all, took the form of a servant and washed His disciples' feet (John 13:12–15). He used His freedom to serve. His followers are called to do the same.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Some people think of freedom as the right to do whatever they want. This passage presents a different vision: freedom is the ability to love and serve without fear, compulsion, or selfishness. Believers can use their freedom to care for others in practical ways, such as meeting needs, sharing burdens, and offering kindness.

When believers are unsure what to do in a particular situation, the law of love offers guidance. Does this action serve my neighbor's good? Does it express love? These questions can guide decisions about time, money, speech, and conduct, especially in matters where Scripture gives no specific command.

Paul's warning in verse 15 also applies to modern churches and communities. When believers bite and devour one another through gossip, criticism, and conflict, they harm the whole body. Love calls for patience, forgiveness, and building one another up.

### Galatians 5:16–18 — Walk by the Spirit

verse: Galatians 5:16-18

#### Simple Explanation

How can believers live in freedom without falling into selfishness? Paul's answer is not more rules but the Holy Spirit. "Walk by the Spirit, and you won’t fulfill the lust of the flesh."

To "walk" is to live day by day. Walking by the Spirit means living in dependence on the Spirit, following His guidance, and drawing on His power. When believers do this, they will not carry out the desires of the flesh.

"The flesh" in Paul's letters often refers not to the physical body but to human nature turned in on itself and away from God, the source of selfish and sinful desires. Even after coming to Christ, believers still feel its pull.

Verse 17 describes the inner conflict: "the flesh lusts against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the flesh; and these are contrary to one another." There is a real struggle in the believer's life. The Spirit pulls toward God and love; the flesh pulls toward self and sin. The phrase "that you may not do the things that you desire" may mean that the Spirit prevents believers from simply following the flesh's desires, or that the conflict keeps believers from doing all the good they wish. Either way, the Christian life involves struggle.

Verse 18 brings assurance: "But if you are led by the Spirit, you are not under the law." Those who follow the Spirit's leading do not need the Law as an external guardian. The Spirit leads them into the life the Law described.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage shows that the Christian life is not passive. There is a real battle. Believers are not yet perfect, and the flesh still exerts its pull. Paul describes a similar struggle in Romans 7 and 8. But the struggle itself is a sign of life. Before coming to Christ, there was no Spirit to oppose the flesh. Now there is.

The answer to the flesh is not trying harder under the Law, which only exposes and sometimes stirs up sin (Romans 7:8). The answer is walking by the Spirit. The Spirit gives new desires, new power, and a new direction. As believers yield to Him, the flesh loses its grip.

Christians have described this process in different ways. Some emphasize a decisive work of the Spirit that gives victory over sin; others emphasize a lifelong, gradual process of growth. Paul's language includes both a decisive break (v24) and an ongoing walk (v25). Both are part of the Spirit's work.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Every believer knows the struggle Paul describes: wanting to do right and feeling the pull of selfishness, anger, lust, or pride. This passage offers both realism and hope. The struggle is normal, but believers are not left to fight alone. The Spirit lives in them.

Walking by the Spirit can be practiced in simple ways: beginning the day in prayer, asking for the Spirit's help in moments of temptation, listening to Scripture, and paying attention to the Spirit's quiet promptings toward love and holiness. Over time, these small steps shape a life.

When believers fail, as all do, they need not despair. The presence of the struggle shows that the Spirit is at work. They can confess, receive forgiveness, and keep walking.

### Galatians 5:22–23 — The Fruit of the Spirit

verse: Galatians 5:22-23

#### Simple Explanation

After listing the "deeds of the flesh," Paul describes something entirely different: "the fruit of the Spirit." Notice the contrast between "deeds" and "fruit." Deeds are things people produce by their own effort. Fruit is something that grows naturally from a living source. The qualities Paul lists are not achievements but the result of the Spirit's life within believers.

Notice also that Paul says "fruit," singular. These nine qualities are not separate fruits that believers can choose among. They are one harvest, growing together.

Love comes first, because it is the foundation of all the others (1 Corinthians 13:13). Joy is a deep gladness in God that does not depend on circumstances. Peace is a settled calm that comes from trusting God, and also harmony with others. Patience is the ability to endure difficult people and situations without giving up or lashing out. Kindness is a gracious, generous attitude toward others. Goodness is moral uprightness that actively does good. "Faith" here likely means faithfulness, being trustworthy and reliable. Gentleness, or meekness, is strength under control, a humble and considerate spirit. Self-control is mastery over one's desires and impulses.

"Against such things there is no law." No law forbids these qualities. They fulfill everything the Law aimed at.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The fruit of the Spirit is a portrait of Jesus Christ. He perfectly displayed love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. As the Spirit forms Christ in believers (Galatians 4:19), these qualities grow in them.

The image of fruit has deep roots in Scripture. Psalm 1 describes the righteous person as a tree planted by streams of water, bearing fruit in season. Jesus said He is the vine and His followers are the branches; those who remain in Him bear much fruit (John 15:5). Fruit grows through connection to the source. Believers bear the fruit of the Spirit by remaining connected to Christ.

Fruit also grows slowly. It takes time, seasons, and sometimes pruning. Believers should not be discouraged if they do not see full maturity immediately. The Spirit is patient, and growth is real even when it is gradual.

This list contrasts sharply with the works of the flesh. Many of the works of the flesh destroy relationships; nearly all of the fruit of the Spirit builds them up. The Spirit's work creates community, harmony, and love.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage offers a beautiful picture of what a Spirit-filled life looks like. It is not measured mainly by spiritual gifts, religious activity, or dramatic experiences, but by character. Believers can ask God to grow these qualities in them and can notice where each is growing or lacking.

Because these qualities are fruit, the way to grow them is not mainly by gritting one's teeth but by staying close to Christ through prayer, Scripture, worship, and fellowship. As believers abide in Him, the Spirit produces what they could not produce alone.

This list also provides a helpful way to evaluate leaders, movements, and teachings. Do they produce love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control? Or do they produce strife, division, pride, and envy? The fruit reveals the source.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Galatians 5 shows what Christian freedom looks like. Christ has set believers free, and they are called to stand firm in that freedom. They do not need to earn God's acceptance through religious rituals or rule-keeping. Anything added to faith in Christ as a requirement for righteousness leads back into bondage. What matters is faith working through love.

But freedom is not a license for selfishness. Paul calls believers to use their freedom to serve one another in love, which fulfills the whole Law. Freedom is not freedom from others but freedom for others. This vision challenges both legalism, which adds rules, and license, which ignores God's call to holiness and love.

The key to living this way is the Holy Spirit. Believers face a real struggle between the flesh and the Spirit, but they are not left to fight in their own strength. As they walk by the Spirit, keeping in step with His leading, the desires of the flesh lose their power. The works of the flesh, such as immorality, hatred, strife, jealousy, anger, and division, give way to the fruit of the Spirit.

That fruit, love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control, is a portrait of Christ Himself. It grows gradually as believers stay connected to Him. It is the true mark of a Spirit-filled life and community. In a world full of conflict and selfishness, a church that displays this fruit becomes a living testimony to the freedom and love of Christ.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for setting me free. Help me to stand firm in the freedom You have given me, and keep me from being entangled again in any yoke of bondage. Let my faith work through love. Teach me to use my freedom not to serve myself but to serve others with a willing and joyful heart. Forgive me for the times I have bitten and devoured others with my words. Holy Spirit, I want to walk by You today. When the desires of my flesh pull at me, strengthen me to follow Your leading. Grow Your fruit in me: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Keep me connected to Christ, the true vine, so that my life bears fruit that honors Him and blesses others. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
