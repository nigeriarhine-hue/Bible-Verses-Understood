# John 11
theme: The Resurrection and the Life at the Tomb of Lazarus
main: When His friend Lazarus falls sick, Jesus deliberately waits, and Lazarus dies. Arriving four days later, Jesus meets grieving Martha with the declaration, "I am the resurrection and the life," weeps with Mary and the mourners, and then calls Lazarus out of the tomb alive. This greatest of His signs leads many to believe, but it also moves the council to plan His death, as the high priest unknowingly prophesies that one man will die for the people.

## Chapter Background and Story

John 11 records the seventh and climactic sign of the first half of John's Gospel. Jesus has turned water into wine, healed an official's son, healed a lame man, fed five thousand, walked on water, and given sight to a man born blind. Now He raises a man who has been dead for four days. This sign reveals Jesus as the Lord of life, and it also sets in motion the events that lead to His own death. The one who gives life to Lazarus will give His own life for the world.

The chapter is also deeply personal. It shows Jesus' friendship with a family in Bethany, His love for them, His grief at their loss, and the different ways two sisters bring their pain to Him.

### The News of Lazarus's Sickness (11:1–16)

Lazarus lived in Bethany, a village on the eastern slope of the Mount of Olives, about two miles from Jerusalem. His sisters were Martha and Mary. Luke describes an earlier visit to their home, when Martha was busy serving and Mary sat at Jesus' feet (Luke 10:38–42). John identifies Mary as the one who anointed the Lord, an event he will describe in the next chapter.

The sisters sent a message to Jesus: [[v3|Lord, behold, he for whom you have great affection is sick]]. They did not ask Him to come; they trusted that His love would move Him. Jesus said, [[v4|This sickness is not to death, but for the glory of God, that God’s Son may be glorified by it]]. Lazarus would die, but death would not be the final outcome.

Then comes a surprising sequence, studied below: Jesus loved them, and so He stayed where He was two more days.

When Jesus finally said, [[v7|Let’s go into Judea again]], the disciples objected. The leaders there had just tried to stone Him (10:31). Jesus answered with an image of walking in daylight: [[v9|If a man walks in the day, he doesn’t stumble]]. As long as His appointed time continued, He could walk safely in the Father's will.

He said, [[v11|Our friend Lazarus has fallen asleep, but I am going so that I may awake him out of sleep]]. The disciples took Him literally, so He told them plainly, [[v14|Lazarus is dead]]. He added that He was glad for their sake that He had not been there, [[v15|so that you may believe]].

Thomas, often remembered for his later doubts, showed loyal courage here: [[v16|Let’s also go, that we may die with him]]. He expected that going to Judea meant death for Jesus, and he was willing to share it.

### Jesus and Martha (11:17–27)

When Jesus arrived, Lazarus had been in the tomb four days. Jews buried their dead on the day of death, and there was a popular belief that the soul lingered near the body for three days. After four days, death was considered completely final. Many mourners had come from Jerusalem to comfort the sisters, as Jewish custom encouraged.

Martha, characteristically active, went out to meet Jesus while Mary stayed home. Her words carried both pain and faith: [[v21|Lord, if you would have been here, my brother wouldn’t have died]]. Yet she added, [[v22|Even now I know that whatever you ask of God, God will give you]].

Jesus told her, [[v23|Your brother will rise again]]. Martha replied with the orthodox belief of many Jews of her day, based on texts like Daniel 12:2: [[v24|I know that he will rise again in the resurrection at the last day]]. Jesus then made one of His greatest statements, studied below. Martha answered with a full confession of faith: [[v27|Yes, Lord. I have come to believe that you are the Christ, God’s Son, he who comes into the world]]. Her confession is as complete as Peter's in the other Gospels.

### Jesus and Mary (11:28–37)

Martha went to call Mary secretly: [[v28|The Teacher is here and is calling you]]. Mary rose quickly and went to Him, followed by the mourners, who thought she was going to the tomb to weep. She fell at Jesus' feet and spoke the same words as Martha: [[v32|Lord, if you would have been here, my brother wouldn’t have died]]. But she did not add anything. She simply wept.

Jesus' response to Mary was different from His response to Martha. With Martha, He spoke words of truth. With Mary, He shared her grief. When He saw her weeping and the mourners weeping, [[v33|he groaned in the spirit and was troubled]]. The Greek word translated "groaned" often expresses strong emotion, even indignation. Many interpreters believe Jesus was not only sad but also deeply moved with anger at death itself, the enemy that brings such sorrow into God's world. He asked where Lazarus had been laid, and then came the shortest verse in the English Bible, studied below.

Some mourners said, [[v36|See how much affection he had for him!]] Others said, [[v37|Couldn’t this man, who opened the eyes of him who was blind, have also kept this man from dying?]]

### Lazarus Raised (11:38–44)

Still deeply moved, Jesus came to the tomb, a cave with a stone across the entrance. He said, [[v39|Take away the stone]]. Martha, practical as ever, objected that after four days there would be a stench. Jesus reminded her, [[v40|Didn’t I tell you that if you believed, you would see God’s glory?]]

They removed the stone. Jesus looked up and prayed, thanking the Father for hearing Him. He said He prayed aloud for the sake of the crowd, [[v42|that they may believe that you sent me]]. Then He called Lazarus out. This is studied below.

### The Council's Plot (11:45–57)

Many who saw what Jesus did believed in Him. But others went and reported to the Pharisees. The chief priests and Pharisees called a meeting of the Sanhedrin, the Jewish ruling council. They were alarmed: [[v48|If we leave him alone like this, everyone will believe in him, and the Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation]]. Their concern was political as well as religious. A popular messianic movement could provoke Rome to crush the nation and remove the leaders' authority and the temple ("our place").

Caiaphas, the high priest that year, spoke bluntly: [[v49|You know nothing at all]]. Then he said, [[v50|it is advantageous for us that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation not perish]]. Caiaphas meant this as cold political calculation: sacrifice one man to save the nation from Rome.

But John sees a deeper meaning: [[v51|being high priest that year, he prophesied that Jesus would die for the nation]], [[v52|and not for the nation only, but that he might also gather together into one the children of God who are scattered abroad]]. Without knowing it, Caiaphas spoke the truth of the gospel. Jesus would indeed die in the place of others, not to save them from Rome, but to save them from sin and death, and to gather God's children from every nation into one family.

From that day, the council planned to kill Jesus. The very miracle that gave Lazarus life led to the decision to put Jesus to death. Jesus withdrew to a town called Ephraim near the wilderness. As Passover approached, crowds in Jerusalem wondered whether He would come to the feast, and the leaders ordered that anyone who knew where He was should report it so they could arrest Him.

## Important Verses Explained

### John 11:5–6 — Because He Loved Them, He Stayed

verse: John 11:5-6

#### Simple Explanation

Lazarus was seriously ill in Bethany, and his sisters, Martha and Mary, sent word to Jesus. They described Lazarus as the one Jesus loved, trusting that this would be enough to bring Him quickly. Jesus was at that time across the Jordan, perhaps a day's journey away.

John then writes, [[v5|Now Jesus loved Martha, and her sister, and Lazarus]]. John wants readers to be clear about this before telling what happened next. Jesus was not indifferent to this family. He loved each of them personally.

[[v6|When therefore he heard that he was sick, he stayed two days in the place where he was]]. The word "therefore" is surprising. We might expect John to say, "Jesus loved them, so He hurried to them." Instead, John says that because He loved them, He stayed. His delay was not a lack of love but an expression of it.

By the time Jesus arrived, Lazarus had been dead four days. If Jesus had come at once, He might have healed Lazarus before he died, as He had healed many others. But Jesus had a greater purpose. He told His disciples that this sickness was for God's glory (11:4) and that He was glad He had not been there, [[v15|so that you may believe]].

The delay was painful for Martha and Mary. Both said to Jesus, "Lord, if you would have been here, my brother wouldn't have died." They did not understand why He had not come sooner. Yet Jesus' delay led to a far greater revelation of His glory than a healing would have.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses teach that God's delays are not denials of His love. Jesus' love for this family was certain, yet His timing did not match their expectations. He allowed them to go through grief in order to give them something greater: a revelation of Himself as the resurrection and the life.

They also show that God's purposes are larger than our immediate requests. Martha and Mary wanted their brother healed. Jesus intended to raise him from the dead, strengthen the faith of His disciples, and reveal His glory to many. What seemed like a failure to act became a moment of overwhelming grace.

Finally, these verses remind believers that Jesus knows the whole story while they see only part of it. The sisters could not see beyond the tomb. Jesus could. Faith trusts His love even when His timing seems hard to understand.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses speak to believers who are waiting for God to act. Many people pray for healing, help, or change and see no immediate answer. It can feel as though God is far away or does not care. John's words offer assurance: Jesus' love is not measured by the speed of His answers. He may delay because He loves, and because He has purposes that are not yet visible.

They also invite believers to be honest with God about their disappointment. Martha and Mary told Jesus exactly how they felt. He did not rebuke them. Believers can bring their questions and grief to Him, trusting His love even when they do not understand His timing.

Finally, these verses encourage patience and hope. God's timing does not always bring the outcome people want in this life; not every sick loved one is healed. But believers can trust that God's love is constant and that His final word is life, not death.

### John 11:25–26 — I Am the Resurrection and the Life

verse: John 11:25-26

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus had arrived in Bethany four days after Lazarus died. Martha went out to meet Him, grieving and confused. She told Jesus that if He had been there, her brother would not have died. Yet she still believed that God would give Jesus whatever He asked.

Jesus told her that her brother would rise again. Martha understood this as a reference to the final resurrection: [[v24|I know that he will rise again in the resurrection at the last day]]. She believed in the resurrection as a distant future event.

Jesus then said, [[v25|I am the resurrection and the life]]. He moved her faith from an event to a Person. Resurrection is not only something that will happen at the end of time; it is found in Jesus Himself. He does not merely promise resurrection; He is the resurrection.

He continued with two promises. First, [[v25|He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies]]. Believers will still face physical death, but death will not be the end. They will live again. Second, [[v26|Whoever lives and believes in me will never die]]. This refers to spiritual and eternal death. Those who believe in Jesus have eternal life that physical death cannot destroy.

Then Jesus asked Martha a personal question: [[v26|Do you believe this?]] He did not only want her to understand the teaching; He wanted her to trust Him. Martha responded with a great confession of faith in Jesus as the Christ and the Son of God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This is the fifth of Jesus' "I am" sayings in John, and perhaps the most comforting. It declares that Jesus has power over death itself. He would prove it, first by raising Lazarus, and then, more completely, by rising from the dead Himself. Lazarus was raised to mortal life and would die again; Jesus was raised to immortal, glorified life, the first fruits of the final resurrection.

It also shows that eternal life is found in union with Christ. Life is not simply a gift separate from Jesus; it is participation in His life. Those who believe in Him share in His resurrection. Paul writes that believers have been raised with Christ and that their life is hidden with Christ in God (Colossians 3:1–3).

Finally, Jesus' question to Martha shows that this truth demands a personal response. Believing in the resurrection as a doctrine is not the same as trusting Jesus as the resurrection and the life. Faith rests in Him.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses bring hope to those who grieve. When believers lose loved ones who trusted in Christ, they can grieve with hope, knowing that death is not the end. Jesus' promise is that those who believe in Him will live, even though they die. Christian funerals often read these words for this reason.

They also help believers face their own mortality. Death is still an enemy, and it still brings sorrow. But for those in Christ, it is not final. Believers can live with courage, knowing that their future is secure in the One who is the resurrection and the life.

Finally, Jesus' question to Martha is asked of every reader: "Do you believe this?" It is possible to hear these words many times without personally trusting Jesus. He invites each person to place their faith in Him for life beyond death.

### John 11:35 — Jesus Wept

verse: John 11:35

#### Simple Explanation

After speaking with Martha, Jesus met Mary, who came out of the house weeping, followed by mourners from Jerusalem. Mary fell at His feet and said the same words Martha had said: if He had been there, her brother would not have died. Unlike Martha, she did not add a statement of faith. She simply wept.

John tells us that when Jesus saw her weeping, and the others weeping with her, [[v33|he groaned in the spirit and was troubled]]. He asked where Lazarus had been laid. The mourners said, [[v34|Lord, come and see]].

Then: [[v35|Jesus wept]]. This is the shortest verse in most English Bibles, but it is one of the most profound. The word for Jesus' weeping is different from the loud wailing of the mourners. It suggests quiet tears.

Why did Jesus weep, when He knew that He was about to raise Lazarus from the dead? He was not weeping because He had lost hope. He wept because He shared the sorrow of His friends. He was moved by their grief and by the painful reality of death itself, which brings such sorrow into the world God made.

The mourners noticed: [[v36|See how much affection he had for him!]] Jesus' tears showed His love.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the full humanity of Jesus. The eternal Word, who was with God and was God, became flesh and felt human emotions, including grief. He was not distant from human pain. Hebrews says that we do not have a high priest who cannot sympathize with our weaknesses (Hebrews 4:15).

It also reveals the heart of God toward human suffering. Jesus is the perfect image of the Father. When He weeps at a grave, He shows that God is not indifferent to the sorrow of His people. God hates death and grieves with those who grieve.

Finally, Jesus' tears show that faith and grief can exist together. Jesus knew He would raise Lazarus, yet He wept. Christians who know that their loved ones will be raised can still grieve. Paul writes that believers do not grieve [[1 Thessalonians 4:13|like the rest, who have no hope]], but they do grieve. Hope does not cancel sorrow; it transforms it.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse brings great comfort to those who are grieving. Jesus understands sorrow. He does not stand at a distance from human pain but enters into it. Those who mourn can bring their tears to Him, knowing that He shares their grief.

It also gives believers permission to grieve. Some people feel that strong faith means never crying or always appearing joyful. But Jesus wept. Grief is a natural and right response to loss. Believers can weep honestly while still holding on to hope.

Finally, the verse teaches believers how to care for those who are grieving. Jesus did not rush to explain or correct Mary. He wept with her. Paul instructs believers to [[Romans 12:15|weep with those who weep]]. Sometimes the most loving response to someone's pain is not words but presence and shared sorrow.

### John 11:43–44 — Lazarus, Come Out!

verse: John 11:43-44

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus stood before the tomb of Lazarus, a cave with a stone across the entrance. He had told them to remove the stone, despite Martha's concern about the smell after four days. Jesus prayed aloud, thanking the Father for hearing Him, so that the crowd would believe that the Father had sent Him.

Then [[v43|he cried with a loud voice, “Lazarus, come out!”]] Jesus did not use elaborate rituals. He spoke with authority. Many have observed that if Jesus had not called Lazarus by name, every grave might have opened at His command. Earlier, He had said, [[John 5:28|the hour comes in which all who are in the tombs will hear his voice]]. Here, one man in one tomb heard His voice.

[[v44|He who was dead came out, bound hand and foot with wrappings, and his face was wrapped around with a cloth]]. Lazarus emerged still wrapped in burial cloths. Jewish burial customs involved wrapping the body in strips of linen with spices. The sight must have been astonishing: a man four days dead walking out of his grave.

Jesus then gave a practical command: [[v44|Free him, and let him go]]. He involved the bystanders in the final step, removing the grave clothes and releasing Lazarus to his family.

This was not a resurrection to eternal, glorified life. Lazarus would eventually die again. But it was a powerful sign pointing to Jesus' authority over death and to the final resurrection.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This miracle demonstrates that Jesus is truly the resurrection and the life, as He had just told Martha. His words have power to give life to the dead. What He did for Lazarus physically, He does spiritually for all who believe: He calls them out of spiritual death into new life. Paul writes that God made believers alive with Christ [[Ephesians 2:5|even when we were dead through our trespasses]].

It also points forward to Jesus' own resurrection. There are important differences. Lazarus came out bound in grave clothes; Jesus' grave clothes were left lying in the tomb (John 20:6–7). Lazarus was raised to die again; Jesus was raised never to die again. Lazarus's raising is a preview; Jesus' resurrection is the victory.

The command "Free him, and let him go" can also be seen as a picture of the community's role. Jesus gives life, but He calls others to help remove what still binds the one who has been raised. New believers often need the love and support of the church to grow in freedom.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage gives hope that no situation is beyond Jesus' power. Lazarus had been dead four days, and everyone considered his case hopeless. Jesus called him out. Believers can bring situations that seem dead and hopeless to Christ, trusting that He has power to bring life, even if His timing and ways differ from what they expect.

It also encourages believers to pray for those who are spiritually dead. No one is too far gone for Jesus' voice to reach. Believers can pray with confidence for friends and family who do not yet know Christ, trusting that His word has power to give life.

Finally, the passage calls believers to help others walk in freedom. Many people who have come to faith still carry the "grave clothes" of past habits, wounds, or fears. The church is called to come alongside them with patience and love, helping them grow into the freedom Christ has given.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

John 11 shows that Jesus' love does not always look the way people expect. He loved Martha, Mary, and Lazarus, and yet He delayed. Believers who are waiting for God to act can take comfort that His delays are not signs of indifference. He sees the whole story and is working for His glory and their good, even when they cannot understand His timing.

The chapter also shows how Jesus meets people in grief. To Martha, He spoke truth about Himself. To Mary, He offered His tears. He meets each person in the way they need. Those who are grieving can bring their pain honestly to Him, knowing that He weeps with them and also speaks words of hope. Those who comfort others can learn from Him to listen, to share sorrow, and to point gently to hope.

At the center of the chapter is Jesus' declaration that He is the resurrection and the life. For believers, death is real and painful, but it is not the end. Those who trust in Christ will live, even though they die. This truth gives courage in facing death, hope in grieving loved ones, and a reason to share the gospel with those who do not yet believe.

Finally, the chapter reveals that Jesus' gift of life came at a cost. The raising of Lazarus led directly to the plot to kill Jesus. Caiaphas's words, spoken in political calculation, became a prophecy of the gospel: one man would die for the people. The life believers receive comes through Jesus' death in their place. That is the deepest reason for gratitude and worship.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the resurrection and the life. Thank You that whoever believes in You will live, even though they die, and that death does not have the final word. When You seem slow to answer my prayers, help me to trust that Your love is real and that You see what I cannot see. Thank You for weeping at the tomb of Your friend, and for understanding the sorrow I carry. Meet me in my grief as You met Martha and Mary, with Your truth and with Your tears. Speak life into the places in my life and in the lives of those I love that seem dead and hopeless. Help me to come alongside others whom You have raised, helping them to walk in freedom. Thank You for dying in my place so that I could live. I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who came into the world. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
