# Galatians 6
theme: Bearing Burdens and Boasting in the Cross
main: Paul shows how life in the Spirit works out in community: gently restoring those caught in sin, bearing one another's burdens to fulfill the law of Christ, examining one's own work rather than comparing, and supporting those who teach. He warns that people reap what they sow, encourages believers not to grow weary in doing good, and urges them to do good to everyone, especially fellow believers. Writing the conclusion in his own hand, Paul exposes the motives of the false teachers and declares that he will boast only in the cross of Christ, through which the world has been crucified to him, because what matters is a new creation.

## Chapter Background and Story

Galatians 6 completes the practical section that began in chapter 5. Paul has described the freedom of believers and the contrast between the works of the flesh and the fruit of the Spirit. Now he shows what Spirit-led living looks like in the everyday life of a church. The Galatian believers had been "biting and devouring" one another (Galatians 5:15). Paul gives them a very different vision: a community that restores, supports, shares, and does good.

Then, in verse 11, Paul takes the pen from the scribe who had been writing his words and writes the closing lines himself. In the ancient world, letters were often dictated, and the author would add a personal note at the end to confirm authenticity. Paul uses his closing to summarize the whole letter. He contrasts the false teachers, who wanted to boast in outward religious achievements, with his own boast in the cross of Christ.

### Restoring and Bearing Burdens (6:1–5)

Paul's instructions about restoring those caught in sin and bearing burdens are studied below.

[[v3|For if a man thinks himself to be something when he is nothing, he deceives himself]]. Pride makes it impossible to bear others' burdens, because a proud person thinks they are above such things. [[v4|But let each man examine his own work, and then he will have reason to boast in himself, and not in someone else]]. Instead of comparing themselves with others, believers should honestly evaluate their own lives before God. Any satisfaction should come from faithfulness to their own calling, not from feeling superior to others.

[[v5|For each man will bear his own burden]]. This might seem to contradict verse 2, but Paul uses different words. In verse 2, "burdens" refers to heavy loads too much for one person to carry alone. In verse 5, the word refers to a pack, the normal load each person carries, such as their own responsibility before God. Believers help one another with crushing burdens, while each remains responsible for their own life and calling.

### Sharing with Teachers (6:6)

[[v6|But let him who is taught in the word share all good things with him who teaches]]. Those who receive teaching in God's word should support their teachers, including materially. Paul had chosen not to accept support from some churches for strategic reasons (1 Corinthians 9:12–15), but he affirmed the principle that those who teach the gospel should be supported (1 Corinthians 9:14). In Galatia, this may also have been a gentle encouragement to support faithful teachers rather than the false teachers who were drawing attention to themselves.

### Sowing and Reaping (6:7–10)

The principle of sowing and reaping, and the call not to grow weary, are studied below.

### Paul's Closing in His Own Hand (6:11–18)

[[v11|See with what large letters I write to you with my own hand]]. Paul's large handwriting may have been due to poor eyesight (see Galatians 4:15), to his lack of practice as a scribe, or to his desire to emphasize these final words, much as someone today might use bold or capital letters.

[[v12|As many as desire to make a good impression in the flesh compel you to be circumcised, just so they may not be persecuted for the cross of Christ]]. Paul exposes the false teachers' motives. They wanted to look good to others, perhaps to Jewish communities who might otherwise persecute them for associating with uncircumcised Gentiles. By getting the Gentile believers circumcised, they could avoid the offense of the cross.

[[v13|For even they who receive circumcision don’t keep the law themselves, but they desire to have you circumcised, so that they may boast in your flesh]]. The false teachers did not even keep the whole Law themselves. Their real goal was to boast in the number of Gentiles they had brought into conformity, as trophies of their influence. Paul's own boast is studied below.

[[v15|For in Christ Jesus neither is circumcision anything, nor uncircumcision, but a new creation]]. This repeats Galatians 5:6 in new words. Outward religious marks do not matter. What matters is the new creation that God brings about in Christ, transforming people from within and beginning the renewal of all things (2 Corinthians 5:17).

[[v16|As many as walk by this rule, peace and mercy be on them, and on God’s Israel]]. "This rule" is the principle Paul just stated: the cross and the new creation are what count. Interpreters differ on the meaning of "God's Israel." Some understand it as a description of the whole church, Jewish and Gentile believers together, as the true people of God in Christ (compare Galatians 3:29). Others think Paul is extending a blessing to Jewish believers specifically, or to the Jewish people whom he hoped would yet turn to Christ (Romans 11:26). Whatever the precise reference, Paul's blessing of peace and mercy reflects God's faithfulness to His promises.

[[v17|From now on, let no one cause me any trouble, for I bear the marks of the Lord Jesus branded on my body]]. The false teachers wanted to put the mark of circumcision on the Galatians' bodies. Paul bore different marks: the scars from beatings, stoning, and persecution suffered for Christ (2 Corinthians 11:23–25). In the ancient world, slaves were sometimes branded with their owner's mark. Paul's scars showed that he belonged to Jesus.

[[v18|The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit, brothers. Amen]]. The letter that began with grace (Galatians 1:3) ends with grace. Despite the sharp words throughout, Paul still calls them "brothers." His final word is a blessing.

## Important Verses Explained

### Galatians 6:1–2 — Restore Gently and Bear One Another's Burdens

verse: Galatians 6:1-2

#### Simple Explanation

Paul now shows what walking by the Spirit looks like when someone in the community falls into sin. "Brothers, even if a man is caught in some fault." The word "caught" suggests being overtaken or surprised by sin, like someone tripped up. Paul is describing a believer who has stumbled.

"You who are spiritual must restore such a one." "Spiritual" here means those who are walking by the Spirit and bearing His fruit (Galatians 5:22–25), not people who think themselves superior. The word "restore" was used for setting a broken bone or mending a torn fishing net. The goal is healing, not punishment or humiliation.

"In a spirit of gentleness." Gentleness is part of the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:23). Restoration must be done kindly and humbly.

"Looking to yourself so that you also aren’t tempted." Those who help others must stay humble and watchful, knowing that they too are vulnerable to temptation. Pride can be its own form of falling.

Verse 2 widens the principle: "Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ." Believers are to help carry the heavy loads others face, whether temptations, sorrows, needs, or struggles. In doing this, they fulfill "the law of Christ," which is the law of love that Christ taught and lived (John 13:34; Galatians 5:14).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

"The law of Christ" is a striking phrase in a letter that warns so strongly against returning to the Law of Moses. Paul is not introducing a new set of rules. He is pointing to Christ's way of love. Christ bore the heaviest burden of all, the sin of the world, on the cross. His followers imitate Him when they help carry the burdens of others.

This passage gives a model for how churches should respond to sin among their members. Jesus taught a similar approach in Matthew 18:15–17, beginning with private, gentle conversation and aiming at restoration. The goal is always to bring the person back to health and fellowship, not to shame them. James 5:19–20 likewise speaks of the great good done when someone turns a wanderer back.

At the same time, gentle restoration does not mean ignoring serious harm. When sin involves abuse or crimes against others, those harmed must be protected, and appropriate authorities must be involved. Restoration of the offender's relationship with God never requires the victim to stay at risk or to keep silent. Gentleness toward the one who has fallen goes together with justice and care for those who have been hurt.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

When a fellow believer stumbles, the natural reactions are often gossip, judgment, or avoidance. This passage calls for something better: a humble, gentle effort to help them return. That might mean a private conversation, an offer of support, prayer together, or walking alongside them as they rebuild.

Bearing one another's burdens can take many practical forms: bringing meals to a grieving family, helping someone through a financial crisis, sitting with a friend in depression, or being an accountability partner for someone fighting temptation. These simple acts of love fulfill the law of Christ.

The warning to "look to yourself" is also important. Those who help others should remain humble, aware of their own weaknesses, and careful not to place themselves in situations of temptation.

### Galatians 6:7–8 — Whatever a Person Sows, They Will Reap

verse: Galatians 6:7-8

#### Simple Explanation

Paul uses another image from farming. "Don’t be deceived. God is not mocked, for whatever a man sows, that he will also reap." Just as a farmer who plants wheat harvests wheat, the choices people make produce corresponding results. No one can fool God by sowing one thing and expecting to reap another.

"God is not mocked" means God cannot be outwitted or treated with contempt. People may think they can live however they please without consequences, but God's moral order stands.

Verse 8 describes two kinds of sowing. "He who sows to his own flesh will from the flesh reap corruption." To sow to the flesh means investing one's life in selfish desires, the works of the flesh listed in Galatians 5:19–21. The harvest is "corruption," decay and ruin. "But he who sows to the Spirit will from the Spirit reap eternal life." To sow to the Spirit means investing one's life in what pleases God, following the Spirit's leading. The harvest is eternal life.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The principle of sowing and reaping runs throughout Scripture (Job 4:8; Proverbs 22:8; Hosea 8:7). It reflects the moral order God has built into His world. Choices matter, and they have consequences, both in this life and in eternity.

This principle might seem to contradict Paul's teaching that salvation is by grace through faith. But Paul sees no contradiction. Eternal life is God's gift through Christ, received by faith. Yet genuine faith, through the Spirit, leads to a life that "sows to the Spirit." A life that persistently sows to the flesh shows that the person is not walking with God. Paul is not teaching salvation by works but describing the direction of a life.

It is also important not to apply this principle mechanically. Not every hardship is a direct result of someone's own sin. Jesus rejected that idea in John 9:1–3, and Job's suffering was not caused by his wrongdoing. The principle describes the general moral order and the final outcome of a life, not a formula to explain every difficulty.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Every day, people sow seeds through their choices: what they watch, how they spend money, how they treat others, how they use their time, and what they feed their minds. This verse invites believers to consider what kind of harvest those seeds will produce. Small choices, repeated over time, shape a life.

This principle offers both warning and hope. It warns that sinful habits will eventually produce painful harvests. But it also promises that faithful choices, such as prayer, honesty, kindness, and generosity, will produce a good harvest. Sowing to the Spirit is never wasted.

For those who have sown poorly in the past, the gospel offers forgiveness and a fresh start. Consequences may remain, but God's grace allows them to begin sowing to the Spirit today.

### Galatians 6:9–10 — Do Not Grow Weary in Doing Good

verse: Galatians 6:9-10

#### Simple Explanation

Farmers know that harvest does not come immediately. There is a long season of waiting between sowing and reaping. Paul applies this to doing good. "Let’s not be weary in doing good, for we will reap in due season if we don’t give up."

Doing good can be tiring. People may not notice or appreciate it. Results may be slow. Paul acknowledges the danger of weariness and discouragement. But he promises that a harvest will come "in due season," at the right time, God's time. The condition is perseverance: "if we don’t give up."

Verse 10 draws the practical conclusion: "So then, as we have opportunity, let’s do what is good toward all men, and especially toward those who are of the household of the faith." Believers should use the opportunities they have to do good to everyone, not just to fellow Christians. At the same time, they have a special responsibility toward their spiritual family, "the household of the faith."

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses connect with the sowing image of verses 7–8. Doing good is sowing to the Spirit, and the harvest is sure, even when it is delayed. Paul expresses similar encouragement in 1 Corinthians 15:58, urging the Corinthians to keep working, [[1 Corinthians 15:58|because you know that your labor is not in vain in the Lord]].

The phrase "as we have opportunity" suggests both readiness and urgency. Opportunities to do good come and go. Life is short, and the time to act is now.

The order of "all men" and [[v10|especially toward those who are of the household of the faith]] reflects a balance. Christian love is not limited to fellow believers; it reaches out to all people, as Jesus taught in the parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37). Yet the church is a family, and family members have particular responsibilities to care for one another. In Galatia, the church's internal conflict made this reminder especially important.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many believers grow weary in doing good: parents raising children, caregivers looking after aging relatives, volunteers serving in thankless roles, pastors laboring in difficult places. This verse speaks directly to them. Their work is not in vain. The harvest will come in God's time.

Weariness is not the same as failure. Sometimes rest, help from others, and renewed time with God are needed to continue. Jesus Himself withdrew to rest and pray. Perseverance does not mean ignoring one's limits; it means not abandoning the good work God has given.

Believers can also look for opportunities to do good each day, toward neighbors, coworkers, strangers, and especially toward fellow believers in need. Small acts of kindness, faithfully repeated, can produce a harvest far greater than anyone expects.

### Galatians 6:14 — Boasting Only in the Cross

verse: Galatians 6:14

#### Simple Explanation

In contrast to the false teachers, who wanted to boast in the number of Gentiles they had circumcised, Paul declares, "But far be it from me to boast except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ."

In the Roman world, the cross was an object of horror and shame. It was the most humiliating form of execution, reserved for slaves and rebels. No one would ever boast about a cross. Yet Paul says the cross is his only boast. Everything he values, his forgiveness, his righteousness, his relationship with God, comes from Christ's death on the cross.

"Through which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world." "The world" here refers to the whole system of values, status, and religious achievement that stands apart from God. Through the cross, that world has lost its power and attraction for Paul. It is as if the world is dead to him and he is dead to it. He no longer looks to the world for his identity, approval, or security.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse sums up the whole letter. The false teachers offered a religion of outward achievement: circumcision, calendar observance, and a good impression. Paul offers the cross, where all human achievement is set aside and God's grace is everything. Boasting in the cross means acknowledging that salvation is entirely God's gift.

Paul makes a similar statement in 1 Corinthians 2:2: [[1 Corinthians 2:2|For I determined not to know anything among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified]]. And in Philippians 3:7–8, he says he counts everything as loss compared with knowing Christ. For Paul, the cross was not just the means of salvation but the center of his whole life and identity.

The double crucifixion, "the world... crucified to me, and I to the world," echoes Galatians 2:20 ("I have been crucified with Christ") and Galatians 5:24 ("Those who belong to Christ have crucified the flesh"). Union with Christ in His death breaks the grip of self, flesh, and world, and opens the way to the new creation (v15).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

People are always tempted to boast in something: achievements, appearance, wealth, education, family background, spiritual accomplishments, or even church involvement. This verse calls believers to a different boast. Their worth and hope rest not on what they have done but on what Christ has done for them on the cross.

Being crucified to the world means that its values no longer control a believer's life. Popularity, success, and status lose their power to define them. This brings tremendous freedom. Believers can live for God's approval, serve humbly, and endure rejection, because their identity is anchored in the cross.

This verse also invites believers to keep the cross central in their faith, worship, and witness. The message of Christ crucified is still the power of God for salvation, and it remains the only true ground for boasting.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Galatians 6 shows how the freedom and life of the Spirit shape a Christian community. When a believer falls into sin, those walking by the Spirit should restore them gently and humbly, aiming at healing rather than shame, while also ensuring that anyone who has been harmed is protected and cared for. Believers are called to bear one another's burdens, fulfilling the law of Christ, the law of self-giving love that He displayed on the cross.

The chapter also calls for honest self-examination instead of comparison. Each person is responsible for their own life and calling before God. At the same time, the community supports those who teach God's word and shares good things with them.

Paul's teaching on sowing and reaping reminds believers that their choices matter. Sowing to the flesh leads to decay; sowing to the Spirit leads to eternal life. This is not salvation by works, but a description of the direction and outcome of a life. For those who grow weary in doing good, Paul offers a promise: the harvest will come in due season. Believers can keep seizing opportunities to do good to all people, especially to their spiritual family.

Finally, Paul closes the letter by pointing to the cross. The false teachers wanted to boast in outward achievements, but Paul boasts only in the cross of Christ. There, the world's values lost their hold on him, and he found his identity in Christ. What matters is not religious performance but the new creation God brings about. This is the heart of the gospel: grace, received through faith, producing new life in Christ.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You carried the heaviest burden of all when You bore my sin on the cross. Make me willing to carry the burdens of others. When a brother or sister stumbles, give me a gentle and humble spirit to help restore them, and keep me watchful over my own heart. Free me from comparing myself with others. Help me to sow to the Spirit in the choices I make each day. When I grow weary in doing good, renew my strength and remind me that the harvest will come in Your time. Show me the opportunities You give me to do good to everyone, especially to Your family. Far be it from me to boast in anything except Your cross. Let the world lose its grip on my heart, and make me a new creation in You. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
