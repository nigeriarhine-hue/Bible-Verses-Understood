# Luke 9
theme: Who Jesus Is, and the Road He Sets His Face Toward
main: Jesus sends out the Twelve, feeds five thousand, and hears Peter confess Him as the Christ of God, then teaches that He must suffer and that His followers must take up their cross. On the mountain, while He prays, His glory is revealed and Moses and Elijah speak of the "departure" He will accomplish in Jerusalem. After further lessons in humility and mercy, Jesus resolutely sets His face toward Jerusalem and warns that following Him requires wholehearted, forward-looking commitment.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 9 is a hinge chapter in Luke's Gospel. Its first half concludes Jesus' ministry in Galilee, focusing on His identity: Herod asks who He is, Peter confesses Him as the Christ, and the Father declares Him His beloved Son. Its second half begins the long journey to Jerusalem, which occupies Luke 9:51–19:27, often called the "travel narrative." Much of Jesus' teaching found only in Luke appears during this journey.

Several events in this chapter are studied in depth in Matthew's and Mark's commentaries: the feeding of the five thousand (Mark 6:31), Peter's confession (Matthew 16:16), taking up the cross (Matthew 16:24), the cost of gaining the world (Mark 8:36), the transfiguration (Matthew 17:5), the healing of the boy (Mark 9:24), greatness and the child (Matthew 18:3–4), and the outsider who cast out demons (Mark 9:40). This chapter highlights Luke's distinctive emphases.

### The Mission of the Twelve (9:1–6)

Jesus called the Twelve together and gave them [[v1|power and authority over all demons, and to cure diseases]]. He sent them out to proclaim the kingdom of God and to heal. They were to take nothing for the journey: no staff, bag, bread, money, or extra tunic. They were to stay in whatever house welcomed them, and if a town rejected them, they were to shake the dust off their feet as a testimony. They went from village to village, preaching the gospel and healing everywhere.

### Herod's Question (9:7–9)

Herod Antipas heard about everything that was happening and was perplexed. Some said John had been raised from the dead; others said Elijah had appeared; others said one of the ancient prophets had come back to life. Herod said, [[v9|I beheaded John, but who is this about whom I hear such things?]] He wanted to see Jesus. Herod's question, "Who is this?", introduces the chapter's central theme. Herod would finally see Jesus during His trial, hoping to see a miracle, but would only mock Him (Luke 23:8–11).

### Feeding the Five Thousand (9:10–17)

When the apostles returned, they told Jesus what they had done. He took them privately toward a town called Bethsaida, but the crowds followed. Jesus [[v11|welcomed them]], spoke to them about the kingdom of God, and healed those who needed it. Luke's word "welcomed" shows Jesus' openness even when His plans for rest were interrupted.

Late in the day, the Twelve urged Him to send the crowd away to find food and lodging. Jesus said, [[v13|You give them something to eat]]. They had only five loaves and two fish. Jesus had the people sit in groups of about fifty, took the loaves and fish, looked up to heaven, blessed and broke them, and gave them to the disciples to distribute. Everyone ate and was satisfied, and twelve baskets of leftovers were collected.

### Peter's Confession and the Call to Follow (9:18–27)

Luke alone tells us that Jesus asked His disciples about His identity [[v18|As he was praying alone]]. Prayer comes before every major turning point in Luke's account. Jesus asked who the crowds said He was. They gave the same answers Herod had heard: John the Baptist, Elijah, or an ancient prophet. Then He asked, [[v20|But who do you say that I am?]] Peter answered, [[v20|The Christ of God]], meaning God's anointed Messiah.

Jesus warned them not to tell anyone and explained that [[v22|The Son of Man must suffer many things]], be rejected, be killed, and be raised on the third day. Then He said to everyone, [[v23|If anyone desires to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me]]. (Many manuscripts of Luke add the word "daily," emphasizing that cross-bearing is an everyday commitment.) Whoever tries to save their life will lose it, but whoever loses their life for Jesus' sake will save it. What good is it to gain the whole world and lose oneself? Whoever is ashamed of Jesus and His words, the Son of Man will be ashamed of when He comes in glory. Finally, He said some standing there would not die before they saw the kingdom of God.

### The Transfiguration (9:28–36)

About eight days later, Jesus took Peter, John, and James up a mountain [[v28|to pray]]. Again Luke emphasizes prayer. [[v29|As he was praying, the appearance of his face was altered, and his clothing became white and dazzling]]. Moses and Elijah appeared in glory and talked with Jesus about [[v31|his departure, which he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem]]. This detail, found only in Luke, is studied below.

Peter and his companions had been very sleepy, but they woke fully and saw Jesus' glory. As Moses and Elijah were leaving, Peter suggested building three shelters, [[v33|not knowing what he said]]. A cloud came and covered them, and they were afraid. A voice from the cloud said, [[v35|This is my beloved Son. Listen to him!]] (Many manuscripts read "my Chosen One.") When the voice had spoken, Jesus was alone. The disciples kept silent about what they had seen.

### The Boy With an Unclean Spirit (9:37–43a)

The next day, a man in the crowd begged Jesus to look at his son, [[v38|for he is my only born child]], a detail Luke adds that deepens the father's anguish. A spirit seized the boy and threw him into convulsions, and the disciples had been unable to drive it out. Jesus rebuked the unclean spirit, healed the boy, and [[v42|gave him back to his father]], an echo of how He gave the widow's son back to his mother in Luke 7:15. Everyone was amazed at the greatness of God.

### Misunderstanding and Pride (9:43b–50)

While everyone marveled, Jesus told His disciples, [[v44|Let these words sink into your ears, for the Son of Man will be delivered up into the hands of men]]. But they did not understand, and Luke says the meaning [[v45|was concealed from them]]; they were afraid to ask Him about it. The full meaning of the cross would be understood only after the resurrection (Luke 24:25–27, 45).

Then an argument started among the disciples about which of them was the greatest. Jesus took a little child, stood the child beside Him, and said that whoever welcomes such a child in His name welcomes Him, and whoever welcomes Him welcomes the Father. [[v48|For whoever is least among you all, this one will be great]]. John then mentioned someone casting out demons in Jesus' name whom they had tried to stop, because he was not one of their group. Jesus said, [[v50|Don’t forbid him, for he who is not against us is for us]].

### Setting His Face Toward Jerusalem (9:51–56)

Now comes the turning point of Luke's Gospel: [[v51|It came to pass, when the days were near that he should be taken up, he intently set his face to go to Jerusalem]]. This verse is studied below.

Jesus sent messengers ahead to a Samaritan village to prepare for His arrival. Samaritans lived in the region between Galilee and Judea. They were descendants of Israelites who had intermarried with other peoples after the Assyrian conquest centuries earlier, and they worshiped on Mount Gerizim rather than in Jerusalem. Jews and Samaritans had a long history of hostility. The Samaritans did not welcome Jesus [[v53|because he was traveling with his face set toward Jerusalem]]. They resented pilgrims heading to the Jerusalem temple.

James and John asked, [[v54|Lord, do you want us to command fire to come down from the sky and destroy them, just as Elijah did?]] They were thinking of Elijah calling down fire on soldiers sent by a king of Samaria (2 Kings 1:9–12). Mark tells us Jesus had nicknamed them [[Mark 3:17|Sons of Thunder]]. But Jesus turned and rebuked them. Many manuscripts add His words, [[v55|You don’t know of what kind of spirit you are]], and [[v56|For the Son of Man didn’t come to destroy men’s lives, but to save them]]. They went on to another village. Jesus refused to respond to rejection with violence. Later in Luke's writings, Samaritans would receive the gospel with joy (Acts 8:4–25), and John himself would go to Samaria to pray for them to receive the Holy Spirit (Acts 8:14–17).

### The Cost of Following Jesus (9:57–62)

As they traveled, three people spoke with Jesus about following Him. One said, [[v57|I want to follow you wherever you go, Lord]]. Jesus replied that foxes have dens and birds have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay His head. Following Him meant giving up security.

Jesus said to another, "Follow me!" The man asked first to go and bury his father. Jesus answered, [[v60|Leave the dead to bury their own dead, but you go and announce God’s Kingdom]]. As discussed at Matthew 8:22, the man may have meant waiting until his father died, which could take years. Jesus' point was that the call of the kingdom takes priority.

A third said, [[v61|I want to follow you, Lord, but first allow me to say good-bye to those who are at my house]]. This request recalls Elisha, who asked Elijah for permission to say goodbye to his parents before following him (1 Kings 19:19–21). Jesus' answer is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 9:31 — The [[Luke 9:31|Departure]] Jesus Would Accomplish

verse: Luke 9:31

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is part of Luke's account of the transfiguration. About eight days after Peter confessed Jesus as the Christ of God, and after Jesus taught that He must suffer, die, and rise, Jesus took Peter, John, and James up a mountain to pray. While He was praying, His face changed and His clothes became dazzling white. Two men appeared and talked with Him: Moses and Elijah.

Only Luke tells us what they talked about: [[v31|who appeared in glory and spoke of his departure, which he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem]].

The word translated "departure" is the Greek word "exodos," from which we get "exodus." It can simply mean "departure" or "death," but in a Jewish context, it would immediately recall the great exodus, when God delivered Israel from slavery in Egypt through Moses. The presence of Moses, the leader of the first exodus, makes the connection even stronger.

Jesus' "exodus" included His death, resurrection, and ascension, His departure from this world back to the Father. Just as the first exodus freed Israel from bondage in Egypt, Jesus' exodus would free His people from bondage to sin and death.

Notice that Luke says this departure was something Jesus would [[v31|accomplish]]. The word means to fulfill or complete. Jesus' death was not an accident or defeat; it was the fulfillment of God's plan, something Jesus would actively carry out.

The setting is also important. The transfiguration took place between Jesus' first prediction of His death (9:22) and His decision to set His face toward Jerusalem (9:51). Moses and Elijah, representing the Law and the Prophets, confirmed that Jesus' coming death was in line with all of Scripture.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that Jesus' death and resurrection are the new and greater exodus. Throughout the Old Testament, the exodus was the defining act of God's salvation. The prophets looked forward to a new exodus, when God would again rescue His people (Isaiah 43:16–19). Jesus fulfilled this hope. Paul calls Christ [[1 Corinthians 5:7|our Passover]], linking His sacrifice to the Passover lamb that marked the first exodus.

The verse also shows that the cross was central to God's plan. Even on the mountain of glory, the conversation was about Jesus' death. Glory and suffering are not opposites in Jesus' story; His glory is revealed through His suffering. This prepared the disciples, though they did not yet understand, for the events in Jerusalem.

Finally, the presence of Moses and Elijah shows that the whole Old Testament points to Jesus. After His resurrection, Jesus explained to His disciples how everything written about Him in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms had to be fulfilled (Luke 24:44).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to see Jesus' death and resurrection as their exodus from slavery. Just as Israel was freed from Egypt, believers have been freed from the power of sin and death through Christ. They can live as free people, no longer slaves to sin.

It also reminds believers that God's plans often include suffering on the way to glory. Jesus' path led through the cross to the resurrection. Believers facing hardship can trust that God is working, and that suffering is not the end of their story.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to read the Old Testament in light of Jesus. The stories of Moses, the exodus, and the prophets all point to Him. Reading Scripture this way deepens understanding of God's unfolding plan of salvation.

### Luke 9:51 — He Set His Face to Go to Jerusalem

verse: Luke 9:51

#### Simple Explanation

This verse marks the turning point of Luke's Gospel. Until now, most of Jesus' ministry has taken place in Galilee. From this point on, Luke describes Jesus as traveling toward Jerusalem, a journey that continues for ten chapters until His arrival in the city (Luke 19:28). Along the way, Jesus teaches many parables and lessons found only in Luke.

[[v51|It came to pass, when the days were near that he should be taken up]]. "Taken up" refers to Jesus' ascension into heaven, but it may also include the whole series of events: His death, resurrection, and ascension. The time for these events was approaching. Just as Moses and Elijah had spoken of His "departure" (9:31), Luke now says the time of His being "taken up" was near.

[[v51|he intently set his face to go to Jerusalem]]. To "set one's face" means to be firmly determined, to resolve with unwavering commitment. The phrase echoes Isaiah 50:7, where the Servant of the Lord says, [[Isaiah 50:7|Therefore I have set my face like a flint, and I know that I won’t be disappointed]]. The Servant in that passage faces mocking and suffering but trusts God to vindicate Him.

Jesus knew what awaited Him in Jerusalem: rejection, suffering, and death. He had told His disciples this repeatedly (9:22, 44). Yet He did not turn away. He resolutely set out toward the city where He would die.

In the very next verses, Jesus faced rejection from a Samaritan village because He was heading toward Jerusalem. His determination to go there cost Him even on the way.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals Jesus' willing obedience and determination to fulfill His mission. He was not dragged to the cross against His will; He chose it. In John's Gospel, Jesus says, [[John 10:18|No one takes it away from me, but I lay it down by myself]]. His resolute face toward Jerusalem shows His love for His Father and for the people He came to save.

It also shows that faithfulness sometimes requires steadfast determination in the face of difficulty. Jesus knew the path ahead was painful, but He did not waver. Hebrews describes Jesus as one who, [[Hebrews 12:2|for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising its shame]].

Finally, the verse reminds believers that Jesus' journey to Jerusalem was a journey toward their salvation. Every step He took brought Him closer to the cross where He would give His life for them.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to follow Jesus with determination. Following Christ may involve difficult paths: standing for truth when it is unpopular, serving when it is costly, or persevering through suffering. Believers can set their faces to obey God, trusting Him with the outcome.

It also encourages believers to remain focused on God's purposes despite distractions and opposition. Jesus faced rejection on the way to Jerusalem, but He did not turn back. Believers may face obstacles and discouragement, but they can keep moving forward in faithfulness.

Finally, this verse invites believers to gratitude. Jesus resolutely walked toward the cross for their sake. Reflecting on His determination to save them can deepen their love for Him and strengthen their own commitment to follow Him.

### Luke 9:62 — No Looking Back From the Plow

verse: Luke 9:62

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes a short series of encounters between Jesus and people who wanted to follow Him, found at the beginning of His journey to Jerusalem. One man offered to follow Jesus wherever He went, and Jesus warned that He had no place to lay His head. Another was called by Jesus but asked to bury his father first, and Jesus told him to proclaim the kingdom instead.

A third man said, [[v61|I want to follow you, Lord, but first allow me to say good-bye to those who are at my house]]. This request seems reasonable. It recalls the prophet Elisha, who, when called by Elijah, asked permission to kiss his father and mother goodbye. Elijah allowed it, and Elisha then slaughtered his oxen, burned his plowing equipment, and followed Elijah (1 Kings 19:19–21).

Jesus responded with an image from farming: [[v62|No one, having put his hand to the plow and looking back, is fit for God’s Kingdom]]. In the ancient world, plows were simple tools pulled by oxen. The farmer guided the plow with one hand, keeping his eyes fixed on a point ahead to plow a straight furrow. If he looked back over his shoulder, the furrow would become crooked, and the work would be ruined.

Jesus was not condemning saying goodbye to one's family. He was addressing the heart attitude behind the request. The phrase "first allow me" suggests divided loyalty. The man wanted to follow Jesus, but on his own terms and timetable. Jesus warned that following Him requires wholehearted commitment and a forward focus, not constant looking back to what was left behind.

"Fit" means useful or suitable. A plowman who keeps looking back is not suited for the job. Similarly, a disciple whose heart is divided between following Jesus and clinging to the past is not ready for the work of God's kingdom.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that discipleship requires undivided commitment. Jesus does not want halfhearted followers who keep one eye on Him and the other on what they have left behind. He calls for wholehearted devotion. This echoes the first commandment, to love God with all one's heart.

It also warns against the danger of looking back. In Genesis 19:26, Lot's wife looked back at Sodom and became a pillar of salt. Jesus later warns, [[Luke 17:32|Remember Lot’s wife!]] Looking back with longing at the old life can draw people away from following Jesus.

Finally, the verse shows that Jesus' call is urgent. The kingdom of God is breaking into the world, and Jesus is on His way to Jerusalem. There is work to be done. Disciples must be focused on what lies ahead.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine whether their commitment to Jesus is wholehearted. Are there areas of life they are holding back, or past ways of living they keep looking back on with longing? Believers can ask God to help them follow Jesus with undivided hearts.

It also encourages believers to keep their eyes forward. Regret, nostalgia, or longing for former sins can distract believers from God's present calling. Like a farmer plowing a straight furrow, believers can fix their eyes on Jesus and the work He has given them. Paul writes about [[Philippians 3:13|forgetting the things which are behind, and stretching forward to the things which are before]].

Finally, this verse does not mean believers should neglect their families or relationships. Elsewhere Jesus affirms honoring parents and caring for family. The point is about priority and focus: Jesus must come first, and following Him should not be postponed or divided.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 9 focuses on two great questions: Who is Jesus, and what does it mean to follow Him? Herod asked, "Who is this?" Peter answered, "The Christ of God," and the Father declared, "This is my beloved Son. Listen to him!" Believers can affirm this confession personally and listen to Jesus above every other voice.

The chapter shows that Jesus' identity is inseparable from His mission of suffering. Even in His glory on the mountain, the conversation was about His "exodus" in Jerusalem. His followers are called to take up their cross, deny themselves, and follow Him. Believers can embrace the path of self-giving love, trusting that it leads to life.

Luke's emphasis on prayer reminds believers that Jesus prayed before Peter's confession and at the transfiguration. Prayer was the setting for revelation. Believers can make prayer central to their lives, especially at important moments.

The disciples' failures, their inability to heal the boy, their argument about greatness, their exclusiveness, and their desire to call down fire, show how slowly even close followers learn Jesus' ways. Believers can examine themselves for pride, rivalry, and a desire for revenge, and learn from Jesus' rebukes. He came not to destroy but to save.

Finally, Jesus set His face toward Jerusalem with determination, and He calls His followers to the same wholehearted commitment. Following Him means giving up security, putting His kingdom first, and keeping one's hand on the plow without looking back. Believers can follow Jesus resolutely, with undivided hearts, on the road He walked.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, I confess with Peter that You are the Christ of God. Thank You for revealing Your glory on the mountain and for the Father's command to listen to You. Thank You for the exodus You accomplished in Jerusalem, setting me free from slavery to sin and death. Help me to deny myself, take up my cross, and follow You.

Forgive me for the pride that seeks to be greatest, the exclusiveness that resents others serving You, and the anger that wants to call down fire on those who reject You. Teach me that You came not to destroy but to save. Thank You for setting Your face toward Jerusalem for my sake. Give me the same determination to follow You wholeheartedly. Keep my hand on the plow and my eyes fixed on You, without looking back. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
