# Philippians 2
theme: The Mind of Christ
main: Paul appeals to the Philippians to complete his joy by being united in love and purpose, doing nothing from selfish ambition but in humility counting others more significant than themselves. He holds up the supreme example of Christ Jesus, who, though He existed in the form of God, emptied Himself, took the form of a servant, and humbled Himself to death on a cross, and whom God therefore highly exalted, so that every knee will bow and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord. Paul calls them to work out their salvation, since God is at work in them, and to shine as lights in a dark world. He then commends Timothy and Epaphroditus as living examples of the selfless, Christlike care he has described.

## Chapter Background and Story

Philippians 2 follows directly from Paul's call at the end of chapter 1 to stand firm [[Philippians 1:27|in one spirit, with one soul]]. The church at Philippi was facing opposition from outside, but there were also signs of tension within. Later in the letter, Paul urges two women, Euodia and Syntyche, to agree in the Lord (Philippians 4:2). Rivalry, pride, and self-interest were threatening the church's unity.

Paul's answer is not merely to tell them to get along. He points them to Christ. Verses 6–11 form one of the most beautiful and profound passages about Jesus in the entire Bible. Many scholars believe it may be an early Christian hymn, perhaps sung in worship, which Paul quotes or composed himself. It traces Christ's journey from the glory of God, down to the humiliation of the cross, and back up to exaltation as Lord of all.

Paul then gives practical instructions and introduces two coworkers, Timothy and Epaphroditus, whose lives embody the humility and self-giving love of Christ.

### A Call to Unity and Humility (2:1–4)

[[v1|If therefore there is any exhortation in Christ, if any consolation of love, if any fellowship of the Spirit, if any tender mercies and compassion]]. The "if" here means "since." Paul lists blessings the Philippians have already experienced: encouragement in Christ, comfort from His love, fellowship in the Spirit, and compassion. Because they have received these, they should share them with one another.

[[v2|make my joy full by being like-minded, having the same love, being of one accord, of one mind]]. Paul piles up phrases for unity. He is not asking them to agree on every opinion but to share the same love and purpose. The humility that makes unity possible is studied below.

### The Example of Christ (2:5–11)

[[v5|Have this in your mind, which was also in Christ Jesus]]. Believers are to share the attitude, the mindset, that Christ had. The description of Christ's humility and exaltation is studied below.

### Working Out Salvation (2:12–13)

This call to obedience, grounded in God's work within, is studied below.

### Shining as Lights (2:14–18)

[[v14|Do all things without complaining and arguing]], [[v15|that you may become blameless and harmless, children of God without defect in the middle of a crooked and perverse generation, among whom you are seen as lights in the world]]. Complaining and arguing were exactly what Israel did in the wilderness (Exodus 16:7–8; Numbers 14:27). Paul echoes Deuteronomy 32:5, where Moses described [[Deuteronomy 32:5|a perverse and crooked generation]]. Believers are called to be different: to shine like stars in a dark night sky.

[[v16|holding up the word of life, that I may have something to boast in the day of Christ that I didn’t run in vain nor labor in vain]]. "Holding up" can mean holding fast to the word of life or holding it out to others. Both fit. Believers cling to the gospel and offer it to the world.

[[v17|Yes, and if I am poured out on the sacrifice and service of your faith, I am glad and rejoice with you all]]. Paul uses the image of a drink offering, wine poured out on a sacrifice in Old Testament worship (Numbers 28:7). He sees his possible death as an offering poured out alongside the Philippians' own sacrifice of faith and service. Even facing death, he rejoices. [[v18|In the same way, you also should be glad and rejoice with me]].

### Timothy and Epaphroditus (2:19–30)

[[v19|But I hope in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy to you soon, that I also may be cheered up when I know how you are doing]]. Timothy had been with Paul when the church at Philippi was founded (Acts 16:1–3, 12). [[v20|For I have no one else like-minded, who will truly care about you]]. [[v21|For they all seek their own, not the things of Jesus Christ]]. Timothy was a rare example of selfless concern for others. [[v22|But you know that he has proved himself. As a child serves a father, so he served with me in furtherance of the Good News]]. Timothy's character had been tested and proven over many years.

[[v25|But I thought it necessary to send to you Epaphroditus, my brother, fellow worker, fellow soldier, and your apostle and servant of my need]]. Epaphroditus was the messenger the Philippians had sent with their gift to Paul. "Apostle" here means messenger or delegate. Paul gives him five titles of honor.

[[v26|since he longed for you all, and was very troubled because you had heard that he was sick]]. [[v27|For indeed he was sick nearly to death, but God had mercy on him, and not on him only, but on me also, that I might not have sorrow on sorrow]]. Epaphroditus nearly died while serving Paul. Notice that Paul, who had seen God work miracles of healing (Acts 19:11–12), did not heal Epaphroditus at will. His recovery was God's mercy. And Paul does not hide his own grief; he would have experienced "sorrow on sorrow." Grief is not a sign of weak faith.

[[v29|Receive him therefore in the Lord with all joy, and hold such people in honor]], [[v30|because for the work of Christ he came near to death, risking his life to supply that which was lacking in your service toward me]]. The word for "risking" was used for gamblers who staked everything. In later centuries, some Christians who cared for the sick during plagues were called "the gamblers," after this verse. Epaphroditus is another example of the mind of Christ: putting others first at great cost.

## Important Verses Explained

### Philippians 2:3–4 — Counting Others Better Than Yourself

verse: Philippians 2:3-4

#### Simple Explanation

Paul now names the attitudes that destroy unity and the attitudes that build it.

"Doing nothing through rivalry or through conceit." "Rivalry" means selfish ambition, the desire to promote oneself at others' expense. "Conceit" means empty pride, a hollow sense of self-importance. Both put self at the center.

"But in humility, each counting others better than himself." Humility here means a lowly view of oneself in relation to others, a willingness to put others first. "Counting others better" does not mean believing that everyone else is more talented or more valuable. It means treating others as more important, giving their needs and interests priority.

"Each of you not just looking to his own things, but each of you also to the things of others." Notice the word "also." Paul does not say believers should neglect their own needs entirely. He says they should not look only to their own interests but also pay attention to the interests of others.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

In the Greek and Roman world, humility was generally not considered a virtue. It was the attitude expected of slaves and the lower classes. Honor, status, and self-promotion were highly valued. Christianity transformed humility into a virtue because of the example of Christ, who though He was God, humbled Himself.

These verses describe the opposite of what comes naturally to sinful human beings. People are inclined to put themselves first, to compete, to compare, and to seek recognition. The gospel reverses this. Jesus taught that the greatest must be the servant of all (Mark 10:43–45), and He washed His disciples' feet (John 13:1–15).

Humility is not self-hatred or thinking poorly of oneself. It is freedom from self-preoccupation. A humble person is secure enough in God's love that they do not need to promote themselves, and so they are free to care about others. Humility also does not mean allowing oneself to be abused or exploited. Christ's humility was a free choice of love, not a submission to cruelty. Caring for others' interests includes caring for one's own safety and the safety of others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses can transform relationships at home, at work, and in church. In conversations, believers can listen more than they speak. In decisions, they can consider how choices will affect others. In conflicts, they can seek to understand the other person's perspective. In service, they can do tasks no one notices.

Selfish ambition can show up in subtle ways: needing credit, competing for attention, feeling envious of others' success, or quietly tearing others down. Humility looks for ways to celebrate others, support them, and help them succeed.

A practical question for each day is: "Whose interests can I put ahead of my own today?" Small acts of humble service build unity and reflect the mind of Christ.

### Philippians 2:6–8 — He Emptied Himself

verse: Philippians 2:6-8

#### Simple Explanation

Paul describes the greatest example of humility in history: Jesus Christ.

"Who, existing in the form of God." Before He became human, Christ existed as God. "Form" here refers to the true nature and essence of something. Christ was fully God.

"Didn’t consider equality with God a thing to be grasped." Christ did not cling to His divine privileges or use them for His own advantage. He did not regard His equality with God as something to hold on to for Himself.

"But emptied himself." This phrase has been much discussed. Most Christian interpreters understand it to mean that Christ did not give up His divine nature, but that He laid aside His heavenly glory and the full use of His divine privileges by becoming human. He "emptied himself" by what He took on, not by what He lost.

"Taking the form of a servant, being made in the likeness of men." The one who existed in the form of God took on the form of a slave. He became truly human.

"And being found in human form, he humbled himself, becoming obedient to the point of death, yes, the death of the cross." Christ's humility went even further. As a human, He obeyed the Father all the way to death, and not just any death, but crucifixion, the most shameful and painful form of execution, reserved for slaves and criminals.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage describes a downward journey of stunning humility: from equality with God, to the form of a servant, to human likeness, to obedience, to death, to death on a cross. Every step went lower. The Creator became a creature. The Lord became a servant. The Holy One died the death of a criminal.

This is the central mystery of the Christian faith: the incarnation. The eternal Son of God became fully human without ceasing to be fully God. Early church councils, such as the Council of Chalcedon in A.D. 451, worked carefully to express this truth: Christ is one person with two natures, fully divine and fully human. Passages like this one were foundational to those confessions.

The reason Paul includes this passage is practical. He wants the Philippians to have "this mind" (v5). If Christ, who had every right to be served, chose to serve, then His followers should do the same. Christ's humility is the pattern for Christian relationships.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

When believers struggle with pride, a desire for recognition, or an unwillingness to serve, this passage offers both a challenge and a comfort. The challenge is to follow Christ's example of laying aside privileges for the sake of others. The comfort is that Christ understands every human weakness and suffering, because He entered fully into human life, even to death.

This passage can also shape how believers think about rights and privileges. Christ had every right to stay in glory, but He gave it up for others. Believers may be called at times to set aside their preferences, comforts, or rights in order to serve others well. This is not because their needs do not matter, but because love sometimes chooses the lower place.

Finally, this passage leads to worship. Reflecting on the depth of Christ's humility can deepen gratitude and love for Him.

### Philippians 2:9–11 — Every Knee Will Bow

verse: Philippians 2:9-11

#### Simple Explanation

Christ's downward journey is followed by an upward one. "Therefore God also highly exalted him." Because Christ humbled Himself, God lifted Him to the highest place. The resurrection and ascension were God's vindication of His obedient Son.

"And gave to him the name which is above every name." In the ancient world, a name represented a person's character and authority. The name given to Jesus is the highest name of all. Verse 11 reveals what it is: "Lord." In the Greek Old Testament, "Lord" was the word used to translate God's personal name, Yahweh, often written as "the LORD."

"That at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of those in heaven, those on earth, and those under the earth." Every being in the universe, angels, humans, and even the dead or the spiritual powers below, will one day bow before Jesus.

"And that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father." All creation will acknowledge His lordship. And this brings glory not only to Jesus but to the Father.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul is echoing Isaiah 45:23, where God declares, [[Isaiah 45:23|to me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall take an oath]]. In Isaiah, these words refer to the LORD, the one true God, who says He will not share His glory with another. Paul applies them to Jesus. This is a powerful statement of Christ's divinity. The honor that belongs to God alone is given to Jesus, and it brings glory to the Father.

The confession "Jesus Christ is Lord" was the earliest Christian creed (Romans 10:9; 1 Corinthians 12:3). In the Roman world, people were expected to declare "Caesar is Lord." Christians who confessed Jesus as Lord were making a bold claim: He, not Caesar, is the true ruler of all.

Christians have discussed what it means that "every knee will bow." Most understand it to mean that all will eventually acknowledge Christ's lordship, some joyfully in worship, others unwillingly at the final judgment. Some hope that it points toward a wider reconciliation of all things. All agree that Christ's lordship is universal and that the wise response is to bow before Him willingly now.

This passage also reveals a principle of God's kingdom: the way up is down. Jesus taught that those who humble themselves will be exalted (Luke 14:11). Christ's own story is the supreme example.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage invites believers to confess Jesus as Lord now, willingly and joyfully. That confession is not merely words; it means submitting every area of life to His authority.

It also brings hope. In a world where injustice, evil, and suffering seem to prevail, this passage declares that Jesus will be acknowledged as Lord by all. The final word belongs to Him.

Finally, it encourages those who choose humility. Choosing the lower place, serving without recognition, and setting aside one's rights can feel like loss. But God honors humility, as He honored His Son. Believers can trust God to lift them up in His time.

### Philippians 2:12–13 — Work Out Your Salvation

verse: Philippians 2:12-13

#### Simple Explanation

"So then, my beloved." Paul draws a practical conclusion from Christ's example. "Even as you have always obeyed, not only in my presence, but now much more in my absence." The Philippians had a history of faithfulness. Paul encourages them to continue, whether or not he is present.

"Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling." This does not mean "work for your salvation," as if they had to earn it. Paul has made clear that salvation is a gift (Philippians 3:9). To "work out" means to bring to completion, to live out in practice what God has already given. The phrase "your own" is plural in Greek, so Paul may especially have in mind the church's shared life: working out what salvation means for their community, including their unity. "With fear and trembling" means with reverence, seriousness, and humble dependence on God.

Verse 13 explains why they can do this: "For it is God who works in you both to will and to work for his good pleasure." God is at work within believers. He shapes their desires ("to will") and empowers their actions ("to work"). Their effort is possible because of His prior work.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses hold together two truths that might seem to be in tension: human effort and divine grace. Believers are called to work, to obey, to grow, and to put their salvation into practice. Yet the power for all of this comes from God. It is not that God does part and believers do part. God works in them, and therefore they work.

This balance guards against two errors. One is passivity, the idea that because salvation is a gift, believers need not make any effort to grow. The other is self-reliance, the idea that spiritual growth depends entirely on human willpower. Paul rejects both. Believers work hard, but they do so in dependence on God's power, knowing that every good desire and action is His gift.

Christian traditions have expressed this relationship in different ways, but all affirm that sanctification, growth in holiness, involves both God's grace and human response. The motivation is not fear of losing God's favor but reverent gratitude for His work.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can approach spiritual growth with both seriousness and confidence. Seriousness, because growth requires real effort: choosing obedience, building habits of prayer and Scripture, resisting temptation, and seeking reconciliation. Confidence, because God Himself is at work in them, giving them both the desire and the ability to do His will.

When believers feel weak or unmotivated, they can ask God to work in them "to will," to give them the desire to obey. When they feel unable, they can ask God to work in them "to work," to give them the strength. Growth is a partnership in which God always takes the lead.

For churches, this verse invites working out together what salvation means in community: how to love one another, resolve conflicts, and live in unity under Christ.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Philippians 2 calls believers to have the mind of Christ. In a world that celebrates self-promotion, competition, and status, Paul urges humility: doing nothing out of selfish ambition or empty pride, treating others as more important, and looking out for their interests as well as one's own.

The reason for this humility is Christ Himself. Though He existed in the form of God, He did not cling to His privileges. He emptied Himself, took the form of a servant, became human, and humbled Himself to death on a cross. God therefore exalted Him and gave Him the name above every name, so that every knee will bow and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord. The way of Christ is the way of humble self-giving, and it is the way God honors.

This chapter also shows how believers grow. They work out their salvation with reverence, not to earn God's favor, but because God is already at work in them, giving them the desire and the power to do His will. As they do, they shine like lights in a dark world, holding out the word of life, without complaining or arguing.

Finally, Paul offers living examples. Timothy genuinely cared for others' welfare rather than his own interests. Epaphroditus risked his life to serve. These ordinary believers showed what the mind of Christ looks like in everyday life. Believers today can follow their example, serving others with humble, self-giving love that reflects the Lord who gave everything for them.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You existed in the form of God, yet You did not cling to Your glory. You emptied Yourself, took the form of a servant, and humbled Yourself to death on a cross. I bow before You and confess that You are Lord, to the glory of God the Father. Give me Your mind. Free me from selfish ambition and empty pride. Teach me to count others as more important and to care for their interests as well as my own. Father, thank You that You are at work in me, giving me both the desire and the strength to do Your will. Help me to work out my salvation with reverence, without complaining or arguing, shining as a light in this world and holding out the word of life. Make me like Timothy and Epaphroditus, caring deeply and serving faithfully for the sake of Christ. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
