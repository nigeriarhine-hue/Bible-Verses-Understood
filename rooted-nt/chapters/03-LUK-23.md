# Luke 23
theme: The Innocent King Who Forgave, Saved, and Entrusted Himself to the Father
main: Pilate and Herod both find Jesus innocent, yet Pilate gives in to the crowd and hands Him over to be crucified. On the way to the cross Jesus grieves for the women of Jerusalem, and on the cross He prays for His executioners' forgiveness, promises paradise to a repentant criminal, and dies entrusting His spirit to the Father. A centurion declares Him righteous, and Joseph of Arimathea buries Him while faithful women watch.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 23 records Jesus' trials before Pilate and Herod, His crucifixion, death, and burial. Luke's account parallels Matthew 27 and Mark 15 (studied at Matthew 27:46, 27:51, 27:54 and Mark 15:15, 15:31, 15:43), but it has several distinctive emphases. Luke repeatedly stresses Jesus' innocence: Pilate declares Him innocent three times, Herod finds no guilt, a criminal says He has done nothing wrong, and the centurion calls Him righteous. Luke also records three sayings of Jesus from the cross found in no other Gospel, each revealing His heart: forgiveness for His enemies, salvation for a dying criminal, and trust in His Father.

### Before Pilate (23:1–7)

After the council condemned Jesus at daybreak (Luke 22:66–71), the whole assembly brought Him to Pilate, the Roman governor. They accused Him of three things: [[v2|perverting the nation, forbidding paying taxes to Caesar, and saying that he himself is Christ, a king]]. These charges were framed to sound like rebellion against Rome. The second was false; Jesus had said the opposite (Luke 20:25). The third twisted His claim to be the Messiah into a political threat.

Pilate asked, [[v3|Are you the King of the Jews?]] Jesus answered, [[v3|So you say]]. Pilate then said to the chief priests and the crowd, [[v4|I find no basis for a charge against this man]]. This was the first of Pilate's three declarations of Jesus' innocence. The accusers insisted that Jesus was stirring up the people from Galilee to Jerusalem. When Pilate heard "Galilee," he learned that Jesus was from Herod Antipas's territory, and since Herod was in Jerusalem for the Passover, Pilate sent Jesus to him.

### Before Herod (23:8–12)

Luke alone records this episode. Herod Antipas, who had beheaded John the Baptist, was very glad to see Jesus. He had wanted to see Him for a long time and [[v8|hoped to see some miracle done by him]]. Herod treated Jesus like an entertainer. He questioned Him at length, but Jesus gave no answer at all. The chief priests and scribes stood there accusing Him vehemently. Herod and his soldiers then treated Jesus with contempt and mocked Him, dressed Him in an elegant robe, and sent Him back to Pilate.

Luke notes, [[v12|Herod and Pilate became friends with each other that very day, for before that they were enemies with each other]]. Two rulers who had been rivals found common ground in their dismissal of Jesus. In Acts 4:27, the early church prays about how Herod and Pilate, with the Gentiles and the people of Israel, gathered against Jesus, fulfilling Psalm 2.

### Pilate Gives In (23:13–25)

Pilate called together the chief priests, rulers, and people and told them that neither he nor Herod had found Jesus guilty of the charges. [[v15|nothing worthy of death has been done by him]]. He proposed to have Jesus punished, probably by flogging, and then release Him. (Verse 17, about releasing a prisoner at the feast, is missing from many early manuscripts.)

But the crowd shouted together, [[v18|Away with this man! Release to us Barabbas!]] Barabbas had been imprisoned for an uprising in the city and for murder. Pilate, wanting to release Jesus, appealed to them again, but they kept shouting, [[v21|Crucify! Crucify him!]] A third time Pilate asked, [[v22|Why? What evil has this man done? I have found no capital crime in him]]. But they insisted with loud shouts, and [[v23|Their voices and the voices of the chief priests prevailed]].

Pilate granted their demand. He released the man guilty of rebellion and murder, [[v25|but he delivered Jesus up to their will]]. Luke's account highlights the injustice: the innocent man was condemned and the guilty man freed. (Pilate's decision is studied at Mark 15:15.)

### The Way to the Cross (23:26–31)

As they led Jesus away, they seized Simon of Cyrene, who was coming in from the country, and made him carry the cross behind Jesus. Luke's wording, "after Jesus," presents Simon as a picture of discipleship: taking up the cross and following Jesus (Luke 9:23).

A large crowd followed, including women who were mourning and wailing for Him. Jesus turned to them and said, [[v28|Daughters of Jerusalem, don’t weep for me, but weep for yourselves and for your children]]. Even on His way to death, Jesus' concern was for others. He foresaw the coming destruction of Jerusalem (Luke 19:41–44; 21:20–24), when the suffering would be so terrible that people would call childless women blessed and would beg the mountains to fall on them (echoing Hosea 10:8). Then He said, [[v31|For if they do these things in the green tree, what will be done in the dry?]] This proverb means that if such things happen to an innocent man (green wood, which does not burn easily), how much worse will judgment be for the guilty (dry wood, which burns readily)?

### The Crucifixion (23:32–43)

Two criminals were led out to be executed with Jesus. At the place called "The Skull," they crucified Him with the criminals on His right and left, fulfilling Isaiah 53:12, which Jesus had quoted the night before (Luke 22:37).

Then Jesus said, [[v34|Father, forgive them, for they don’t know what they are doing]]. This prayer is studied below. (Some early manuscripts lack this verse, but it is widely attested and fits Luke's portrait of Jesus perfectly.) The soldiers divided His clothes by casting lots, fulfilling Psalm 22:18.

The people stood watching. The rulers sneered, [[v35|He saved others. Let him save himself, if this is the Christ of God, his chosen one!]] The soldiers mocked Him too, offering Him sour wine and saying, [[v37|If you are the King of the Jews, save yourself!]] Above Him was an inscription: [[v38|THIS IS THE KING OF THE JEWS]].

One of the criminals hanging beside Him also insulted Him: [[v39|If you are the Christ, save yourself and us!]] But the other criminal rebuked him. Their exchange and Jesus' response are studied below.

### The Death of Jesus (23:44–49)

It was about noon, and darkness came over the whole land until three in the afternoon, [[v45|The sun was darkened]]. The curtain of the temple was torn in two. (The torn curtain is studied at Matthew 27:51.) Then Jesus cried out with a loud voice, [[v46|Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!]] and breathed His last. This saying is studied below.

The centurion who saw what happened [[v47|glorified God, saying, “Certainly this was a righteous man.”]] Where Matthew and Mark record him saying "Truly this was the Son of God," Luke emphasizes the word "righteous" or "innocent," which fits his theme of Jesus' innocence. The Gentile officer recognized the truth that Pilate had declared and that the leaders had rejected.

The crowds who had gathered to watch went home [[v48|beating their chests]], a sign of grief and perhaps of repentance. All those who knew Jesus, including the women who had followed Him from Galilee, stood at a distance watching.

### The Burial (23:50–56)

A man named Joseph, a member of the council, [[v50|a good and righteous man]] from the town of Arimathea, [[v51|he had not consented to their counsel and deed]]. Luke makes clear that not every council member agreed with condemning Jesus. Joseph was waiting for God's kingdom. He went to Pilate and asked for Jesus' body, took it down, wrapped it in linen, and placed it in a tomb cut in the rock, where no one had yet been laid.

It was the day of Preparation, and the Sabbath was about to begin at sunset. The women who had come with Jesus from Galilee followed and saw the tomb and how His body was laid. They went home and prepared spices and perfumes, and then [[v56|On the Sabbath they rested according to the commandment]]. Their faithful presence at the cross and the tomb would make them the first witnesses of the resurrection.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 23:34 — [[Luke 23:34|Father, Forgive Them]]

verse: Luke 23:34

#### Simple Explanation

These are the first words Jesus spoke from the cross in Luke's Gospel. He had been betrayed by Judas, denied by Peter, falsely accused before the council, mocked by Herod's soldiers, condemned by Pilate despite being declared innocent, and handed over to be crucified. Roman soldiers had nailed Him to a cross between two criminals. Crucifixion was one of the most painful and humiliating forms of execution ever devised.

In this moment of agony, Jesus prayed: [[v34|Father, forgive them, for they don’t know what they are doing]].

[[v34|Father]]. Even on the cross, Jesus addressed God with intimacy and trust. Throughout Luke, Jesus prayed to His Father at every crucial moment.

[[v34|forgive them]]. "Them" likely includes the Roman soldiers who were crucifying Him, and possibly all who were responsible for His death: the religious leaders, Pilate, and the crowd. Jesus did not call down judgment on them. He asked the Father to forgive them.

[[v34|for they don’t know what they are doing]]. The soldiers were carrying out orders without understanding that they were crucifying the Son of God. The leaders and crowds, too, did not fully understand who Jesus was. Peter later said to the people of Jerusalem, [[Acts 3:17|I know that you did this in ignorance, as did also your rulers]]. Their ignorance did not make them innocent, since they still needed forgiveness, but it was a reason Jesus appealed for mercy.

Immediately after this prayer, Luke notes that the soldiers divided His clothes by casting lots. Even as Jesus prayed for them, they continued their cruel work.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This prayer reveals the heart of Jesus and the heart of God. Jesus had taught His followers to love their enemies, bless those who curse them, and pray for those who mistreat them (Luke 6:27–28). On the cross, He did exactly that. His teaching was not just words; He lived it in the most extreme circumstances.

It also reveals the purpose of the cross. Jesus died so that sinners could be forgiven. His prayer for His executioners points to the forgiveness He was making possible for all people through His death. Even those who crucified Him could be forgiven. In Acts 2, many of the people of Jerusalem who had called for His crucifixion repented and were forgiven.

Finally, this prayer became a model for Jesus' followers. Stephen, the first Christian martyr, prayed as he was being stoned, [[Acts 7:60|Lord, don’t hold this sin against them!]] Throughout history, many believers facing persecution have prayed for their persecutors, following Jesus' example.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to forgive those who hurt them. If Jesus could forgive those who were crucifying Him, believers can ask for His grace to forgive those who have wronged them. Forgiveness may be difficult and may take time, but Jesus shows that it is possible.

It also encourages believers to pray for those who hurt them. Praying for enemies softens the heart and reflects God's love. It does not mean pretending wrongs did not happen or remaining in harmful situations; believers can pursue safety and justice while still refusing hatred and praying for the good of those who harmed them.

Finally, this verse gives assurance to anyone who wonders whether they can be forgiven. If Jesus prayed for the forgiveness of those crucifying Him, no one is beyond the reach of His mercy. Believers can come to Him confident that He desires to forgive.

### Luke 23:42–43 — "Today You Will Be With Me in Paradise"

verse: Luke 23:42-43

#### Simple Explanation

These verses record a conversation between Jesus and one of the two criminals crucified beside Him. Both men had been condemned to death, likely for violent crimes such as robbery or rebellion. One of them joined the crowd in mocking Jesus: [[v39|If you are the Christ, save yourself and us!]]

But the other criminal rebuked him: [[v40|Don’t you even fear God, seeing you are under the same condemnation?]] He continued, [[v41|And we indeed justly, for we receive the due reward for our deeds, but this man has done nothing wrong]]. This criminal recognized three things: he feared God; he admitted that he deserved his punishment; and he declared that Jesus was innocent.

Then he turned to Jesus and said, [[v42|Lord, remember me when you come into your Kingdom]]. Some manuscripts read simply "Jesus, remember me." This is remarkable faith. Jesus was dying on a cross, mocked and apparently powerless. Yet this man believed that Jesus was a king who would come into His kingdom. He asked only to be remembered, a humble request.

Jesus answered, [[v43|Assuredly I tell you, today you will be with me in Paradise]]. "Assuredly" (or "Truly") emphasizes certainty. "Today" means that very day, not at some distant future time. "With me" promises personal fellowship with Jesus. "Paradise" is a Persian word for a garden, used in Jewish writings for the place of blessedness where the righteous dead are with God, recalling the garden of Eden. The man asked to be remembered in the future; Jesus promised him far more, immediately.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal that salvation is by grace through faith, not by works. This man had no time to do good deeds, be baptized, or make restitution. He had lived a life deserving death. Yet he turned to Jesus in faith and repentance, and Jesus promised him paradise. Paul writes that salvation is [[Ephesians 2:8|not of yourselves; it is the gift of God]].

They also show what genuine repentance looks like. The man feared God, admitted his guilt without excuse, recognized Jesus' innocence, and trusted Jesus as King. These are the marks of true faith.

Finally, these verses provide comfort about death. Jesus promised the criminal that he would be with Him that very day. Many Christians understand this as teaching that believers who die go immediately into the presence of Christ, as Paul says, [[Philippians 1:23|having the desire to depart and be with Christ]]. Christians have different views about the details of life after death before the final resurrection, but all affirm that believers are safe with Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses offer hope to anyone who thinks it is too late to turn to God. The criminal turned to Jesus in his final hours and was welcomed. No matter what someone has done or how much time has passed, they can come to Jesus in faith.

They also invite believers to approach Jesus with the same honest repentance: admitting their sin, recognizing His righteousness, and asking for His mercy. Salvation does not depend on a record of good works but on trusting Jesus.

Finally, these verses comfort those facing death or grieving loved ones who trusted in Christ. Jesus' promise, "Today you will be with me," assures believers that death does not separate them from Him.

### Luke 23:46 — [[Luke 23:46|Father, Into Your Hands I Commit My Spirit]]

verse: Luke 23:46

#### Simple Explanation

These are Jesus' final words before His death in Luke's Gospel. He had been on the cross for hours. From noon until three in the afternoon, darkness covered the land, and the curtain in the temple was torn in two. Then [[v46|Jesus, crying with a loud voice, said, “Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!”]]

[[v46|crying with a loud voice]]. Crucified victims usually grew weaker until they could barely speak. Jesus' loud cry suggests that He was not simply overcome by death but actively gave up His life.

[[v46|Father]]. As in His first words on the cross (v. 34), Jesus addressed God as Father. His relationship with the Father remained the center of His life even in death.

[[v46|into your hands I commit my spirit]]. These words come from Psalm 31:5, a psalm of trust in God amid suffering and danger. Jewish people sometimes prayed this psalm as an evening prayer before sleep. Jesus added "Father" to the psalm's words. "Commit" means to entrust or hand over something valuable to someone's safekeeping. Jesus entrusted His life to His Father, confident that the Father would receive Him and raise Him.

[[v46|Having said this, he breathed his last]]. Jesus died after entrusting Himself to God.

Luke's account of Jesus' death emphasizes peace and trust. Matthew and Mark record Jesus' cry, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (studied at Matthew 27:46). Luke records this final word of trust. Both are true: Jesus experienced the depths of forsakenness as He bore sin, and He died entrusting Himself fully to His Father.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals Jesus' perfect trust in His Father, even in death. Throughout His life, He had lived in dependence on the Father, praying at every crucial moment. In death, He entrusted Himself completely to God. Peter later wrote that Jesus, when He suffered, [[1 Peter 2:23|committed himself to him who judges righteously]].

It also shows that Jesus' death was voluntary. He gave up His spirit; it was not simply taken from Him. In John 10:18, Jesus says that no one takes His life from Him; He lays it down of His own accord.

Finally, this verse gives believers a model for facing death. Stephen, as he was being stoned, prayed, [[Acts 7:59|Lord Jesus, receive my spirit!]] Throughout Christian history, many believers have prayed these words of Psalm 31 as they died. Because Jesus entrusted His spirit to the Father and was raised, believers can entrust their spirits to God with confidence.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to entrust their lives to God every day. "Into your hands I commit my spirit" can be a prayer at the beginning or end of each day, expressing trust in God's care. It is fitting for times of uncertainty, fear, illness, or danger.

It also comforts believers facing death, whether their own or that of loved ones. Jesus has gone before them through death and has shown that the Father's hands are safe. Believers can face death not with terror but with trust.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to trust God when they cannot see the outcome. Jesus entrusted Himself to the Father before the resurrection, in the darkness of the cross. Believers can trust God in their own dark moments, believing that He is faithful and will bring life out of death.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 23 presents Jesus as the innocent King who suffered unjustly. Pilate, Herod, the criminal, and the centurion all recognized His innocence, yet He was condemned to satisfy a crowd. Believers can respond with gratitude, recognizing that the innocent one died in the place of the guilty, just as Barabbas was released while Jesus was crucified.

The chapter also warns against the failures of those who opposed Jesus. Pilate gave in to pressure despite knowing the truth. Herod treated Jesus as entertainment. The crowd was swayed by its leaders. Believers can ask God for courage to stand for truth, to take Jesus seriously, and to resist going along with the crowd.

Jesus' words from the cross teach believers how to live and die. His prayer, "Father, forgive them," calls believers to forgive those who hurt them and to pray for their enemies. His promise to the criminal, "Today you will be with me in Paradise," gives hope to anyone who turns to Him in faith, no matter how late. And His final prayer, "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit," teaches believers to entrust their lives and their deaths to God.

Finally, the examples of Simon, the mourning women, Joseph of Arimathea, and the women from Galilee show different forms of faithfulness. Believers can carry the cross after Jesus, grieve over sin, act courageously for Him even when others oppose Him, and remain present in faithful love. These quiet acts of faithfulness prepare the way for the joy of the resurrection.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You were innocent, yet You were condemned in my place. Thank You for bearing the cross for me. Forgive me for the times I have gone along with the crowd or failed to take You seriously. Give me courage to stand for what is true and right.

Thank You for praying, "Father, forgive them," even as You were being crucified. Help me to forgive those who have hurt me and to pray for their good. Like the criminal beside You, I confess that I deserve judgment, and I ask You to remember me in Your kingdom. Thank You for Your promise of paradise to all who trust in You. Teach me to entrust my life to the Father each day, and when my time comes, help me to say with You, "Father, into Your hands I commit my spirit." In Jesus’ name, Amen.
