# Matthew 17
theme: Glory on the Mountain, Struggle in the Valley
main: On a high mountain, three disciples see Jesus transfigured in glory, meet Moses and Elijah, and hear the Father command them to listen to His Son. Coming down, they face a suffering boy whom the other disciples could not help, and Jesus teaches that even small, genuine faith in God can accomplish what seems impossible. Jesus again predicts His death and resurrection and, in the matter of the temple tax, shows both His unique Sonship and His willingness to avoid needless offense.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 17 follows directly from chapter 16, where Peter confessed Jesus as the Christ, the Son of the living God, and Jesus revealed that He must suffer, die, and rise. He then told His disciples that following Him meant taking up the cross, and He promised that some of them would see the Son of Man coming in His kingdom (Matthew 16:28). Six days later, three of them saw a glimpse of that glory. The transfiguration confirmed Peter's confession and reassured the disciples that the path of suffering Jesus described would lead to glory.

### The Transfiguration (17:1–8)

Jesus took Peter, James, and John up [[v1|a high mountain by themselves]]. Matthew does not name the mountain. Tradition has identified it as Mount Tabor in lower Galilee, but many scholars think Mount Hermon, near Caesarea Philippi, is more likely, since that is where the previous events took place. These three disciples formed an inner circle; they would later be the ones Jesus took with Him into Gethsemane (Matthew 26:37).

There Jesus [[v2|was changed before them]]. The Greek word is "metamorphoo," from which we get "metamorphosis." His face shone like the sun, and His clothes became as white as light. This was not a reflected glow, as when Moses' face shone after speaking with God (Exodus 34:29–35), but a revelation of Jesus' own glory, usually hidden during His earthly life.

Moses and Elijah appeared, talking with Him. Moses represented the Law, through which God gave Israel His covenant; Elijah represented the Prophets, and he was expected to return before the day of the Lord. Both had met God on a mountain (Sinai, also called Horeb), and both had unusual departures from this life: Moses was buried by God Himself (Deuteronomy 34:5–6), and Elijah was taken up in a whirlwind (2 Kings 2:11). Luke tells us they spoke with Jesus about His "departure" (literally "exodus") that He was about to accomplish in Jerusalem (Luke 9:31). Their presence showed that the whole Old Testament pointed to Jesus and His coming death.

Peter, overwhelmed, offered to build three shelters, one each for Jesus, Moses, and Elijah. These may have been like the booths built during the Feast of Tabernacles, which celebrated God's presence with Israel in the wilderness. Peter may have wanted to prolong the moment, or he may have thought the kingdom had fully arrived. But his suggestion placed Jesus on the same level as Moses and Elijah.

While Peter was still speaking, a bright cloud overshadowed them, and a voice spoke from it: [[v5|This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Listen to him]]. The cloud recalls the cloud of God's presence that led Israel in the wilderness and filled the tabernacle and temple (Exodus 40:34–35; 1 Kings 8:10–11). The words repeat what the Father said at Jesus' baptism, with an added command.

The disciples fell on their faces in fear. Jesus came, touched them, and said, [[v7|Get up, and don’t be afraid]]. When they looked up, they saw only Jesus. Moses and Elijah had gone. The scene ended with Jesus alone, as the one to whom they must now listen.

### The Question About Elijah (17:9–13)

As they came down the mountain, Jesus told them not to tell anyone about the vision until after His resurrection. Before that, people would misunderstand it. The disciples asked about the teaching of the scribes that Elijah must come first, based on Malachi 4:5–6. If Jesus was the Messiah, where was Elijah?

Jesus agreed that Elijah comes first to restore all things, but He said Elijah had already come, and people did not recognize him; they did to him whatever they wanted. In the same way, the Son of Man would suffer at their hands. The disciples understood that He was speaking about John the Baptist, who had been executed by Herod (Matthew 14). John's fate foreshadowed Jesus' own.

### The Boy the Disciples Could Not Heal (17:14–21)

When they reached the crowd, a man knelt before Jesus and begged Him to have mercy on his son. The boy was [[v15|epileptic]] (literally "moonstruck," a term people then used for seizures, which they associated with the moon) and suffered terribly, often falling into fire or water. Matthew tells us that a demon was the cause in this case (v. 18). This should not be taken to mean that epilepsy in general is caused by demons. The Gospels distinguish between ordinary illnesses and demonic oppression (Matthew 4:24 lists them separately), and in this particular case the boy's seizures were connected with a demon.

The father had brought the boy to the disciples, but they could not heal him. Jesus responded with frustration: [[v17|Faithless and perverse generation! How long will I be with you? How long will I bear with you?]] His words were addressed to the generation as a whole, including the disciples, the crowd, and the scribes who were arguing (Mark 9:14). He then told them to bring the boy to Him, rebuked the demon, and the boy was healed at that moment.

Privately, the disciples asked why they had failed. Jesus told them it was because of their unbelief (literally, "little faith"). He then gave a vivid promise about faith as small as a mustard seed, which is studied below. Verse 21, [[v21|But this kind doesn’t go out except by prayer and fasting]], is not found in many of the oldest manuscripts of Matthew and may have been added from Mark 9:29, where Jesus says this kind can come out only by prayer. Either way, the point is that the disciples had been relying on themselves rather than depending on God.

### Second Prediction of the Passion (17:22–23)

While they were gathering in Galilee, Jesus again told His disciples that He would be betrayed into human hands, killed, and raised on the third day. This was the second of three major predictions (see Matthew 16:21 and 20:17–19). This time the disciples did not argue; they were [[v23|exceedingly sorry]]. They seemed to hear the part about His death more clearly than the promise of His resurrection.

### The Temple Tax (17:24–27)

When they returned to Capernaum, the collectors of the [[v24|didrachma]] (the two-drachma temple tax) asked Peter whether his teacher paid it. Every Jewish man over twenty was expected to pay this annual tax of half a shekel for the upkeep of the temple, based on Exodus 30:13–16. Peter immediately said yes.

When Peter came into the house, Jesus spoke first, asking him whether kings collect taxes from their own children or from others. Peter answered, "From others." Jesus said, [[Matthew 17:26|Therefore the children are exempt]]. The point was that Jesus, as the Son of God, was not obligated to pay a tax for His Father's house. By extension, His followers, as God's children, shared in that freedom.

Nevertheless, Jesus did not want to cause offense. He told Peter to go to the lake, cast a line, and take the first fish he caught. In its mouth he would find a "stater," a coin worth four drachmas, exactly enough to pay the tax for both Jesus and Peter. Matthew does not record Peter actually catching the fish, but the instruction shows Jesus' authority and provision. It also shows His willingness to set aside His rights for the sake of others.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 17:5 — [[Matthew 17:5|Listen to Him]]

verse: Matthew 17:5

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes the high point of the transfiguration. Jesus had taken Peter, James, and John up a high mountain, where He was transformed before them, His face shining like the sun and His clothes white as light. Moses and Elijah appeared and spoke with Him. Peter, not knowing what to say, offered to build three shelters, one for each of them.

[[v5|While he was still speaking]], God interrupted Peter. A bright cloud overshadowed them. In the Old Testament, God's presence was often revealed in a cloud: it led Israel through the wilderness, rested on Mount Sinai when God gave the law, and filled the tabernacle and temple. The disciples would have understood that God Himself was present.

A voice spoke from the cloud: [[v5|This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased]]. These are the same words the Father spoke at Jesus' baptism (Matthew 3:17), echoing Psalm 2:7 (the royal Son) and Isaiah 42:1 (the chosen Servant). The Father again affirmed Jesus' identity.

Then He added a new command: [[v5|Listen to him]]. This phrase recalls Deuteronomy 18:15, where Moses promised that God would raise up a prophet like him, and said, [[Deuteronomy 18:15|You shall listen to him]]. Jesus is that prophet, and more. The command also corrected Peter's suggestion. Peter wanted to honor Jesus alongside Moses and Elijah; the Father declared that Jesus alone is His Son and the one to whom they must listen. The command was especially fitting after Peter had resisted Jesus' teaching about the cross in chapter 16. The disciples needed to listen to Jesus even when His words were hard to accept.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse affirms the supremacy of Jesus over even the greatest figures of the Old Testament. Moses and Elijah were honored servants of God, but Jesus is God's Son. The Law and the Prophets pointed to Him, and now He is the final and complete revelation of God. Hebrews 1:1–2 says that God spoke in the past through the prophets, but in these last days [[Hebrews 1:2|has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son]].

The transfiguration also strengthened the disciples for what lay ahead. They had just heard that Jesus must suffer and die. The glimpse of His glory showed them that the cross would not be the end. Peter later wrote about this experience, saying that they were eyewitnesses of His majesty when they heard the voice from heaven on the holy mountain (2 Peter 1:16–18).

The command "Listen to him" also reminds believers that hearing God's voice now means attending to Jesus. His words, recorded in the Gospels and explained by His apostles, are the way God speaks to His people.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to listen to Jesus above all other voices. Many voices compete for attention: culture, media, friends, personal desires, and even religious traditions. The Father's command is clear: listen to His Son. Believers can make it a priority to read and reflect on Jesus' words regularly, letting them shape their thoughts and decisions.

Listening to Jesus also means obeying Him. In the Bible, to "listen" or "hear" often includes the idea of obeying. Believers can ask not only "What did Jesus say?" but "How should I respond?" This is especially important when His words are difficult or challenge their preferences, as Peter discovered.

Finally, this verse offers encouragement in difficult seasons. The disciples were about to face the shock of Jesus' arrest and crucifixion. The memory of His glory would help sustain them. Believers can remember that the Jesus they follow is the glorious Son of God, and that suffering in this life will give way to glory.

### Matthew 17:20 — Faith Like a Mustard Seed

verse: Matthew 17:20

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is Jesus' answer to His disciples' question about why they could not heal the boy. While Jesus was on the mountain with Peter, James, and John, the other disciples had tried to drive out the demon that was tormenting the boy, but they failed. Earlier, Jesus had given the Twelve authority to cast out demons (Matthew 10:1, 8), and they had done so before. Now they were puzzled.

Jesus answered, [[v20|Because of your unbelief]]. Many manuscripts read "little faith," the word Jesus often used for His disciples' wavering trust (Matthew 6:30; 8:26; 14:31; 16:8). The problem was not that they lacked a technique but that they were not truly relying on God. Perhaps they had begun to think the power was theirs.

Then Jesus said, [[v20|if you have faith as a grain of mustard seed, you will tell this mountain, ‘Move from here to there,’ and it will move]]. The mustard seed was proverbially tiny, as Jesus had said in the parable of Matthew 13:31–32. His point is that the size of faith matters less than its object. Even very small faith, if it is genuine trust in God, can accomplish great things, because God is the one who acts.

"Move this mountain" was a common Jewish expression for doing something that seemed impossible. Jesus was probably pointing to the mountain they had just come down from. He was not encouraging people to rearrange landscapes but using a vivid image for overcoming impossible obstacles. He concluded, [[v20|nothing will be impossible for you]], meaning nothing that is within God's will for His servants to accomplish.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that the power of faith lies not in the believer but in God. Faith is not a force we generate or a technique for getting what we want. It is trust in God, and God is the one who does the impossible. That is why even small faith, if it is real, can achieve great things. Jesus elsewhere says, [[Matthew 19:26|With men this is impossible, but with God all things are possible]].

The context also shows that faith grows through dependence on God, expressed in prayer. Mark's version of this account records Jesus saying that this kind can only come out by prayer (Mark 9:29). The disciples may have treated their earlier success as their own ability. Faith that depends on God will naturally pray.

At the same time, this verse must be read alongside the rest of Scripture. It does not mean that believers will receive whatever they want if they have enough faith, or that unanswered prayers are always due to a lack of faith. Paul prayed three times for his "thorn in the flesh" to be removed, and God's answer was, [[2 Corinthians 12:9|My grace is sufficient for you]]. Faith trusts God's power and God's wisdom together.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers who feel that their faith is small. Jesus does not require great faith before God can act. He invites people to trust God with whatever faith they have. Believers facing overwhelming situations can bring them to God, trusting that He is able to do what they cannot.

It also calls believers to depend on God rather than on their own abilities, experience, or past success. Even long-time believers can drift into self-reliance in their spiritual lives and ministries. Regular prayer, honest acknowledgment of weakness, and seeking God's help keep faith directed toward Him.

Finally, believers can hold this promise together with trust in God's wisdom. When prayers are not answered in the way they hoped, they need not conclude that their faith was too small. They can continue to trust God, who sometimes moves mountains and sometimes gives grace to endure them.

### Matthew 17:27 — Free, Yet Willing to Avoid Offense

verse: Matthew 17:27

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes a short episode in Capernaum about the temple tax. Each Jewish man was expected to pay half a shekel each year for the support of the temple in Jerusalem, a practice based on Exodus 30:13–16. When the tax collectors asked Peter whether Jesus paid it, Peter quickly said yes.

When Peter came into the house, Jesus raised the issue first. He asked whether kings collect taxes from their own children or from others. Peter answered that kings tax others, not their own families. Jesus concluded, [[v26|Therefore the children are exempt]]. Since the temple was His Father's house, Jesus, as God's Son, should not have to pay a tax for its upkeep. He was making a claim about His unique relationship with God.

But then Jesus said, [[v27|But, lest we cause them to stumble]]. To "cause to stumble" means to give offense or become an obstacle to someone's faith. If Jesus refused to pay, the tax collectors and others might think He did not respect the temple or God's law. That could become an unnecessary barrier to their receiving His message. So Jesus chose to pay, even though He was not obligated.

He told Peter to go to the lake, cast a hook, and take the first fish that came up. In its mouth he would find a "stater," a silver coin worth four drachmas, exactly enough to pay the two-drachma tax for both Jesus and Peter. Jesus included Peter in the payment, showing His care for His disciple and including him among God's children.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This short story reveals two truths about Jesus. First, He is the Son of God, with a unique relationship to the Father and authority over the temple. Earlier He had said that [[Matthew 12:6|one greater than the temple is here]]. Second, He was willing to set aside His rights for the sake of others. This anticipates His greatest act of self-giving on the cross. Paul describes Jesus as one who, though He was in the form of God, did not consider equality with God something to be used for His own advantage, but emptied Himself (Philippians 2:6–7).

The verse also teaches a principle that the apostle Paul developed later. Believers have freedom in Christ, but they should be willing to limit their freedom for the sake of others, so as not to cause unnecessary offense or hinder someone's faith (1 Corinthians 8:9–13; 9:19–23). Christian freedom is not about insisting on rights but about loving others.

Finally, the miraculous provision shows that Jesus can meet His followers' needs, often in unexpected ways. The Lord who commands the sea and its creatures provided what was needed.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to consider how they use their freedom. There may be many things believers are free to do, but they can ask whether exercising that freedom would cause unnecessary offense or become a stumbling block for others. Sometimes love means setting aside personal rights to serve others or to avoid harming someone's faith.

It also encourages believers to fulfill civic and community responsibilities in a way that honors Christ. Paying taxes, keeping commitments, and respecting legitimate authority can be part of a good witness. Jesus' example shows that believers should not create needless conflict over matters that do not compromise their faith.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that God provides for His people. When they choose to act in love and integrity, even at personal cost, they can trust that God will supply what they need.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 17 moves from the mountaintop to the valley, and both settings speak to the life of faith. On the mountain, the disciples saw Jesus' glory and heard the Father's command to listen to Him. Believers today may experience times of special closeness to God, but even ordinary days are lived in the presence of the glorious Son of God. The Father's command, "Listen to him," remains the guiding principle for every season. Making time to hear Jesus' words in Scripture, and responding with obedience, keeps believers centered on Him.

The valley shows the reality of struggle. The disciples could not help the suffering boy because their faith had drifted from dependence on God. Believers often experience similar failures when they rely on their own abilities, routines, or past successes. Jesus' answer is not to try harder but to trust God, even with small faith, and to depend on Him in prayer. This applies to ministry, family responsibilities, and personal struggles.

The chapter also continues to prepare the disciples for the cross. Jesus repeated that He would be betrayed, killed, and raised. The transfiguration showed them that suffering would lead to glory. Believers facing hardship can remember that the path of following Jesus may include suffering, but it leads to resurrection and glory.

Finally, the temple tax story teaches a balance between freedom and love. Jesus was free from the obligation but chose to pay to avoid needless offense. Believers can follow His example by setting aside their rights for the sake of others, fulfilling responsibilities with integrity, and trusting God to provide what they need.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, thank You for revealing the glory of Your beloved Son on the mountain and for commanding us to listen to Him. Help me to hear His voice above all the other voices in my life and to obey what He says, even when His words are hard to accept.

When I face situations that seem impossible, remind me that even small faith in You can move mountains, because nothing is too hard for You. Forgive me for relying on my own strength, and teach me to depend on You in prayer. Strengthen my hope when I walk through suffering, knowing that the cross leads to glory. Help me to use my freedom with love, setting aside my rights when doing so will help others, and to trust You to provide all I need. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
