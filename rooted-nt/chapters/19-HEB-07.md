# Hebrews 7
theme: A Priest Forever After the Order of Melchizedek
main: Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, met Abraham after a battle, blessed him, and received a tenth of the spoils from him. His name means "king of righteousness" and his title "king of peace," and because Scripture records no genealogy, birth, or death for him, he resembles the Son of God as a priest who remains forever. His greatness is shown in that Abraham, the ancestor of the Levitical priests, gave him a tenth and received his blessing. If perfection could have come through the Levitical priesthood, there would have been no need for a different priest in the order of Melchizedek. Jesus, from the tribe of Judah, became a priest not by ancestry but by the power of an indestructible life, as Psalm 110 declares. The former regulation was set aside because it was weak and could make nothing perfect, and a better hope has been introduced, through which believers draw near to God. Because God confirmed Jesus' priesthood with an oath, He is the guarantee of a better covenant. Unlike the many priests who died, Jesus lives forever, so He is able to save completely those who come to God through Him, since He always lives to intercede for them. He is the high priest we need: holy, blameless, pure, exalted above the heavens, who offered Himself once for all.

## Chapter Background and Story

Melchizedek appears only twice in the Old Testament. In Genesis 14:17–20, after Abraham rescued his nephew Lot by defeating a coalition of kings, Melchizedek, king of Salem and priest of God Most High, came out with bread and wine, blessed Abraham, and received a tenth of the plunder. Then he disappears from the story. Centuries later, in Psalm 110:4, God declares to David's Lord, the Messiah, "You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek."

These two brief passages become the foundation of one of the most profound arguments in the New Testament. The author of Hebrews wants to show that Jesus is a true high priest, even though He was not descended from Aaron or from the tribe of Levi. Psalm 110 shows that God always intended a different kind of priesthood: one that combined kingship and priesthood, that was based on God's oath rather than ancestry, and that would last forever.

This chapter can feel complex to modern readers, but its message is simple and powerful: Jesus is a better high priest than any who came before, and because He lives forever, He can save completely all who come to God through Him.

### Who Was Melchizedek? (7:1–3)

The description of Melchizedek is studied below.

### Greater Than Abraham and Levi (7:4–10)

[[v4|Now consider how great this man was, to whom even Abraham the patriarch gave a tenth out of the best plunder]]. Abraham was the revered founder of Israel. Yet he gave a tenth to Melchizedek, acknowledging Melchizedek's greater position.

[[v5|They indeed of the sons of Levi who receive the priest’s office have a commandment to take tithes from the people according to the law, that is, of their brothers, though these have come out of the body of Abraham]], [[v6|but he whose genealogy is not counted from them has accepted tithes from Abraham, and has blessed him who has the promises]]. Under the Law, the Levitical priests received tithes from their fellow Israelites. But Melchizedek, who was not a Levite, received tithes from Abraham himself.

[[v7|But without any dispute the lesser is blessed by the greater]]. In the ancient world, the one who gives a blessing has greater standing than the one who receives it. Melchizedek blessed Abraham, showing his superiority.

[[v8|Here people who die receive tithes, but there one receives tithes of whom it is testified that he lives]]. The Levitical priests were mortal. Melchizedek, because Scripture records no death for him, is presented as one who "lives," pointing to the eternal priesthood of Christ.

[[v9|We can say that through Abraham even Levi, who receives tithes, has paid tithes]], [[v10|for he was yet in the body of his father when Melchizedek met him]]. The author uses a way of thinking common in the ancient world: descendants were considered present in their ancestor. In a sense, Levi, the ancestor of the priests, paid tithes to Melchizedek through Abraham. So Melchizedek's priesthood is greater than Levi's. The author signals that this is a figure of speech: "We can say."

### A Change in the Priesthood (7:11–19)

[[v11|Now if perfection was through the Levitical priesthood (for under it the people have received the law), what further need was there for another priest to arise after the order of Melchizedek, and not be called after the order of Aaron?]] If the Levitical priesthood could have brought people into complete relationship with God, why would God, through David, speak of a new priest of a different order? The promise of a new priest shows that the old one was not final.

[[v12|For the priesthood being changed, there is of necessity a change made also in the law]]. The priesthood and the Law were tied together. A new priesthood means a new covenant arrangement. Chapter 8 will develop this.

[[v13|For he of whom these things are said belongs to another tribe, from which no one has officiated at the altar]]. [[v14|For it is evident that our Lord has sprung out of Judah, about which tribe Moses spoke nothing concerning priesthood]]. Jesus descended from Judah, the royal tribe of David (Matthew 1:1–3; Revelation 5:5), not from Levi. Under the Law, He could not have been a priest. His priesthood rests on a different basis.

[[v15|This is yet more abundantly evident, if after the likeness of Melchizedek there arises another priest]], [[v16|who has been made, not after the law of a fleshly commandment, but after the power of an endless life]]; [[v17|for it is testified, “You are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek.”]] Levitical priests became priests because of a legal requirement about physical descent. Jesus became a priest through "the power of an endless life," His resurrection life that can never end. Why the old regulation was set aside is studied below.

### Confirmed by an Oath (7:20–22)

[[v20|Inasmuch as he was not made priest without the taking of an oath]] [[v21|(for they indeed have been made priests without an oath), but he with an oath by him that says of him, “The Lord swore and will not change his mind, ‘You are a priest forever, according to the order of Melchizedek.’”]] The Levitical priests were appointed without an oath. But God swore an oath when He appointed the Messiah as priest. As Hebrews 6:17–18 explained, God's oath makes His purpose unchangeable.

[[v22|By so much, Jesus has become the guarantee of a better covenant]]. A "guarantee" is a pledge or surety, one who stands behind a promise to ensure it is kept. Jesus guarantees the new covenant. This is the first mention of the "better covenant," which chapter 8 will explain.

### A Priest Who Lives Forever (7:23–28)

Jesus' eternal priesthood and His perfect character are studied below. [[v28|For the law appoints men as high priests who have weakness, but the word of the oath, which came after the law, appoints a Son forever who has been perfected]]. The contrast is complete. The Law appointed weak, mortal, sinful men. God's oath, given after the Law through David, appointed the Son, perfect and eternal.

## Important Verses Explained

### Hebrews 7:1–3 — Melchizedek, King of Righteousness and Peace

verse: Hebrews 7:1-3

#### Simple Explanation

The author summarizes the story from Genesis 14. Melchizedek was "king of Salem, priest of God Most High." Salem is generally identified with Jerusalem (Psalm 76:2). Remarkably, Melchizedek was both a king and a priest, a combination not permitted in Israel, where kings came from Judah and priests from Levi.

He "met Abraham returning from the slaughter of the kings and blessed him." And Abraham gave him "a tenth part of all," a tithe.

Verse 2 explains the meaning of his name and title. "Melchizedek" means "king of righteousness." "King of Salem" means "king of peace," since "Salem" is related to the Hebrew word *shalom*. Righteousness and peace come together in him.

Verse 3: "without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life." In Genesis, Melchizedek appears suddenly, with no mention of his parents, ancestors, birth, or death. This was unusual, especially for a priest, since priests in Israel had to prove their descent from Aaron.

"But made like the Son of God, remains a priest continually." Because of the silence of Scripture about his origins and death, Melchizedek serves as a picture, a foreshadowing, of the Son of God, whose priesthood truly has no beginning or end.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Interpreters have understood Melchizedek in different ways. Most understand him to have been a real human king and priest, and believe the author is drawing a lesson from what Scripture says and does not say about him. The silence about his genealogy and death makes him a fitting picture of Christ's eternal priesthood. Note that the author says Melchizedek was "made like the Son of God," not that the Son was made like Melchizedek: Christ is the reality, and Melchizedek is the picture. Some interpreters, especially in earlier centuries, have suggested that Melchizedek was an appearance of the pre-incarnate Christ, though most do not hold this view. Either way, he points to Jesus.

Melchizedek's names are significant. Jesus is the true King of righteousness and King of peace. Isaiah prophesied that the Messiah would be called "Prince of Peace" and would reign in justice and righteousness (Isaiah 9:6–7). In Christ, righteousness and peace meet. Psalm 85:10 says, [[Psalm 85:10|Righteousness and peace have kissed each other]].

The bread and wine Melchizedek brought (Genesis 14:18) have reminded many Christians of the Lord's Supper, though Hebrews does not mention this connection.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Melchizedek's story shows that God was preparing for Christ long before He came. Even in an obscure episode in Genesis, God placed a picture of His Son. Believers can read the Old Testament with expectation, looking for the ways it points to Christ.

The names "king of righteousness" and "king of peace" remind believers that in Christ they have both. He makes them righteous before God and brings them peace with God (Romans 5:1). They can rest in His reign.

Finally, Melchizedek's eternal priesthood, pictured in the silence of Scripture, points to the security believers have in Christ, whose priesthood will never end.

### Hebrews 7:18–19 — A Better Hope

verse: Hebrews 7:18-19

#### Simple Explanation

"For there is an annulling of a foregoing commandment because of its weakness and uselessness." The "foregoing commandment" is the regulation in the Law about the Levitical priesthood. It has been set aside, not because it was evil, but because it was "weak" and "useless" for bringing people fully to God.

Verse 19: "(for the law made nothing perfect)." The Law could not bring people to complete fellowship with God. It could reveal sin, regulate worship, and point to Christ, but it could not cleanse the conscience or make people truly right with God (Hebrews 10:1–4).

"And a bringing in of a better hope, through which we draw near to God." Something better has come: a hope based on Christ's priesthood. Through Him, believers "draw near to God." This was the very thing the old system could not fully accomplish.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The phrase "draw near to God" is central to Hebrews. Under the old covenant, access to God was limited. The people stood outside; priests entered the Holy Place; only the high priest entered the Most Holy Place, once a year. Now, through Christ, all believers can draw near (Hebrews 4:16; 10:19–22).

The "weakness" of the Law is not a criticism of God, who gave it. The Law did exactly what God intended: it revealed His holiness, exposed sin, and prepared for Christ. Paul says the Law was weak [[Romans 8:3|through the flesh]], because sinful humans could not keep it. It was a tutor leading to Christ (Galatians 3:24).

The "better hope" is one of many "better" things in Hebrews: a better covenant (7:22), better promises (8:6), better sacrifices (9:23), a better possession (10:34), a better country (11:16), and a better resurrection (11:35). In every case, Christ brings what the old covenant could only anticipate.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers do not need to rely on religious rituals or rules to draw near to God. Through Christ, they can come to Him directly and confidently.

This passage also helps believers appreciate the Old Testament without being bound by its regulations. The Law served a good purpose, and its moral principles reflect God's character, but its priestly system has been fulfilled in Christ.

Finally, the "better hope" invites believers to live with confidence. Their hope is not in their own ability to keep rules, but in Christ, who has opened the way to God.

### Hebrews 7:24–25 — Able to Save Completely

verse: Hebrews 7:24-25

#### Simple Explanation

Verse 23 noted that there were many Levitical priests because death kept each one from continuing. Over the centuries, priests died and were replaced.

Verse 24: "But he, because he lives forever, has his priesthood unchangeable." Jesus will never die again. His priesthood is permanent. He will never be replaced.

Verse 25: "Therefore he is also able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him." "To the uttermost" can mean completely, fully, or forever. Jesus saves entirely: every part of a person, for all time. There is no limit to His saving power.

"Those who draw near to God through him." Salvation comes to those who approach God through Jesus.

"Seeing that he lives forever to make intercession for them." Jesus continually prays for His people. He stands before the Father on their behalf, representing them, pleading for them, and applying the benefits of His sacrifice to them.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This is one of the most comforting verses in Scripture. Jesus' intercession is ongoing. He did not save His people once and then leave them. He continues to work on their behalf. Romans 8:34 says that Christ Jesus, who died and was raised, is at the right hand of God and is interceding for us. First John 2:1 says that if anyone sins, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous.

Jesus' intercession was foreshadowed in His earthly ministry. He prayed for Peter that his faith would not fail (Luke 22:31–32). He prayed for His disciples and for all who would believe through them (John 17:9, 20). That same intercession continues now.

"To the uttermost" means that no sin is too great, no situation too hopeless, no person too far gone. Jesus is able to save completely. And because He lives forever, His saving work will never end.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers who feel weak, tempted, or overwhelmed can take comfort that Jesus is praying for them right now. They are not alone in their struggles.

This verse also offers hope to anyone who feels beyond saving. Jesus is able to save "to the uttermost." There is no limit to His power or His grace.

Finally, believers can rest in the permanence of Christ's priesthood. Their salvation does not depend on a human priest who might fail or die. It rests on the living Christ, who never changes.

### Hebrews 7:26–27 — Holy, Blameless, and Offered Once for All

verse: Hebrews 7:26-27

#### Simple Explanation

"For such a high priest was fitting for us." Jesus is exactly the high priest we need. The author describes Him with five phrases.

"Holy": devoted to God, pure in character. "Guiltless": innocent, without evil or deceit. "Undefiled": morally pure, untouched by sin. "Separated from sinners": though He lived among sinners and befriended them, He was different from them in His sinlessness, and now He is exalted above them. "Made higher than the heavens": exalted to the highest place, at God's right hand.

Verse 27: "who doesn’t need, like those high priests, to offer up sacrifices daily, first for his own sins, and then for the sins of the people." Human high priests had to offer sacrifices repeatedly, and they had to offer for their own sins first. Jesus had no sins of His own.

"For he did this once for all, when he offered up himself." Jesus' sacrifice was "once for all," complete and never to be repeated. And remarkably, He was both the priest and the sacrifice. He offered Himself.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses introduce a major theme that chapters 9 and 10 will develop: the sufficiency of Christ's single sacrifice. Under the old covenant, sacrifices had to be repeated endlessly, because they could not truly take away sin (Hebrews 10:1–4, 11). Christ's sacrifice was different. It was offered once, and it was enough.

The fact that Jesus is both priest and sacrifice is unique. In the old system, the priest offered an animal. Jesus offered His own life. He is the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world (John 1:29), and He is the High Priest who offers that Lamb.

Jesus' sinlessness is essential. A sinful priest would need a sacrifice for himself. A sinful sacrifice would be unacceptable. Jesus, being holy and blameless, is both the perfect priest and the perfect offering (1 Peter 1:18–19).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can rest in the finished work of Christ. His sacrifice was once for all. Nothing needs to be added. They do not need to earn God's forgiveness through repeated efforts or rituals.

This passage also invites worship. Jesus is holy, blameless, and exalted, and He offered Himself for sinners. His love and sacrifice deserve wholehearted praise.

Finally, believers can find confidence in approaching God. Their high priest is perfect, and His sacrifice is complete. They can draw near to God with assurance.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Hebrews 7 shows that Jesus is a better high priest than any who came before. Through the mysterious figure of Melchizedek, king of righteousness and king of peace, God foreshadowed a priest who would combine kingship and priesthood, whose priesthood would rest not on ancestry but on God's oath, and who would remain a priest forever. Jesus fulfills this picture perfectly.

The chapter explains that the Levitical priesthood, though given by God, could not bring perfection. It was weak because it depended on mortal, sinful men and could not fully cleanse or bring people near to God. In Christ, a better hope has been introduced, through which believers can draw near to God. Jesus guarantees a better covenant.

At the heart of the chapter is a glorious truth: because Jesus lives forever, He is able to save completely those who come to God through Him, and He always lives to intercede for them. Believers are never alone. Their high priest is praying for them right now. No sin is too great and no situation too hopeless for His saving power.

Finally, Jesus is exactly the high priest we need: holy, blameless, pure, separated from sinners, and exalted above the heavens. Unlike the old priests, He did not need to offer sacrifices repeatedly or for His own sins. He offered Himself once for all. Believers can rest in His finished work, draw near to God with confidence, and worship the King of righteousness and peace who is their eternal priest.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the true King of righteousness and King of peace, a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. Thank You that Your priesthood rests on the power of an indestructible life and on the Father's unchangeable oath. Thank You for bringing a better hope, through which I can draw near to God. Thank You that You are the guarantee of a better covenant. I praise You that You are able to save completely those who come to God through You, and that You always live to intercede for me. When I am weak, remind me that You are praying for me. Thank You that You are holy, blameless, and pure, and that You offered Yourself once for all as the perfect sacrifice. Help me to rest in Your finished work and to draw near to God with confidence. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
