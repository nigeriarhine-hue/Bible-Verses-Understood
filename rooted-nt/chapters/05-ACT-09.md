# Acts 9
theme: The Persecutor Becomes a Preacher, and the Church Grows in Peace
main: Saul of Tarsus, on his way to arrest believers in Damascus, is stopped by a blinding light and the voice of the risen Jesus asking why Saul is persecuting Him. Blind for three days, Saul waits and prays until a fearful disciple named Ananias obeys the Lord, calls him "Brother Saul," and lays hands on him so that he regains his sight and is filled with the Spirit. Saul immediately begins proclaiming Jesus as the Son of God, escapes plots against his life, and is welcomed in Jerusalem through Barnabas. The church enjoys peace and growth, and Peter heals Aeneas and raises Tabitha from the dead.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 9 tells one of the most important events in Christian history: the conversion of Saul, who would become the apostle Paul. Luke considers this event so important that he tells it three times in Acts (here, in 22:3–16, and in 26:9–18). Paul himself refers to it in his letters (1 Corinthians 15:8–10; Galatians 1:13–17; Philippians 3:4–11; 1 Timothy 1:12–16).

Saul was born in Tarsus, a major city in the Roman province of Cilicia (in modern-day Turkey). He was a Jew of the tribe of Benjamin, a Pharisee, a Roman citizen by birth, and a student of the great rabbi Gamaliel in Jerusalem (Acts 22:3; Philippians 3:5). He was zealous for the Law and saw the followers of Jesus as a dangerous threat to Israel's faith. He was present at Stephen's death and led the persecution that followed.

### Saul on the Road to Damascus (9:1–9)

[[v1|But Saul, still breathing threats and slaughter against the disciples of the Lord, went to the high priest]]. Saul's hostility was so intense that it was like the very air he breathed. He asked the high priest for letters to the synagogues in Damascus, about 150 miles north of Jerusalem, authorizing him to arrest any who belonged to [[v2|the Way]] and bring them bound to Jerusalem. "The Way" was an early name for the Christian movement (Acts 19:9, 23; 24:14), perhaps reflecting Jesus' words, [[John 14:6|I am the way]].

As Saul approached Damascus, [[v3|suddenly a light from the sky shone around him]]. Paul later said it was about noon and the light was brighter than the sun (Acts 26:13). He fell to the ground and heard a voice. This encounter is studied below.

The men traveling with Saul [[v7|stood speechless, hearing the sound, but seeing no one]]. (In Acts 22:9, Paul says they did not [[Acts 22:9|understand]] or "hear" the voice; the Greek word can mean either hearing a sound or understanding speech, so they heard a sound but did not understand the words.)

Saul got up, but when he opened his eyes, he could see nothing. His companions led him by the hand into Damascus. The man who had come to drag others away in chains was now led like a helpless child. [[v9|He was without sight for three days, and neither ate nor drank]]. These three days of darkness and fasting were a time of profound reflection and repentance.

### Ananias Sent to Saul (9:10–19)

In Damascus lived a disciple named Ananias (not the same man as in chapter 5). The Lord spoke to him in a vision, telling him to go to a house on Straight Street and ask for Saul of Tarsus, who was praying. Saul had seen a vision of a man named Ananias coming to restore his sight. The street called Straight still exists in Damascus today.

Ananias was afraid: [[v13|Lord, I have heard from many about this man, how much evil he did to your saints at Jerusalem]]. He knew Saul had come with authority to arrest believers. His honesty with the Lord is understandable. The Lord's answer is studied below.

Ananias obeyed. His greeting is also studied below. [[v18|Immediately something like scales fell from his eyes, and he received his sight. He arose and was baptized]]. Saul ate and regained his strength.

### Saul Preaches Christ (9:19–31)

Saul stayed with the believers in Damascus, and [[v20|Immediately in the synagogues he proclaimed the Christ, that he is the Son of God]]. This is the only time in Acts that the title "Son of God" appears in a summary of preaching. Those who heard him were amazed: [[v21|Isn’t this he who in Jerusalem made havoc of those who called on this name?]] Saul grew stronger and confounded the Jews of Damascus, [[v22|proving that this is the Christ]].

Paul writes in Galatians 1:17–18 that after his conversion he went to Arabia (probably the Nabatean kingdom east and south of Damascus) and later returned to Damascus, and that three years passed before he went to Jerusalem. Luke's phrase [[v23|When many days were fulfilled]] covers this period.

Eventually, some Jews in Damascus plotted to kill him. They watched the city gates day and night. But Saul's disciples [[v25|took him by night and let him down through the wall, lowering him in a basket]]. Paul later mentions this humbling escape as one of the things he "boasts" about in his weakness (2 Corinthians 11:32–33). The hunter had become the hunted.

When Saul came to Jerusalem, the believers were afraid of him. Barnabas's role is studied below. Saul preached boldly in Jerusalem and debated with the Greek-speaking Jews, the same groups Stephen had debated, but they tried to kill him. The believers took him down to Caesarea and sent him home to Tarsus, where he would remain for several years until Barnabas brought him to Antioch (11:25–26).

Luke then summarizes: [[v31|So the assemblies throughout all Judea, Galilee, and Samaria had peace and were built up. They were multiplied, walking in the fear of the Lord and in the comfort of the Holy Spirit]]. The persecution had ended with its leader's conversion.

### Peter Heals Aeneas and Raises Tabitha (9:32–43)

Luke now turns back to Peter, who was traveling among the believers. At Lydda, near the coast, he found a man named Aeneas who had been paralyzed and bedridden for eight years. Peter said, [[v34|Aeneas, Jesus Christ heals you. Get up and make your bed!]] Peter made it clear that Jesus was the healer. Aeneas got up immediately, and many in the region turned to the Lord.

In nearby Joppa lived a disciple named Tabitha (Aramaic) or Dorcas (Greek), both meaning "gazelle." [[v36|This woman was full of good works and acts of mercy which she did]]. She became sick and died. The believers sent for Peter. When he arrived, [[v39|All the widows stood by him weeping, and showing the tunics and other garments which Dorcas had made while she was with them]]. Dorcas had clothed the poor widows with her own hands. Her love was visible in every garment.

Peter sent everyone out, knelt, and prayed. Then he said, [[v40|Tabitha, get up!]] The scene recalls Jesus raising Jairus's daughter, when He put everyone out and said, "Talitha cumi" (Mark 5:40–41), words that differ from Peter's by only one letter in Aramaic. She opened her eyes and sat up. Peter presented her alive to the believers and widows, and many in Joppa believed in the Lord.

Peter stayed in Joppa with [[v43|a tanner named Simon]]. Tanners worked with the skins of dead animals, which made them ritually unclean according to the Law. Many strict Jews avoided them. Peter's willingness to stay with a tanner hints that God was already preparing him for the even greater barrier he would cross in the next chapter.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 9:4–5 — [[Acts 9:4|Why Do You Persecute Me?]]

verse: Acts 9:4-5

#### Simple Explanation

Saul was traveling to Damascus with letters from the high priest, ready to arrest followers of Jesus and bring them back to Jerusalem in chains. He was convinced he was serving God. As he neared the city, a blinding light from heaven flashed around him.

[[v4|He fell on the earth, and heard a voice saying to him, “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?”]] The voice called him by name, twice, a pattern in Scripture that signals a personal and urgent call from God (Genesis 22:11; Exodus 3:4; 1 Samuel 3:10).

Notice the question: "Why do you persecute me?" Saul had been persecuting the followers of Jesus, not Jesus Himself, or so he thought. But the voice identified completely with the believers. To attack them was to attack Him.

Saul asked, [[v5|Who are you, Lord?]] He recognized that he was encountering a heavenly being, but he did not yet know who it was. The word "Lord" may have been a respectful address, but it soon took on its full meaning.

[[v5|The Lord said, “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.”]] In that moment, Saul's entire understanding collapsed. The Jesus he believed was a dead false messiah was alive and speaking to him from heaven in glory. Everything he had believed about Jesus was wrong. Everything the believers had said was true.

The Lord then told him, [[v6|But rise up and enter into the city, then you will be told what you must do]]. The proud leader would now have to wait and receive instructions.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This encounter reveals the deep union between Christ and His people. Jesus said, "Why do you persecute me?" The church is the body of Christ (1 Corinthians 12:27). What happens to believers matters to Jesus personally. He feels their suffering. Jesus had taught that whatever is done to the least of His brothers is done to Him (Matthew 25:40).

It also shows that no one is beyond the reach of God's grace. Saul was the church's fiercest enemy, actively trying to destroy it. Yet Jesus appeared to him not in judgment but in mercy. Paul later wrote, [[1 Timothy 1:15|Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners, of whom I am chief]].

Finally, the encounter shows that conversion is ultimately the work of God. Saul was not searching for Jesus; he was fighting against Him. Jesus took the initiative and confronted him. Paul later described himself as one whom God [[Galatians 1:15|separated me from my mother’s womb, and called me through his grace]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage gives hope for those who seem far from God. Some people actively oppose the Christian faith. Saul's story shows that God can transform even the most hostile heart. Believers can pray with hope for those who seem least likely to believe.

It also comforts believers who are persecuted or mistreated for their faith. Jesus identifies with them. Their suffering is His suffering. They are not forgotten.

Finally, the passage invites everyone to reconsider what they think about Jesus. Saul was certain he was right, but he was wrong. Many people today have firm opinions about Jesus without having truly encountered Him. Like Saul, they can ask, "Who are you, Lord?" and be open to His answer.

### Acts 9:15–16 — A Chosen Vessel

verse: Acts 9:15-16

#### Simple Explanation

The Lord had told Ananias, a disciple in Damascus, to go to Saul and restore his sight. Ananias was understandably afraid. He had heard about the harm Saul had done to believers in Jerusalem, and he knew Saul had come to Damascus with authority to arrest them. Going to Saul seemed dangerous, even foolish.

The Lord answered, [[v15|Go your way, for he is my chosen vessel to bear my name before the nations and kings, and the children of Israel]]. God had chosen Saul for a special purpose. A "vessel" is a container or instrument. Saul would carry the name of Jesus to others.

Notice the three groups: "the nations" (the Gentiles), "kings," and "the children of Israel." Saul would become the apostle to the Gentiles, carrying the gospel across the Roman world. He would also testify before kings and rulers, including King Agrippa and possibly Caesar. And he would continue to preach to his own Jewish people, often going first to the synagogue in each city.

Then the Lord added something sobering: [[v16|For I will show him how many things he must suffer for my name’s sake]]. Saul, who had made believers suffer, would now suffer for the name of Jesus. This was not revenge but the path of discipleship. Paul later described his sufferings in detail: beatings, imprisonments, shipwrecks, dangers, hunger, and sleepless nights (2 Corinthians 11:23–28).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal God's sovereign choice and purpose. Saul did not choose God; God chose Saul. Paul later wrote that God's grace toward him was undeserved and transforming: [[1 Corinthians 15:10|But by the grace of God I am what I am]].

They also show that God's choice is for service, not privilege. Saul was chosen to carry Christ's name to others. Every believer is chosen by God for a purpose, to bear witness to Christ and to serve others.

Finally, these verses show that suffering is often part of faithful service. Jesus did not promise Saul an easy life. He promised suffering. Yet Paul later wrote that he considered his sufferings worth it, because through them he came to know Christ more deeply (Philippians 3:10).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to see themselves as vessels for Christ. Every believer can carry the name of Jesus into their families, workplaces, and communities. God has a purpose for each life.

They also prepare believers to accept suffering as part of following Jesus. Faithful service does not guarantee comfort. Believers who suffer for their faith can remember that this has always been part of the path of discipleship, and that God is with them in it.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to trust God when He calls them to do something frightening. Ananias was afraid, but he obeyed. God may call believers to reach out to people they fear or to do things that feel risky. They can trust that God knows what He is doing.

### Acts 9:17 — [[Acts 9:17|Brother Saul]]

verse: Acts 9:17

#### Simple Explanation

Ananias had every reason to be afraid of Saul. Saul had come to Damascus to arrest believers like him. But the Lord told Ananias that Saul was His chosen instrument, and so Ananias went.

[[v17|Ananias departed and entered into the house]]. He obeyed despite his fear. He walked into the house where the persecutor was staying.

[[v17|Laying his hands on him, he said, “Brother Saul]]. These two words are among the most beautiful in Acts. Ananias did not call him "Saul the persecutor" or "enemy." He called him "Brother." Saul had come to Damascus as an enemy of the church; now he was welcomed as a member of the family. Ananias touched the man who had come to bind believers, laying his hands on him in blessing.

[[v17|the Lord, who appeared to you on the road by which you came, has sent me that you may receive your sight and be filled with the Holy Spirit]]. Ananias explained that he had been sent by the same Lord who had appeared to Saul on the road. He came to bring healing and the gift of the Spirit.

Immediately something like scales fell from Saul's eyes, and he could see again. He got up and was baptized.

Ananias is mentioned only briefly in Scripture, and we know little else about him. But his faithful obedience and his welcoming words made him a key figure in the conversion of the greatest missionary of the early church.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the power of grace to transform relationships. Through Christ, an enemy becomes a brother. Paul later wrote that believers who were once alienated from God and from one another have been reconciled through the cross (Ephesians 2:13–16). Ananias's words put that reconciliation into practice.

It also shows the importance of ordinary believers in God's work. Ananias was not an apostle or a famous leader. He was simply a faithful disciple who listened to God and obeyed. God often uses unknown believers to accomplish great things.

Finally, the restoring of Saul's sight is a picture of spiritual sight. Saul had been spiritually blind, unable to see that Jesus was the Messiah. Now his eyes were opened physically and spiritually. Paul later described his mission as opening people's eyes, [[Acts 26:18|that they may turn from darkness to light]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse challenges believers to welcome those whose past is troubling. When someone with a difficult history comes to faith, believers may be suspicious or hesitant. Ananias's example encourages them to welcome new believers as brothers and sisters, trusting God's work in their lives. At the same time, wisdom and appropriate safeguards remain important, especially where harm has been done to vulnerable people.

It also encourages believers to obey God even when they are afraid. Ananias's fear was real, but his obedience was greater. Believers can follow his example, trusting God when He leads them into uncomfortable situations.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that small acts of faithfulness matter. A single visit, a word of welcome, or a prayer can change someone's life and have effects far beyond what anyone can imagine.

### Acts 9:26–27 — Barnabas Stands with Saul

verse: Acts 9:26-27

#### Simple Explanation

After Saul's conversion and his ministry in Damascus, he traveled to Jerusalem. He wanted to join the believers there. But [[v26|they were all afraid of him, not believing that he was a disciple]]. The believers in Jerusalem remembered Saul as the man who had dragged their friends and family members to prison. Some may have thought his conversion was a trick to infiltrate the church and arrest more believers.

Their fear was understandable. Saul had done real harm. Trust is not easily rebuilt.

[[v27|But Barnabas took him and brought him to the apostles]]. Barnabas, whose name means "Son of Encouragement" (4:36), stepped forward. He took Saul by the hand, so to speak, and brought him to the apostles.

Barnabas [[v27|declared to them how he had seen the Lord on the way, and that he had spoken to him, and how at Damascus he had preached boldly in the name of Jesus]]. He told them Saul's story: his encounter with Jesus and his bold preaching in Damascus. Barnabas vouched for him.

Because of Barnabas, Saul was accepted. He stayed with the apostles and preached boldly in Jerusalem.

Barnabas would continue to play a key role in Saul's life. Later, he brought Saul from Tarsus to Antioch to help lead the growing church there, and the two traveled together on the first missionary journey (Acts 11:25–26; 13:2).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage shows the vital ministry of encouragement. Without Barnabas, Saul might have remained isolated and rejected by the Jerusalem church. Barnabas saw what God was doing in Saul's life and stood with him when others would not. Paul later urged believers to [[1 Thessalonians 5:11|exhort one another, and build each other up]].

It also shows the importance of advocacy in the church. Barnabas used his credibility to open a door for someone else. Those who are trusted can help others be welcomed, especially those who are new, different, or have a troubled past.

Finally, the passage shows that God often works through relationships. Saul's transformation was God's work, but God used Ananias and Barnabas to bring Saul into the community of believers. Faith grows within community.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage encourages believers to be like Barnabas. Many people in churches feel unwelcome, overlooked, or misunderstood, whether new believers, newcomers, or people with difficult histories. Believers can take the initiative to welcome them, listen to their stories, and help them find their place.

It also reminds believers that encouragement can change lives. A word of support or an act of trust can give someone the confidence to keep going. Barnabas's encouragement helped launch the ministry of the greatest missionary in history.

Finally, the passage acknowledges that fear and caution are understandable. The Jerusalem believers had good reasons to be wary. Wisdom is needed in welcoming those with a history of harm. But fear should not have the final word when there is clear evidence of God's transforming work.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 9 shows that no one is beyond the reach of God's grace. Saul, the fierce persecutor of the church, became its greatest missionary. Believers can pray with hope for those who seem far from God, even those who oppose the faith, knowing that Jesus can meet anyone and transform their life.

The chapter also shows how deeply Jesus identifies with His people. When Saul persecuted the believers, Jesus said, "Why do you persecute me?" Believers who suffer for their faith can take comfort in knowing that Jesus shares their suffering. Believers are also challenged to treat one another with care, since how they treat Christ's people is how they treat Christ.

Acts 9 highlights the importance of ordinary believers. Ananias obeyed despite his fear and welcomed Saul as a brother. Barnabas stood with Saul when others were afraid. Dorcas served widows with her hands. These faithful believers show that every act of obedience, welcome, and service matters in God's kingdom.

Finally, the chapter shows a church growing in peace, walking in the fear of the Lord and the comfort of the Holy Spirit, with the power of Jesus at work through healing and restoration. Believers today can seek to be part of such a church: humble, encouraging, welcoming, and confident in the power of the risen Lord.

## Chapter Prayer

Make me a faithful vessel to carry Your name, even when it costs me. Give me the courage of Ananias to obey You when I am afraid, and the heart of Barnabas to welcome and encourage others.
