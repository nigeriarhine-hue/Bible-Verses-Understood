# John 7
theme: Rivers of Living Water at the Feast of Booths
main: At the Feast of Booths in Jerusalem, Jesus teaches openly in the temple while the city argues about who He is. He says that anyone willing to do God's will can recognize that His teaching comes from God, and He calls people to judge rightly rather than by appearances. On the great final day of the feast, He cries out an invitation to the thirsty to come to Him and drink, promising rivers of living water, which John explains as the Holy Spirit. The crowd divides, the officers sent to arrest Him return amazed, and Nicodemus begins to speak up for Him.

## Chapter Background and Story

John 7 takes place around the Feast of Booths, also called Tabernacles, one of the three great pilgrim festivals of Israel. For seven days, families lived in temporary shelters made of branches to remember how God cared for Israel during forty years in the wilderness (Leviticus 23:33–43). It was also a joyful harvest festival. By Jesus' time, two ceremonies had become central: a daily procession in which a priest carried water from the Pool of Siloam and poured it out at the altar, accompanied by prayers for rain and readings from the prophets, and the lighting of great lamps in the temple courts. Both form the background for what Jesus says in John 7 and 8.

The chapter is filled with questions and divided opinions. "Where is He?" "Is He a good man?" "How does He know so much?" "Is this the Christ?" "Can the Christ come from Galilee?" John shows that Jesus forces people to decide.

### Jesus and His Brothers (7:1–9)

Jesus remained in Galilee because the leaders in Judea wanted to kill Him (5:18). As the Feast of Booths approached, His brothers urged Him to go to Jerusalem and show Himself openly: [[v4|If you do these things, reveal yourself to the world]]. John adds a painful note: [[v5|For even his brothers didn’t believe in him]]. These were Jesus' younger brothers, sons of Mary and Joseph, including James and Jude (Mark 6:3). Their advice sounds like that of people who wanted Jesus to become famous, without understanding His mission.

Jesus answered, [[v6|My time has not yet come, but your time is always ready]]. Throughout John, Jesus moves according to the Father's timing, which leads toward His "hour" of death and glorification. His brothers could go to the feast whenever they liked, because the world did not hate them. But the world hated Jesus, [[v7|because I testify about it, that its works are evil]].

There is great hope in this sad scene. After the resurrection, Jesus' brothers became believers (Acts 1:14). James became a leader of the church in Jerusalem and is traditionally regarded as the author of the letter of James, and Jude is traditionally regarded as the author of the letter of Jude.

### Teaching in the Temple (7:10–24)

After His brothers left, Jesus went up privately. The crowds were whispering about Him. Some said, [[v12|He is a good man]]. Others said, [[v12|Not so, but he leads the multitude astray]]. Yet people were afraid to speak openly.

In the middle of the feast, Jesus began teaching publicly in the temple. The leaders were amazed: [[v15|How does this man know letters, having never been educated?]] Jesus had not studied under a recognized rabbi. He answered that His teaching came from the One who sent Him, and that anyone willing to do God's will would recognize it. This is studied below.

Jesus then confronted them directly. Moses gave them the law, yet none of them kept it, since they were seeking to kill Him. The crowd, apparently unaware of the leaders' plans, replied, [[v20|You have a demon! Who seeks to kill you?]]

Jesus referred back to the healing at Bethesda (5:1–9), the [[v21|one work]] that had caused such controversy. He pointed out that they circumcised baby boys on the Sabbath when the eighth day fell on it, because the law required circumcision on the eighth day (Leviticus 12:3). If they allowed that work on the Sabbath, why were they angry that He made a man completely well on the Sabbath? He concluded with a call to right judgment, studied below.

### Is This the Christ? (7:25–36)

Some people of Jerusalem knew that the leaders wanted to kill Jesus, and they wondered why He was being allowed to speak openly. Could the rulers have concluded that He was the Christ? But they had an objection: [[v27|we know where this man comes from, but when the Christ comes, no one will know where he comes from]]. Some believed that the Messiah would appear suddenly from an unknown origin.

Jesus cried out that they did know where He came from in an earthly sense, but they did not know the One who sent Him. [[v29|I know him, because I am from him, and he sent me]]. His true origin was heavenly.

They tried to seize Him, [[v30|but no one laid a hand on him, because his hour had not yet come]]. Many in the crowd believed in Him, reasoning that the Messiah could hardly do more signs than He had done. The Pharisees and chief priests sent officers, the temple police, to arrest Him.

Jesus said that He would be with them only a little while longer and then return to the One who sent Him. [[v34|You will seek me and won’t find me. You can’t come where I am]]. His listeners misunderstood, wondering whether He planned to go to the Jews scattered among the Greeks. Ironically, the gospel would indeed go to the Greeks after His death and resurrection.

### The Great Invitation (7:37–39)

On the last and greatest day of the feast, after days of water ceremonies and prayers for rain, Jesus stood and cried out an invitation to the thirsty. This is studied below.

### Division and Unbelief (7:40–52)

The crowd divided. Some said, [[v40|This is truly the prophet]]. Others said, [[v41|This is the Christ]]. Others objected that the Messiah would not come from Galilee, since Scripture said He would be a descendant of David from Bethlehem (Micah 5:2). John's readers know the irony: Jesus was born in Bethlehem and descended from David, though He grew up in Nazareth.

The officers returned without Jesus. When the leaders asked why, they answered, [[v46|No man ever spoke like this man!]] The Pharisees scorned them and the crowd: [[v49|But this multitude that doesn’t know the law is cursed]]. Their contempt for ordinary people is striking.

Then Nicodemus, who had come to Jesus by night in John 3, spoke up: [[v51|Does our law judge a man unless it first hears from him personally and knows what he does?]] It was a cautious defense, appealing to fairness rather than declaring faith. The others mocked him: [[v52|Are you also from Galilee? Search and see that no prophet has arisen out of Galilee]]. In fact, the prophet Jonah came from Gath Hepher in Galilee (2 Kings 14:25), so their claim was not even accurate. Nicodemus's small step of courage would later grow; he would help bury Jesus' body (John 19:39).

## Important Verses Explained

### John 7:17 — Willing to Do God's Will

verse: John 7:17

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus was teaching in the temple courts during the Feast of Booths. The religious leaders were amazed at His knowledge because He had never studied under one of the recognized rabbis. In that culture, a teacher's authority came from the teachers he had learned from. They wondered where Jesus' learning came from.

Jesus answered, [[v16|My teaching is not mine, but his who sent me]]. He was not inventing new ideas or relying on human authorities. His teaching came directly from God.

Then He explained how a person could recognize this: [[v17|If anyone desires to do his will, he will know about the teaching, whether it is from God or if I am speaking from myself]]. The key is a willingness to do God's will. Jesus says that understanding His teaching does not depend first on education or intellectual ability, but on the attitude of the heart. Those who truly want to obey God will recognize that Jesus' words come from God.

This shows that knowing truth is not only a matter of the mind. A person who is unwilling to obey may find endless reasons to doubt. A person who sincerely wants to do God's will is open to recognizing His voice.

In the next verse, Jesus offers another test: [[v18|He who speaks from himself seeks his own glory, but he who seeks the glory of him who sent him is true]]. Jesus did not seek His own glory but the Father's. That is a mark of a true messenger.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals a deep connection between obedience and understanding. In Scripture, knowing God is never merely academic. Psalm 111:10 says that the fear of the Lord is the beginning of wisdom. A heart surrendered to God becomes able to perceive His truth. Resistance to God's will often lies behind resistance to His Word.

It also shows that faith is not blind. Jesus invites people to test His teaching. Those who seek to do God's will will come to know whether His teaching is from God. This is an invitation to honest seeking, not to a leap in the dark.

Finally, the verse highlights Jesus' humility and unity with the Father. Though He is the eternal Word, He says His teaching is from the Father who sent Him. Jesus' whole ministry was marked by seeking the Father's glory rather than His own.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages anyone who has questions about Jesus. Questions are not a barrier to faith when they come from a sincere heart. Jesus promises that those who truly desire to do God's will will come to know whether His teaching is from God. Honest seekers can approach His words with openness.

It also challenges believers to consider whether their struggles with Scripture are sometimes struggles of the will rather than the mind. When a teaching of Jesus is hard to accept, the difficulty may lie in unwillingness to obey. Surrender often opens the way to understanding.

Finally, the verse offers a test for any teacher or leader. Those who speak for God should seek His glory rather than their own. Believers can be wise in listening for teachers who point to Christ rather than to themselves.

### John 7:24 — Judge Righteous Judgment

verse: John 7:24

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus spoke these words to people who were angry with Him for healing a man on the Sabbath (the healing at the pool of Bethesda in John 5). He pointed out their inconsistency. The Law required boys to be circumcised on the eighth day after birth. When the eighth day fell on a Sabbath, they circumcised the boy anyway, because they understood that the command to circumcise took priority. They performed this work on the Sabbath without hesitation.

Jesus argued that if they could perform circumcision, which affected one part of the body, on the Sabbath, they should not be angry that He [[v23|made a man completely healthy on the Sabbath]]. Restoring a whole person fulfills the purpose of God's law.

Then He concluded, [[v24|Don’t judge according to appearance, but judge righteous judgment]]. They had looked at the outward appearance: a man carrying a mat, a healing done on the Sabbath. They had not looked deeper at what God was doing or at the purpose of the law. Their judgment was shallow and therefore wrong.

This verse is sometimes misunderstood as forbidding all judgment. But Jesus does not tell them to stop judging; He tells them to judge rightly. Right judgment looks beneath the surface, considers the truth, and is consistent and fair.

Jesus' words echo the Law itself, which commanded Israel's judges to judge fairly and not to show partiality (Deuteronomy 16:18–19).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reflects God's own way of seeing. When Samuel looked at Jesse's sons, God told him that people look at the outward appearance, [[1 Samuel 16:7|but the LORD looks at the heart]]. God's judgment is never superficial. Jesus calls people to share, as far as humans can, God's concern for truth beneath the surface.

The verse also shows the danger of religious rule-keeping that misses the heart of God's law. The leaders were so focused on external regulations that they could not recognize God's mercy at work. Right judgment requires understanding the purpose of God's commands, which is love for God and neighbor.

Finally, the verse invites people to judge Jesus Himself rightly. Many in the chapter judged Him by appearances: His Galilean background, His lack of formal training, His family. Right judgment looks at the evidence of His words, His works, and His character.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse challenges believers to resist quick, surface-level judgments about others. People are often judged by their appearance, background, accent, education, or social status. Jesus calls His followers to look deeper, to seek the truth, and to be fair.

It also calls believers to be consistent. The leaders accepted one kind of Sabbath work but condemned another that was even more merciful. Christians can apply standards to others that they do not apply to themselves. Righteous judgment is honest and consistent.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that discernment is still necessary. Jesus does not forbid all moral evaluation. Believers must distinguish truth from error and right from wrong. But they are called to do so carefully, humbly, and with God's purposes of mercy in view.

### John 7:37–39 — Rivers of Living Water

verse: John 7:37-39

#### Simple Explanation

These words were spoken [[v37|on the last and greatest day of the feast]] of Booths. Each morning of the festival, a priest led a procession to the Pool of Siloam, filled a golden pitcher with water, carried it back to the temple, and poured it out at the altar while the people sang. The ceremony remembered how God provided water from the rock in the wilderness and expressed prayers for rain for the coming year. Many also connected it with prophecies of water flowing from the temple (Ezekiel 47:1–12; Zechariah 14:8) and with Isaiah's words, [[Isaiah 12:3|Therefore with joy you will draw water out of the wells of salvation]].

On the climactic day, Jesus [[v37|stood and cried out]]. Teachers usually sat to teach, so His standing and crying out showed the urgency of His invitation: [[v37|If anyone is thirsty, let him come to me and drink!]] While the people celebrated water poured out in a ceremony, Jesus offered the reality the ceremony pointed to. He Himself is the source of living water.

He continued, [[v38|He who believes in me, as the Scripture has said, from within him will flow rivers of living water]]. No single Old Testament verse says exactly these words; Jesus seems to be summarizing the promises of passages like Isaiah 58:11, Ezekiel 47, and Zechariah 14:8. The Greek is also ambiguous about whose "within" is meant. Many read it as describing the believer, from whom the Spirit's life flows to others. Others read it as describing Christ, from whom the living water flows, like the water from the rock in the wilderness. Both readings are faithful to John's message, since the Spirit flows from Christ to believers and through them to others.

John explains, [[v39|But he said this about the Spirit, which those believing in him were to receive]]. The Spirit had not yet been given in this new way, [[v39|because Jesus wasn’t yet glorified]]. In John's Gospel, Jesus' glorification refers to His death, resurrection, and return to the Father. After that, the Spirit would be poured out on all believers, as happened at Pentecost (Acts 2).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This invitation reveals that Jesus is the fulfillment of everything the Feast of Booths celebrated. God gave Israel water in the wilderness; Jesus gives living water that satisfies the soul. The prophets promised a river flowing from God's temple to bring life; Jesus is the true temple (John 2:21), and from Him the life-giving Spirit flows.

It also shows the generosity of God's gift. Jesus does not speak of a trickle but of "rivers." The Holy Spirit's presence in believers is abundant, bringing life, renewal, and power. And the Spirit's life does not stop with the individual; it flows outward to bless others.

Finally, the passage connects the gift of the Spirit with Jesus' death and resurrection. The Spirit comes because Jesus was glorified through the cross. Every blessing believers receive from the Spirit flows from the finished work of Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Jesus' invitation is open to "anyone" who is thirsty. Many people today feel spiritually dry, weary, or empty. They may be surrounded by religious activity, as the crowd was at the feast, and still be thirsty. Jesus calls them to come to Him personally and drink.

This passage also encourages believers to depend on the Holy Spirit. The Christian life is not meant to be lived in human strength alone. The Spirit dwells in believers, renewing them, giving them joy, and enabling them to love and serve.

Finally, the image of rivers flowing outward reminds believers that God's gifts are meant to be shared. When the Spirit fills a person, His life overflows in love, kindness, encouragement, and witness. Believers are called to be channels of the living water to a thirsty world.

### John 7:46 — No Man Ever Spoke Like This Man

verse: John 7:46

#### Simple Explanation

Earlier in the feast, the chief priests and Pharisees had sent officers to arrest Jesus (7:32). These were the temple police, Levites responsible for keeping order in the temple. They were under the authority of the religious leaders and were expected to carry out orders.

When the officers returned empty-handed, the leaders demanded, [[v45|Why didn’t you bring him?]] Their answer was simple: [[v46|No man ever spoke like this man!]] They had gone to arrest Jesus, but as they listened to Him, they were so struck by His words that they could not bring themselves to seize Him.

This is a remarkable testimony. These officers were not disciples of Jesus. They had no reason to favor Him and every reason to obey their superiors. Yet His words had a power and authority they had never encountered.

The Pharisees responded with scorn, asking whether the officers had also been deceived and pointing out that none of the rulers believed in Him. They dismissed the crowds who followed Jesus as ignorant of the law. But the officers' testimony stood.

The other Gospels also record that people were astonished at Jesus' teaching, [[Mark 1:22|for he taught them as having authority, and not as the scribes]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The officers' words point to the unique authority of Jesus' teaching. Other teachers quoted earlier authorities; Jesus spoke on His own authority, saying, "Most certainly I tell you." His words carried the power of God because He is the Word made flesh (John 1:14). When He spoke, people encountered not just ideas but the presence of God.

The scene also shows how God can work in unexpected people. The religious experts rejected Jesus, while those sent to arrest Him were moved by His words. God's truth is not limited to the educated or powerful. It reaches those with ears to hear.

Finally, the leaders' response reveals the danger of pride. They assumed that because the rulers did not believe, Jesus could not be the Christ. Their trust in their own position kept them from considering the evidence.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites people to listen to Jesus' words for themselves. Many people form opinions about Jesus based on what others say. The officers' experience shows the power of actually hearing Him. Reading the Gospels with an open heart can bring people face to face with words unlike any other.

It also encourages believers to trust the power of Jesus' words when sharing their faith. They do not need clever arguments to convince others. Pointing people to what Jesus said and did allows His words to do their work.

Finally, the leaders' scorn for "this multitude" warns against spiritual pride. Believers can look down on those they consider less informed or less religious. Jesus' words reached ordinary people and officers alike. His people should honor all whom He came to reach.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

John 7 shows that Jesus divides opinion. Some called Him good, others a deceiver; some said prophet, others Christ; some wanted to arrest Him, while others could not bring themselves to touch Him. People today also hold many opinions about Jesus. The chapter invites readers to look past rumor and appearances and to judge rightly, considering His words, His works, and His character.

The chapter also offers hope for families. Jesus' own brothers did not believe in Him at first, yet later they became leaders in the church. Believers who grieve over family members who do not believe can take heart. God's timing is not ours, and the story is not yet finished.

At the center of the chapter is Jesus' great invitation: anyone who is thirsty can come to Him and drink. Religious celebrations, traditions, and ceremonies can point to God, but only Christ can satisfy the soul. Through His death and resurrection, He gives the Holy Spirit, who fills believers and flows out through them to others. Believers are invited to keep coming to Him and to live in dependence on the Spirit.

Finally, John 7 encourages small steps of courage. Nicodemus did not make a bold confession, but he spoke up for fairness when others were condemning Jesus. Later his faith would become public. Believers may feel timid, but God honors faithful steps, and He can grow small courage into bold love.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You stood in the temple and cried out for the thirsty to come to You and drink. I come to You now, thirsty and in need. Fill me with Your Holy Spirit, and let Your living water flow through me to others. Give me a heart that truly desires to do God's will, so that I may recognize Your voice and understand Your teaching. Keep me from judging by appearances, and teach me to see people and situations as You see them, with truth and mercy. Thank You that no one ever spoke like You. Let Your words have their full authority in my life. I pray for family members and friends who do not yet believe; work in their hearts as You did in the hearts of Your own brothers. Give me courage to speak up for what is right and to honor You when others reject You. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
