# John 5
theme: The Son Who Gives Life and the Witnesses Who Point to Him
main: At the pool of Bethesda, Jesus heals a man who had been sick for thirty-eight years, and because He does it on the Sabbath, the religious leaders begin to seek His death. Jesus responds with one of His most important speeches about His relationship with the Father: the Son does what the Father does, gives life, and holds authority to judge. He then names the witnesses that testify about Him, John the Baptist, His works, the Father, and the Scriptures, and warns that refusing Him is refusing the life that the Scriptures promise.

## Chapter Background and Story

John 5 marks a turning point in the Gospel. Until now, Jesus has met curiosity, misunderstanding, and growing faith. From this chapter on, open hostility begins. The healing at Bethesda leads directly to the first recorded plot to kill Jesus (5:16–18), and the long speech that follows explains why the conflict is so serious. The issue is not merely a broken Sabbath rule; it is who Jesus claims to be.

### The Pool of Bethesda (5:1–9)

Jesus went up to Jerusalem for [[v1|a feast of the Jews]]. John does not name the feast, and scholars have suggested Passover, Pentecost, Tabernacles, or another festival. By the sheep gate, north of the temple, was a pool called Bethesda, a name that may mean "house of mercy." It had five covered porches. Archaeologists have uncovered a large double pool in this area with porticoes around it and a colonnade between the two basins, which matches John's description well.

The porches were crowded with [[v3|those who were sick, blind, lame, or paralyzed]]. Verse 4, which explains that an angel stirred the water and the first person in was healed, appears in the World English Bible and in many later manuscripts but is absent from the earliest and most reliable ones. Many scholars believe it was added later as an explanation of the popular belief reflected in verse 7. Either way, the sick man's words show what people there believed: when the water moved, the first one into the pool would be healed.

One man had been sick for thirty-eight years, longer than most people in that crowd had been alive. Jesus saw him, knew how long he had suffered, and asked, [[v6|Do you want to be made well?]] This question is studied below. The man did not answer yes or no. He explained his helplessness: he had no one to put him into the pool, and someone always got there first. Jesus did not carry him to the water. He said, [[v8|Arise, take up your mat, and walk]]. At once the man was well, picked up his mat, and walked.

Then John adds the detail that drives the rest of the chapter: [[v9|Now that day was a Sabbath]].

### Conflict over the Sabbath (5:10–18)

Religious leaders saw the healed man carrying his mat and told him, [[v10|It is the Sabbath. It is not lawful for you to carry the mat]]. The Law of Moses commanded rest on the Sabbath, and the prophets had warned against carrying burdens on that day (Jeremiah 17:21–22). Over time, teachers had developed detailed rules defining what counted as work, and carrying an object from one place to another was on the list. The leaders were so focused on the rule that they showed no joy at all that a man crippled for thirty-eight years was walking.

The man passed the responsibility to his healer, but he did not know Jesus' name, because Jesus had slipped away into the crowd. Later Jesus found him in the temple and said, [[v14|Behold, you are made well. Sin no more, so that nothing worse happens to you]]. Jesus does not say that the man's illness was caused by a specific sin; elsewhere He rejects that assumption (John 9:2–3). But He warns that there is something worse than thirty-eight years of paralysis: facing God's judgment unrepentant. Physical healing was a gift; it was not the whole of what the man needed.

The man went and told the leaders that Jesus had healed him. We are not told why. It may have been innocent, or it may have been a way to protect himself. In contrast with the man born blind in John 9, who boldly defends Jesus, this man shows no clear sign of faith.

The leaders began to persecute Jesus. His answer was brief but explosive: [[v17|My Father is still working, so I am working, too]]. Jewish teachers agreed that God, in one sense, never stops working on the Sabbath: He continues to sustain the world, give life, and judge. Jesus claimed the same freedom for Himself, because the Father is His Father. John explains why this enraged them: He [[v18|called God his own Father, making himself equal with God]]. They understood exactly what He meant.

### The Son and the Father (5:19–30)

Jesus did not soften His claim. He explained it. He began, [[v19|Most certainly, I tell you, the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he sees the Father doing]]. This is not a confession of weakness. It describes a perfect unity of will and action. The Son never acts independently of the Father, and whatever the Father does, the Son does likewise. The Father loves the Son and shows Him everything, and He will show Him greater works than healing a lame man.

Jesus then names two works that belong to God alone: giving life and judging. [[v21|For as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, even so the Son also gives life to whom he desires]]. And the Father has given all judgment to the Son, [[v23|that all may honor the Son, even as they honor the Father]]. To dishonor the Son is to dishonor the Father who sent Him. There is no way to honor God while rejecting Jesus.

Verse 24, studied below, applies this to anyone who hears Jesus' word. Verses 25–29 speak of two "hours." The first is already present: [[v25|the hour comes, and now is, when the dead will hear the Son of God’s voice; and those who hear will live]]. This refers to people who are spiritually dead receiving new life when they hear and believe. The second hour is future: [[v28|the hour comes in which all who are in the tombs will hear his voice]], and they will come out, some to the resurrection of life and some to the resurrection of judgment. This echoes Daniel 12:2.

Some readers wonder whether verse 29, which mentions [[v29|those who have done good]] and [[v29|those who have done evil]], teaches salvation by works. In context, Jesus has just said that eternal life comes by hearing His word and believing (5:24). Throughout John, the "work" God requires is to believe in the One He sent (John 6:29). The life a person lives reveals whether they have truly received that life; good deeds are the fruit of faith, not its price.

Jesus closes this section by returning to His dependence on the Father: [[v30|I don’t seek my own will, but the will of my Father who sent me]].

### The Witnesses to Jesus (5:31–47)

In Jewish law, a matter had to be confirmed by two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15). Jesus acknowledges that if He were His only witness, His testimony would not be legally sufficient. So He calls His witnesses.

The first is John the Baptist. The leaders had sent messengers to question John (John 1:19), and he had testified to the truth. Jesus calls him [[v35|the burning and shining lamp]], in whose light the people were glad to rejoice for a while. But Jesus does not ultimately depend on human testimony; He mentions John for their sake, [[v34|that you may be saved]].

The second witness is Jesus' works: [[v36|the very works that I do, testify about me, that the Father has sent me]]. The healing at Bethesda was one of those works.

The third witness is the Father Himself. Yet the leaders had never heard His voice or seen His form, and they did not have His word living in them, because they did not believe the One He sent.

The fourth witness is the Scriptures, studied below. Jesus then exposes the root problem: [[v44|How can you believe, who receive glory from one another, and you don’t seek the glory that comes from the only God?]] Their concern for human approval kept them from receiving Him.

Finally, Jesus says that He will not need to accuse them before the Father. Moses, the one in whom they had placed their hope, would accuse them, [[v46|for he wrote about me]]. The Law itself pointed to Christ, in its promises, its sacrifices, and its promise of a prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15). To reject Jesus was, in the end, to disbelieve Moses.

## Important Verses Explained

### John 5:6 — Do You Want to Be Made Well?

verse: John 5:6

#### Simple Explanation

The scene is a crowded pool in Jerusalem, surrounded by porches full of people who were blind, lame, and paralyzed. They waited there day after day, hoping the water would move and they could be first into it. Among them lay a man who had been sick for thirty-eight years. John tells us that Jesus [[v6|saw him lying there]] and [[v6|knew that he had been sick for a long time]]. Out of the whole crowd, Jesus went to him.

Jesus' question may seem strange at first: [[v6|Do you want to be made well?]] Surely anyone who had been sick that long would want healing. But the question is gentle and searching. After thirty-eight years, a person's whole life is shaped around an illness. Hope can fade. The man may have stopped expecting anything to change. Healing would also mean a new life with new responsibilities: work, relationships, and a place in the community after decades of dependence.

The man's answer shows where his hope had settled. He did not say yes. He explained why healing was impossible for him: [[v7|Sir, I have no one to put me into the pool when the water is stirred up, but while I’m coming, another steps down before me]]. He was thinking only of the pool and of his lack of help. He did not yet realize that the One standing in front of him did not need the pool at all.

Jesus did not argue with him or wait for a better answer. He simply spoke: [[v8|Arise, take up your mat, and walk]]. The power to heal came from Jesus' word, not from the water or from the man's ability to reach it. What the man could not do in thirty-eight years, Jesus did in a moment.

This healing also shows that Jesus' grace does not depend on a person already having strong faith. The man did not know Jesus' name (5:13). Jesus took the initiative, asked the question, and gave the healing.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Jesus' question reveals His respect for the person in front of Him. He does not treat the man as a project or an object of pity. He speaks to him as someone whose desire matters. God's grace is never forced upon people as if they were machines; He engages the heart, inviting a response even while He alone supplies the power.

The scene also shows the difference between human systems of hope and the grace of Christ. At the pool, healing (as people understood it) went to the fastest and strongest, to those with someone to help them. The weakest and loneliest were always left behind. Jesus reverses this. He goes to the one who has no one. The gospel is good news especially for those who cannot get themselves to the water.

Many interpreters also see a picture of spiritual helplessness here. Before Christ, human beings are not merely sick but, as Paul says, [[Romans 5:6|weak]], unable to save themselves. Rescue comes not because we finally manage to reach the right place, but because the Son of God comes to us and speaks life.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Jesus' question still speaks to people who have lived with something for a very long time: an illness, an addiction, a broken relationship, a habit of fear or bitterness. After years, it is possible to stop hoping, or to grow so used to a situation that change feels threatening. Jesus' question invites honesty. He meets people in their long-standing need and asks whether they are willing to receive what He offers, even when it means a new way of living.

The man's answer also reflects a common pattern: explaining why things cannot change instead of looking to the One who can change them. Many people rehearse the reasons they are stuck: no one helps, others always get ahead, the right moment has passed. Jesus does not depend on the conditions people think are necessary. He can work where every human option has failed.

This passage also gives comfort to those who feel overlooked. In a crowd of hurting people, Jesus noticed one man who had been waiting longer than almost anyone. He sees those whom others step over, and His compassion moves toward the forgotten. His people are called to do the same, noticing those who have no one to help them and bringing them the care of Christ.

### John 5:17 — My Father Is Still Working

verse: John 5:17

#### Simple Explanation

After Jesus healed the man at the pool, the religious leaders confronted the man for carrying his mat on the Sabbath. When they learned that Jesus had healed him and told him to carry it, they began to persecute Jesus, [[v16|because he did these things on the Sabbath]].

Jesus' defense is short: [[v17|My Father is still working, so I am working, too]]. The Sabbath commemorated God resting from His work of creation (Genesis 2:2–3). Yet Jewish teachers recognized that God had not stopped all activity. Every Sabbath, babies are born, the sun rises, rain falls, and people die. God continues to sustain the universe, give life, and judge. If God stopped working, creation would collapse.

Jesus applies this to Himself. His Father works on the Sabbath, and so He works too. Healing the lame man was not a violation of the Sabbath's purpose but a work of the same God who never ceases to care for His creation.

Notice the phrase [[v17|My Father]]. Jesus did not say "our Father," as His disciples would later pray. He spoke of God as His own Father in a unique way, and He claimed the right to do what God does.

The leaders understood immediately. John writes that they sought all the more to kill Him, because He [[v18|called God his own Father, making himself equal with God]]. They did not misunderstand Him. Jesus never corrected them by saying, "You have taken Me wrongly." Instead, He went on to explain how the Son and the Father work together.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is one of the clearest early claims to deity in John's Gospel. Jesus does not merely claim to be a prophet who heals by God's power. He claims to share in God's own ongoing work, and to stand in a relationship with the Father that gives Him the same authority over the Sabbath that God has. Jesus is not a rival to God or a second god; He is the Son who acts in perfect unity with the Father, as the following verses explain.

The verse also reveals the heart of the Sabbath. God gave the Sabbath as a gift of rest and a sign of His covenant, not as a burden that prevents mercy. Jesus says elsewhere that the Sabbath was made for people, not people for the Sabbath (Mark 2:27). On the Sabbath, God continues to do good, and so Jesus does good.

Finally, the verse shows that God is never idle in the world. Even when He seems silent, the Father is still at work, holding all things together and carrying out His purposes. Jesus' ministry was the visible expression of the Father's constant work of mercy.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse offers comfort to people who wonder whether God is doing anything in their situation. Jesus says that His Father is always working. Even when believers cannot see what He is doing, God has not stopped caring, sustaining, and working out His purposes. Times that seem quiet are not times when God is absent.

It also challenges a religion that values rules above people. The leaders cared more about a mat being carried than about a man being healed. Christians can fall into the same pattern, valuing traditions, preferences, or appearances more than the restoration of people. Jesus' example calls His followers to keep mercy at the center of their faith.

Finally, the verse calls everyone to face Jesus' claim. He did not present Himself as one good teacher among many. He spoke of God as His own Father and claimed to do God's work. A person must decide whether that claim is true. If it is, Jesus deserves the same honor as the Father.

### John 5:24 — Passed from Death into Life

verse: John 5:24

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus spoke these words in the middle of a long answer to religious leaders who wanted to kill Him. He had just explained that the Father gives the Son the authority to give life and to judge. Now He tells how a person receives that life.

[[v24|he who hears my word and believes him who sent me]]. Two actions are named: hearing and believing. "Hearing" means more than letting sounds enter the ears. It means listening and receiving what Jesus says. "Believing him who sent me" means trusting the Father who sent Jesus, which in John's Gospel is inseparable from trusting Jesus Himself. To believe the Father is to accept His testimony about His Son.

Jesus then gives three promises. First, such a person [[v24|has eternal life]]. The verb is present tense. Eternal life is not only something to hope for after death; it is a present possession. In John, eternal life is the life of God Himself, knowing the Father and the Son (John 17:3).

Second, the believer [[v24|doesn’t come into judgment]]. Jesus has just said that the Father has given all judgment to the Son. The One who will judge the world promises that those who believe will not face condemnation. This does not mean believers will never give an account of their lives (Romans 14:12), but that they will not be condemned.

Third, the believer [[v24|has passed out of death into life]]. This too is described as already done. Before believing, a person is spiritually dead, separated from God. Through faith, that person crosses over, like someone moving from one country to another, from the realm of death into the realm of life.

Jesus begins this statement with [[v24|Most certainly I tell you]], a solemn formula He uses to emphasize something of the greatest importance.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is one of the clearest statements in the Bible of the assurance that belongs to those who trust in Christ. Salvation is not a reward for earning enough merit. It is received through hearing and believing. And it is not uncertain until the very end; Jesus speaks of life as already given, judgment as already removed, and the passage from death to life as already accomplished.

The verse also shows that eternal life is relational. It comes through hearing Jesus' word and trusting the Father who sent Him. Life flows from being joined to the One who has life in Himself (5:26). Just as the dead man Lazarus would later come out of the tomb at Jesus' voice (John 11:43–44), spiritually dead people come to life when they hear His word.

Paul expresses the same truth when he writes, [[Romans 8:1|There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus]]. Jesus' words in John 5:24 lie behind the confident hope of believers throughout history.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse gives believers confidence. Many Christians live with constant fear that they have not done enough to be accepted by God. Jesus says that those who hear His word and believe have eternal life now. Their security rests on His promise, not on the strength of their feelings on a particular day.

It also invites those who have not yet believed. The way to life is open: hear what Jesus says and trust the One who sent Him. There is no long list of achievements to complete first. The crossing from death to life happens through faith.

Finally, the verse shapes how believers live. Those who have passed from death into life no longer belong to the old realm. Eternal life is meant to be lived now, in fellowship with God, in growing obedience, and in love for others. The life Jesus gives begins today and continues forever.

### John 5:39–40 — The Scriptures Testify About Jesus

verse: John 5:39-40

#### Simple Explanation

Near the end of His speech, Jesus spoke to religious leaders who knew the Scriptures extremely well. Many of them had memorized large portions of the Law and studied it carefully every day. Jesus acknowledged this: [[v39|You search the Scriptures, because you think that in them you have eternal life]]. They believed that diligent study of the Law was the path to life in the age to come.

Then He said something startling: [[v39|and these are they which testify about me]]. The Scriptures they studied so carefully were pointing to Jesus. The promises to Abraham, the Passover lamb, the prophet like Moses, the king from David's line, the suffering servant in Isaiah, all of them found their fulfillment in Him.

Yet the tragedy was this: [[v40|Yet you will not come to me, that you may have life]]. They held the map in their hands but refused to go to the destination. They studied the testimony while rejecting the One to whom it testified.

The problem was not lack of knowledge. It was unwillingness. The word "will not" describes a choice. Jesus explains part of the reason a few verses later: they were seeking glory from one another rather than the glory that comes from God (5:44).

The leaders were not wrong to search the Scriptures. Jesus never criticizes careful study of God's Word. What He exposes is study that stops short of coming to Him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses teach that Christ is the center of the whole Bible. After His resurrection, Jesus showed His disciples, beginning [[Luke 24:27|from Moses and from all the prophets]], the things written about Himself. The Old Testament is not a collection of unrelated stories and laws; it is the unfolding of God's plan that leads to Christ. Reading Scripture rightly means reading it with Him in view.

The verses also show that the Scriptures are not an end in themselves. They are God's inspired Word and deserve deep reverence, but life is not found in the pages as such. Life is found in the Person to whom the pages point. Scripture's purpose is to bring people to Christ.

Finally, the verses reveal that knowledge without surrender can become a barrier. It is possible to know much about the Bible and still refuse to come to the One it reveals. True understanding of Scripture leads to faith, humility, and worship.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to read the whole Bible with Jesus at the center. When reading the Old Testament stories, laws, psalms, and prophecies, believers can ask how they prepare for, point to, or are fulfilled in Christ. This makes the Bible a unified story rather than a collection of disconnected parts.

They also caution against Bible knowledge that does not lead to relationship. Studying Scripture can become an academic exercise, a source of pride, or a way to win arguments. The purpose of reading is to come to Jesus, to know Him, trust Him, and follow Him.

Finally, these verses speak to anyone who has heard about Jesus for years without coming to Him. Familiarity with religious things is not the same as life. Jesus' invitation remains: come to Him and have life.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

John 5 shows that Jesus notices the people others overlook. At the crowded pool, He went to a man who had waited thirty-eight years with no one to help him. Many people today live with long-standing suffering and quiet despair. Jesus sees them, knows how long they have been there, and comes to them with power that does not depend on their strength. His followers are called to share that compassion, paying attention to those who have no one.

The chapter also warns against a faith that cares more about rules than about people. The religious leaders saw a mat being carried and missed a miracle. Believers today can become so focused on doing religion correctly that they fail to rejoice when God restores someone. Mercy, not mere rule-keeping, reflects the heart of the Father who is always working.

At the center of the chapter stands Jesus' claim to be the Son who shares the Father's work, gives life, and judges the world. This means that no one can honor God while dismissing Jesus. It also means that those who hear His word and believe can live with deep assurance. They have eternal life now, will not be condemned, and have already crossed from death into life.

Finally, John 5 calls readers to let all the evidence lead them to Jesus. John the Baptist, Jesus' works, the Father, and the Scriptures all testify about Him. It is possible to know the Bible well and still refuse to come to Christ, often because of concern for the approval of others. The chapter invites a humble response: seeking God's approval more than people's, reading Scripture to find Christ, and coming to Him to receive life.

## Chapter Prayer

Heavenly Father, thank You that You are always working, even when I cannot see what You are doing. Thank You for sending Your Son, who saw the man no one helped and spoke a word that made him well. Lord Jesus, You know the places in my life where I have waited a long time and stopped hoping. Help me to bring them honestly to You and to trust that Your power does not depend on my strength. Thank You that whoever hears Your word and believes the Father who sent You has eternal life and has passed from death into life. Let that promise give me peace and confidence. Keep me from caring more about rules or appearances than about people, and from seeking the praise of others instead of Your approval. Open my eyes to see You on every page of Scripture, and draw me to come to You for life. Give me Your compassion for those who are overlooked and alone. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
