# Jude 1
theme: Contend for the Faith and Keep Yourselves in God's Love
main: Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ and brother of James, writes to those who are called, loved by God the Father, and kept for Jesus Christ. He had wanted to write about the salvation they share, but he felt compelled instead to urge them to contend for the faith that was once for all entrusted to God's people. Certain ungodly people have slipped in secretly; they pervert the grace of God into a license for immorality and deny Jesus Christ, our only Master and Lord. Jude reminds his readers of God's judgment in the past: on Israelites in the wilderness who did not believe, on angels who abandoned their proper place, and on Sodom and Gomorrah. The intruders reject authority, slander what they do not understand, and follow the way of Cain, the error of Balaam, and the rebellion of Korah. They are like hidden reefs, waterless clouds, fruitless trees, wild waves, and wandering stars. Enoch prophesied judgment on such people, and the apostles foretold that scoffers would come in the last time. But Jude urges the faithful to build themselves up in their most holy faith, pray in the Holy Spirit, keep themselves in God's love, and wait for the mercy of Jesus Christ that leads to eternal life. They should show mercy to those who doubt and rescue others from the fire. The letter ends in praise to the God who is able to keep them from stumbling and to present them blameless before His glory with great joy.

## Chapter Background and Story

Jude is a short letter with an urgent tone. It reads like a warning sounded by a watchman. Its language is vivid and strong, full of images from nature and stories from the Old Testament and Jewish tradition. Yet beneath its warnings lies deep pastoral care. Jude begins and ends by assuring believers that they are loved and kept by God.

The intruders Jude describes were not outsiders attacking the church from without. They had slipped in among the believers and were taking part in the church's fellowship meals. They apparently taught that God's grace freed them to live however they pleased, especially in sexual matters. They rejected spiritual authority, claimed special insight through dreams, and caused divisions. Jude does not engage their arguments in detail. Instead, he shows from Scripture that such behavior has always come under God's judgment, and he strengthens the faithful to stand firm.

### Greeting (vv1–2)

[[v1|Jude, a servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James, to those who are called, sanctified by God the Father, and kept for Jesus Christ:]] Jude describes believers in three beautiful ways. They are "called," invited by God into relationship with Him. They are "sanctified by God the Father"; many manuscripts read beloved in God the Father instead, and both ideas express God's tender care. And they are "kept for Jesus Christ," protected and preserved until the day they meet Him. Before Jude says anything about danger, he reminds believers of their security in God. This theme of being "kept" returns at the end of the letter (vv21, 24).

[[v2|May mercy, peace, and love be multiplied to you]]. Jude prays not merely that believers will have these gifts, but that they will be multiplied, poured out in abundance.

### The Call to Contend and the Danger Within (vv3–4)

### Examples of Judgment from the Past (vv5–7)

[[v5|Now I desire to remind you, though you already know this, that the Lord, having saved a people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who didn’t believe]]. Jude's first example is Israel in the wilderness. God rescued His people from Egypt, but many who experienced that rescue later refused to trust Him and died in the desert (Numbers 14:26–35). Being part of God's people outwardly is not the same as genuine, persevering faith.

[[v6|Angels who didn’t keep their first domain, but deserted their own dwelling place, he has kept in everlasting bonds under darkness for the judgment of the great day]]. The second example concerns angels who abandoned their proper place. Many interpreters connect this with Genesis 6:1–4, where [[Genesis 6:2|God’s sons]] took human wives, a passage that ancient Jewish writings, such as the book of 1 Enoch, interpreted as angels sinning by crossing the boundary God had set. Others understand Jude to refer more generally to the original rebellion of angels against God. Either way, the point is clear: even angels who rejected God's order face judgment. Notice the wordplay: those who did not "keep" their position are now "kept" in chains for judgment.

[[v7|Even as Sodom and Gomorrah and the cities around them, having in the same way as these given themselves over to sexual immorality and gone after strange flesh, are shown as an example, suffering the punishment of eternal fire]]. The third example is Sodom and Gomorrah, cities destroyed for their wickedness (Genesis 19). Scripture describes their sins in several ways, including arrogance, neglect of the poor, and sexual immorality (Ezekiel 16:49–50). Jude highlights their sexual immorality and their pursuit of "strange flesh." Interpreters understand this phrase in different ways: some see a reference to the men of Sodom seeking sexual relations with angelic visitors, matching the example of angels crossing boundaries in verse 6, while others see a broader reference to sexual sin outside God's design. The common thread in all three examples is rebellion against the boundaries God has set, followed by judgment.

### The Character of the Intruders (vv8–16)

[[v8|Yet in the same way, these also in their dreaming defile the flesh, despise authority, and slander celestial beings]]. The intruders claimed revelations through dreams to justify their behavior. They indulged in immorality, rejected the authority of the Lord, and spoke contemptuously about spiritual beings.

[[v9|But Michael, the archangel, when contending with the devil and arguing about the body of Moses, dared not bring against him an abusive condemnation, but said, “May the Lord rebuke you!”]] This account does not appear in the Old Testament. According to early church writers, it came from a Jewish work known as the Assumption of Moses or Testament of Moses. Jude uses a story familiar to his readers to make a point: even Michael, a mighty archangel, did not presume to pronounce judgment on the devil in his own authority, but left judgment to the Lord. The intruders, by contrast, arrogantly slandered spiritual realities. Jude's use of such traditions does not mean he regarded these books as Scripture; Paul also cited non-biblical sources when they illustrated truth (Acts 17:28).

[[v10|But these speak evil of whatever things they don’t know. They are destroyed in these things that they understand naturally, like the creatures without reason]]. The intruders mocked what they did not understand, and they were ruled by their instincts like animals, which led them to ruin.

[[v11|Woe to them! For they went in the way of Cain, and ran riotously in the error of Balaam for hire, and perished in Korah’s rebellion]]. Jude points to three Old Testament figures. Cain murdered his brother out of envy and rejected God's correction (Genesis 4). Balaam was willing to lead Israel into sin for money (Numbers 22–25; 31:16). Korah rebelled against the leadership God had appointed through Moses and was destroyed (Numbers 16). The intruders show the same jealousy, greed, and rebellion. Jude speaks of Korah's rebellion as though the intruders have already "perished" in it, so certain is their judgment.

[[v12|These are hidden rocky reefs in your love feasts when they feast with you, shepherds who without fear feed themselves; clouds without water, carried along by winds; autumn trees without fruit, twice dead, plucked up by the roots;]] [[v13|wild waves of the sea, foaming out their own shame; wandering stars, for whom the blackness of darkness has been reserved forever]]. Jude paints five vivid pictures. The intruders are like hidden reefs that can wreck a ship, endangering the church's fellowship meals, the "love feasts." They are like shepherds who feed only themselves. They are like clouds that promise rain but bring none, trees that bear no fruit even at harvest time, waves that churn up only foam and filth, and stars that wander from their course and vanish into darkness. Each image shows something that looks promising but is empty or dangerous.

[[v14|About these also Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, saying, “Behold, the Lord came with ten thousands of his holy ones,]] [[v15|to execute judgment on all, and to convict all the ungodly of all their works of ungodliness which they have done in an ungodly way, and of all the hard things which ungodly sinners have spoken against him.”]] Jude quotes a prophecy found in the book of 1 Enoch (1:9), a Jewish writing well known in his day. Enoch himself is the man who [[Genesis 5:24|walked with God]]. By quoting this passage, Jude affirms the truth of its message: the Lord will come with His holy ones to judge the ungodly. Quoting a writing does not necessarily mean treating the whole work as Scripture; Jude draws on a familiar text whose message agreed with what Scripture teaches about the Lord's coming judgment.

[[v16|These are murmurers and complainers, walking after their lusts—and their mouth speaks proud things—showing respect of persons to gain advantage]]. The intruders grumbled, followed their desires, boasted, and flattered people when it served their interests.

### Remember and Persevere (vv17–23)

[[v17|But you, beloved, remember the words which have been spoken before by the apostles of our Lord Jesus Christ.]] [[v18|They said to you, “In the last time there will be mockers, walking after their own ungodly lusts.”]] Jude turns to the faithful with "But you, beloved." The appearance of such people should not shake their faith. The apostles had warned that scoffers would come (Acts 20:29–30; 2 Timothy 3:1–5; 2 Peter 3:3).

[[v19|These are those who cause divisions and are sensual, not having the Spirit]]. The intruders may have claimed to be spiritual, but Jude says they do not have the Spirit at all. Their divisiveness and their focus on natural desires show it.

The call to keep oneself in God's love and to show mercy are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Jude 1:3–4 — Contend for the Faith

verse: Jude 1:3-4

#### Simple Explanation

Jude tells his readers that he had planned a different letter: "while I was very eager to write to you about our common salvation." He wanted to celebrate the salvation all believers share. But circumstances changed: "I was constrained to write to you exhorting you to contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints."

"Contend earnestly" translates a word that pictures an athlete straining in a contest or a soldier fighting a battle. Jude calls believers to make a determined effort to defend and preserve the faith.

"The faith" here means the body of truth about Jesus Christ that God has revealed. It was "once for all delivered," meaning it was given completely and does not need to be replaced or updated. "The saints" are all God's people.

Verse 4 explains why this is urgent: "For there are certain men who crept in secretly." These people had entered the church unnoticed. They were "turning the grace of our God into indecency," using God's grace as an excuse for immoral living, and "denying our only Master, God, and Lord, Jesus Christ," rejecting His authority over their lives.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses teach that the Christian faith has content. It is not merely a feeling or a personal preference, but a revealed message about who God is and what He has done in Jesus Christ. This message was entrusted to the church through the apostles and is preserved in Scripture. Believers are its stewards, not its inventors.

The danger Jude describes is subtle. The intruders did not openly deny grace; they twisted it. They took the wonderful truth that God forgives freely and turned it into permission to sin. Paul faced the same distortion: [[Romans 6:1|What shall we say then? Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound?]] His answer, and Jude's, is a firm no. True grace does not leave people in sin; it trains them to say no to ungodliness (Titus 2:11–12).

Jude also shows that denying Christ can be done not only with words but with life. To reject His lordship by living in defiance of Him is a practical denial of who He is. Faith and obedience belong together.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Contending for the faith does not mean being harsh, argumentative, or unkind. Jude himself, as the letter goes on, calls for mercy toward those who doubt. Contending means holding firmly to the truth of the gospel, knowing it well, living it faithfully, and refusing to let it be quietly changed into something else.

Believers today can contend for the faith by studying Scripture carefully, being part of churches that teach it faithfully, and gently correcting distortions when they appear, especially distortions that make grace an excuse for sin.

This passage also invites personal reflection. Believers can examine whether they are treating God's grace as a gift that leads to holiness, or as a reason to excuse what God calls wrong. Grace is free, but it is never cheap.

### Jude 1:20–21 — Keep Yourselves in God's Love

verse: Jude 1:20-21

#### Simple Explanation

After describing the intruders, Jude turns to the faithful: "But you, beloved." He gives them four instructions for staying strong.

First, "keep building up yourselves on your most holy faith." The faith is like a foundation, and believers are to keep building their lives upon it through learning, worship, and obedience.

Second, they are to keep "praying in the Holy Spirit." This means praying with the help and guidance of the Spirit, depending on Him rather than on mere human effort (Romans 8:26; Ephesians 6:18).

Third, in verse 21: "Keep yourselves in God’s love." Believers cannot make God love them; He already does. But they can remain in the place where His love is enjoyed, staying close to Him and walking in His ways.

Fourth, they are to keep "looking for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ to eternal life." They wait with hope for the return of Christ, when His mercy will bring them fully into eternal life.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses balance the beginning of the letter. In verse 1, Jude said that believers are "kept for Jesus Christ" by God. Now he says, "Keep yourselves in God’s love." Both are true. God keeps His people, and His people respond by keeping themselves in His love. God's protection does not make human effort unnecessary; it makes it possible. Jesus said something similar: [[John 15:9|Even as the Father has loved me, I also have loved you. Remain in my love]].

The four instructions also reveal the Trinity at work in the Christian life. Believers pray in the Holy Spirit, keep themselves in the love of God the Father, and wait for the mercy of the Lord Jesus Christ. The whole life of faith is lived in relationship with the triune God.

Notice that Jude's answer to the danger of false teaching is not anxious suspicion but positive spiritual growth. The best defense against error is a strong and growing faith, rooted in truth, sustained by prayer, and secure in God's love.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can build themselves up in the faith by regularly reading and studying Scripture, learning sound teaching, worshiping with other believers, and putting what they learn into practice.

They can pray in the Holy Spirit by asking for His help, listening for His guidance, and depending on Him when they do not know what to pray.

Keeping oneself in God's love means staying close to Him through daily trust and obedience, and turning away from anything that draws the heart away from Him. When believers drift, they can return quickly, knowing that God's love has not changed.

Looking for Christ's mercy gives believers hope. Whatever difficulties they face now, they can live with expectation, knowing that Jesus will return and bring them into the fullness of eternal life.

### Jude 1:22–23 — Mercy for Those Who Waver

verse: Jude 1:22-23

#### Simple Explanation

Jude now tells believers how to care for others affected by the false teaching. "On some have compassion, making a distinction." Some people in the church were doubting or wavering. They needed mercy and patient help, not harsh condemnation.

Verse 23 continues: "and some save, snatching them out of the fire with fear." Others were in greater danger, closer to being drawn into destructive sin. Believers are to act urgently, like someone pulling a person from a burning building.

Jude adds a caution: "hating even the clothing stained by the flesh." Those who help others must be careful not to be drawn into sin themselves. They are to love the person while hating the sin that defiles.

The Greek manuscripts of these verses vary, and some translations describe two groups of people while others describe three. But the main teaching is the same: believers are to respond to those affected by error with discerning mercy, urgent rescue, and careful holiness.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

After strong words about judgment, Jude calls for mercy. This reflects the heart of God, who is rich in mercy and does not want anyone to perish (2 Peter 3:9). Those who receive mercy are called to show mercy. Believers who have just been told to wait for "the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ" (v21) are now to extend that mercy to others.

The phrase "making a distinction" shows that wisdom is needed. Not every situation is the same. Some people need gentle patience as they work through honest doubts. Others need urgent intervention. Love takes the time to understand where each person is.

The warning to hate "the clothing stained by the flesh" reflects the reality that sin can be contagious. Paul gave a similar caution when he urged believers to restore those caught in sin gently, while watching themselves so that they would not also be tempted (Galatians 6:1). Mercy and holiness go together.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers today will meet people who are wrestling with doubt, confused by false ideas, or drifting into harmful choices. This passage calls them to respond with compassion. Doubt is not the same as unbelief, and many who struggle with questions need patient friends who will walk with them.

At the same time, there are moments that call for urgency, when someone is in serious spiritual or moral danger. Love does not stay silent when a person is heading toward destruction. It speaks and acts with courage.

Those who help others should also guard their own hearts, seeking accountability and staying close to God. Helping someone out of the fire does not mean stepping into it.

### Jude 1:24–25 — Able to Keep You from Stumbling

verse: Jude 1:24-25

#### Simple Explanation

Jude ends his letter with a hymn of praise. After warning about danger, he lifts believers' eyes to God: "Now to him who is able to keep them from stumbling." God is able to protect His people from falling away. Their security rests not on their own strength but on His power.

God is also able "to present you faultless before the presence of his glory in great joy." One day believers will stand before God, not ashamed, but blameless, because of what Christ has done. And this will be an occasion of "great joy," both for them and for God.

In verse 25, Jude gives glory "to God our Savior, who alone is wise." All praise belongs to Him: "glory and majesty, dominion and power." And this praise extends across all time, "both now and forever."

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This doxology is one of the most comforting passages in Scripture. Jude has spoken of those who fell: Israelites in the wilderness, rebellious angels, the cities of the plain, Cain, Balaam, Korah, and the intruders. A reader might wonder whether they too could fall. Jude's answer is to point to God, who is able to keep His people from stumbling.

The letter thus ends where it began. In verse 1, believers are "kept for Jesus Christ." In verse 21, they are to keep themselves in God's love. And in verse 24, God is able to keep them from stumbling. God's keeping power surrounds the whole life of faith.

The picture of being presented "faultless" with "great joy" is rich with grace. Believers will not stand before God on the basis of their own record, but clothed in the righteousness of Christ (2 Corinthians 5:21). The joy of that day will be the joy of a Father welcoming His children home, and of a Savior presenting the people He redeemed.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers who feel weak or worry about falling away can take great comfort in this passage. Their perseverance does not finally depend on their own strength, but on the God who is able to keep them. They can bring their fears to Him and trust Him to hold them fast.

This doxology also invites worship. When believers face difficult times or troubling news, they can do what Jude did: turn their hearts to praise God for who He is and what He is able to do. Worship shifts the focus from fear to faith.

Finally, believers can live with hope, knowing that one day they will stand before God's glory, not in shame but in great joy. That future can shape how they live today, with confidence, gratitude, and a desire to honor Him.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Jude's letter calls believers to take the faith seriously. The message of the gospel has been entrusted once for all to God's people, and believers are called to contend for it, to hold it firmly, live it faithfully, and refuse to let it be twisted. Jude warns especially against turning God's grace into an excuse for sin. Grace is a free gift, but it leads to a transformed life.

The letter also reminds believers that God's judgment is real. The examples of Israel in the wilderness, the rebellious angels, and Sodom and Gomorrah show that God does not ignore rebellion. The images of empty clouds, fruitless trees, and wandering stars warn against a faith that looks impressive but has no substance. Believers can let these warnings lead them to examine their hearts and walk humbly with God.

Yet Jude's main concern is not to frighten but to strengthen. He calls the faithful to build themselves up in their most holy faith, pray in the Holy Spirit, keep themselves in God's love, and wait for the mercy of Jesus Christ. These practices keep believers rooted and growing, even when error surrounds them.

Jude also calls for mercy. Those who doubt need compassion. Those in danger need urgent help. Believers are to reach out with love, while guarding their own hearts from sin.

Above all, Jude points believers to the God who is able to keep them from stumbling and to present them faultless before His glory with great joy. Their security rests in Him. This truth gives courage to face any challenge and leads naturally to praise.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, thank You that I am called, loved by You, and kept for Jesus Christ. Give me courage to contend for the faith once for all delivered to Your people, and keep me from ever turning Your grace into an excuse for sin. Guard my heart from pride, rebellion, and empty religion. Help me to build my life on my most holy faith, to pray in the Holy Spirit, to keep myself in Your love, and to wait with hope for the mercy of my Lord Jesus Christ. Give me compassion for those who doubt and courage to help those in danger, while keeping my own heart pure. Thank You that You are able to keep me from stumbling and to present me faultless before Your glory with great joy. To You, the only wise God our Savior, be glory and majesty, dominion and power, now and forever. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
