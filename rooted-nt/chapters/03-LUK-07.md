# Luke 7
theme: Great Faith, Deep Compassion, and Much Love
main: Jesus heals the servant of a humble Gentile centurion, raises the only son of a grieving widow at Nain, and reassures the imprisoned John the Baptist by pointing to His works of mercy. At a Pharisee's dinner, a woman known as a sinner washes Jesus' feet with her tears; Jesus teaches through a parable that those forgiven much love much, declares her sins forgiven, and sends her away in peace.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 7 follows the Sermon on the Plain, in which Jesus taught about loving enemies, showing mercy, and building life on His words. This chapter shows Jesus living out that teaching. He responds to a Gentile soldier, a grieving widow, a doubting prophet, and a sinful woman. Each story reveals His compassion for people at the edges of society and His authority to heal, raise the dead, and forgive sins. The chapter also contrasts those who receive Jesus with humble faith and those who reject Him with proud self-righteousness. (The centurion's faith is studied at Matthew 8:10, and John's question at Matthew 11:3.)

### The Centurion's Servant (7:1–10)

After finishing His sermon, Jesus entered Capernaum. A centurion there had a servant who was [[v2|dear to him]] and was sick and near death. A centurion was a Roman officer in charge of about a hundred soldiers. In Galilee, he may have served Herod Antipas, but he was certainly a Gentile.

Luke's version includes details not found in Matthew. The centurion did not come to Jesus personally at first; he sent Jewish elders to ask Jesus to come and heal his servant. The elders urged Jesus to help, saying, [[v4|He is worthy for you to do this for him, for he loves our nation, and he built our synagogue for us]]. This Gentile officer had shown respect and generosity toward the Jewish people.

Jesus went with them. But before He reached the house, the centurion sent friends with a message: [[v6|Lord, don’t trouble yourself, for I am not worthy for you to come under my roof]]. The elders said he was "worthy," but the centurion said he was "not worthy." His humility was genuine. He explained that he understood authority. As an officer, he gave orders and they were carried out. He believed Jesus could simply say the word and his servant would be healed.

Jesus was amazed and said to the crowd, [[v9|I tell you, I have not found such great faith, no, not in Israel]]. When the messengers returned, they found the servant well.

### Raising the Widow's Son at Nain (7:11–17)

Soon afterward, Jesus went to a town called Nain, about six miles southeast of Nazareth. His disciples and a large crowd went with Him. As He approached the town gate, a funeral procession was coming out. The dead man was [[v12|the only born son of his mother, and she was a widow]]. A large crowd from the town was with her. This scene, found only in Luke, is studied below.

Jesus had compassion on her and said, [[v13|Don’t cry]]. He went up and touched the bier (an open coffin or stretcher used to carry the body). The bearers stood still. Jesus said, [[v14|Young man, I tell you, arise!]] The dead man sat up and began to speak, and Jesus gave him back to his mother.

Everyone was filled with awe and praised God, saying, [[v16|A great prophet has arisen among us!]] and [[v16|God has visited his people!]] The people may have remembered that the prophet Elijah raised the son of a widow in Zarephath (1 Kings 17:17–24), and Elisha raised the son of a woman in Shunem, a village just a few miles from Nain (2 Kings 4:32–37). Jesus was acting like the great prophets, but with even greater authority; He raised the young man with a simple command. News about Him spread throughout Judea and the surrounding region.

### John the Baptist's Question (7:18–35)

John's disciples told him about all these things. John was in prison (Luke 3:20). He sent two of his disciples to ask Jesus, [[v19|Are you the one who is coming, or should we look for another?]] Luke adds that at that very time Jesus cured many people of diseases, afflictions, and evil spirits, and gave sight to many who were blind. Then He told John's messengers to report what they had seen and heard: the blind see, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the poor have good news preached to them. These were the signs of the Messiah's age described in Isaiah (35:5–6; 61:1). He added, [[v23|Blessed is he who finds no occasion for stumbling in me]].

After John's messengers left, Jesus spoke to the crowds about John, calling him more than a prophet and the messenger promised in Malachi 3:1. No one born of women was greater than John, yet the least in God's kingdom is greater than he.

Luke adds a comment not found in Matthew: [[v29|When all the people and the tax collectors heard this, they declared God to be just, having been baptized with John’s baptism]]. Those who had responded to John's call to repentance acknowledged that God's way was right. [[v30|But the Pharisees and the lawyers rejected the counsel of God, not being baptized by him themselves]]. By refusing John's baptism, they rejected God's purpose for them.

Jesus compared His generation to children in the marketplace who complain that others will not play their games. John came fasting, and they said he had a demon. Jesus came eating and drinking, and they called Him a glutton and a drunkard, a friend of tax collectors and sinners. Whatever God sent, they rejected. But [[v35|Wisdom is justified by all her children]]; those who accept God's wisdom show that it is right.

### The Sinful Woman at the Pharisee's House (7:36–50)

One of the Pharisees, named Simon, invited Jesus to eat with him. Jesus went and reclined at the table. At formal meals, guests reclined on cushions or couches around a low table, leaning on one elbow with their feet stretched away from the table. Such meals were often semi-public; uninvited people could come in and watch.

A woman in that town [[v37|who was a sinner]] learned that Jesus was eating there. Luke does not say what her sin was, but she was known in the town for an immoral life. She brought an alabaster jar of perfume. Standing behind Jesus at His feet, weeping, she began to wet His feet with her tears and wipe them with her hair. She kissed His feet and poured perfume on them. Letting her hair down in public was considered improper for a woman, and her actions would have shocked the guests. (This event is different from the anointing at Bethany shortly before Jesus' death, recorded in Matthew 26, Mark 14, and John 12.)

Simon the Pharisee thought to himself, [[v39|This man, if he were a prophet, would have perceived who and what kind of woman this is who touches him, that she is a sinner]]. In Simon's view, a true prophet would know her reputation and would not allow her to touch Him.

Jesus knew Simon's thoughts and said, "Simon, I have something to tell you." He told a parable about two debtors. One owed a moneylender five hundred denarii, about a year and a half's wages. The other owed fifty denarii. Neither could pay, so the lender cancelled both debts. Jesus asked which would love him more. Simon answered, [[v43|He, I suppose, to whom he forgave the most]]. Jesus said he had judged correctly.

Then Jesus turned toward the woman and said to Simon, [[v44|Do you see this woman?]] This question is studied below. Jesus pointed out that Simon had neglected the customary courtesies of hospitality: he gave no water for Jesus' feet, no kiss of greeting, and no oil for His head. But the woman had washed His feet with her tears, kissed them continually, and anointed them with perfume.

Then Jesus said, [[v47|Therefore I tell you, her sins, which are many, are forgiven, for she loved much. But one to whom little is forgiven, loves little]]. This verse is studied below. Jesus told the woman, [[v48|Your sins are forgiven]]. The other guests began to say among themselves, [[v49|Who is this who even forgives sins?]] Jesus said to the woman, [[v50|Your faith has saved you. Go in peace]].

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 7:13 — Compassion for a Grieving Widow

verse: Luke 7:13

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is at the heart of the story of the widow of Nain, found only in Luke's Gospel. Jesus was approaching the gate of the town of Nain, accompanied by His disciples and a large crowd. At the same time, a funeral procession was coming out of the town. In Jewish custom, burial took place on the same day as death, usually outside the town. Mourners, often including hired flute players and wailing women, would follow the body.

The dead man was [[v12|the only born son of his mother, and she was a widow]]. Luke's description reveals the depth of this woman's tragedy. She had already lost her husband. Now she had lost her only son. In that society, a widow without a son had no one to provide for her or protect her. She faced not only grief but also poverty and vulnerability. A large crowd from the town was with her, showing community sympathy, but no one could change her situation.

Then Luke writes, [[v13|When the Lord saw her, he had compassion on her and said to her, “Don’t cry.”]] Several details are significant.

Luke calls Jesus "the Lord" here, the first time he uses this title by itself in his own narration. It emphasizes Jesus' divine authority, which He is about to demonstrate.

[[v13|saw her]]. Jesus noticed her. In the large crowd, His attention was on the grieving mother.

[[v13|he had compassion on her]]. The Greek word means a deep, gut-level feeling of pity and love. No one asked Jesus to help. The widow did not request a miracle. Jesus acted entirely out of His own compassion.

[[v13|Don’t cry]]. These words could sound harsh if spoken by anyone else. But Jesus said them because He was about to remove the cause of her tears.

Jesus then touched the bier, which would normally make a person ritually unclean, and commanded the young man to rise. The man sat up and began to speak, and Jesus [[v15|gave him to his mother]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the compassionate heart of Jesus. He sees human suffering and is moved by it. His miracles were not merely demonstrations of power; they flowed from love. This compassion reflects the character of God, who is described as the defender of widows (Psalm 68:5).

The story also shows Jesus' authority over death. He did not pray for the young man to be raised, as Elijah and Elisha did; He commanded him to rise. This anticipates Jesus' own resurrection and His promise to raise all who belong to Him. Death is not the final word when Jesus is present.

Finally, the phrase "gave him to his mother" shows that Jesus restored not only life but also relationship and security. The widow's future was restored along with her son. Jesus cares about the whole person and their circumstances.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse brings comfort to those who are grieving. Jesus sees their sorrow and has compassion on them. While He may not reverse every loss in this life, He promises that death will not have the final word. Those who belong to Him will be raised, and He will wipe away every tear (Revelation 21:4).

It also invites believers to notice those who are suffering. Jesus saw the widow in the crowd. Believers can pay attention to people who are grieving, lonely, or vulnerable, especially widows, the elderly, and those who have lost family support, and respond with compassion and practical help. James writes that pure religion includes caring for widows in their affliction (James 1:27).

Finally, this verse reminds believers that Jesus acts out of compassion even when people do not ask. God's grace often comes before we seek it. Believers can trust that Jesus cares about them even when they are too overwhelmed to pray.

### Luke 7:44 — "Do You See This Woman?"

verse: Luke 7:44

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes in the middle of a tense dinner at the home of Simon, a Pharisee who had invited Jesus to eat with him. During the meal, a woman known in the town as a sinner came in and began weeping at Jesus' feet, wetting them with her tears, wiping them with her hair, kissing them, and anointing them with perfume. Simon was offended and thought that if Jesus were a prophet, He would know what kind of woman was touching Him.

Jesus responded by telling Simon a parable about two debtors, one forgiven a large debt and one a small debt, and asked which would love the lender more. Simon correctly answered that the one forgiven more would love more.

Then Jesus [[v44|Turning to the woman]], said to Simon, [[v44|Do you see this woman?]] Jesus physically turned toward her while speaking to Simon. This gesture directed Simon's attention to her as a person. Simon saw her only as "a sinner," a category that defined and dismissed her. Jesus asked him to truly see her.

Jesus continued, contrasting Simon's behavior with hers. [[v44|I entered into your house, and you gave me no water for my feet]]. In that culture, a host would normally provide water for guests to wash their feet after walking on dusty roads. Simon had not done this. [[v44|but she has wet my feet with her tears, and wiped them with the hair of her head]]. The woman had done far more than Simon, using her own tears and hair.

In the next verses, Jesus noted that Simon gave Him no kiss of greeting, but she had not stopped kissing His feet; Simon did not anoint His head with oil, but she had anointed His feet with perfume. Simon, the respectable host, had been cold and neglectful. The woman, the known sinner, had shown overflowing love.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus sees people as they truly are, beyond their reputations and labels. Simon saw a sinner; Jesus saw a woman who loved much because she had been forgiven much. God looks at the heart, not at outward appearances or social status (1 Samuel 16:7).

It also reveals the blindness of self-righteousness. Simon was religious, respectable, and careful about purity. But he failed to see his own lack of love and his own need for forgiveness. He judged the woman while neglecting basic hospitality to Jesus. Self-righteousness often makes people blind to their own faults and to the worth of others.

Finally, the verse shows Jesus' defense of the vulnerable. In front of the respected guests, Jesus honored this woman and rebuked the host. He took her side against the judgment of the religious community. Jesus consistently welcomed and defended those whom society rejected.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to see people as Jesus sees them. It is easy to label people by their past, their reputation, or their appearance, and then dismiss them. Jesus calls His followers to look beyond labels and see each person as someone made in God's image, capable of love and repentance.

It also challenges believers to examine their own hearts for self-righteousness. Simon was more concerned with the woman's sins than with his own coldness. Believers can ask whether they are quick to judge others while overlooking their own lack of love, gratitude, or hospitality.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to defend and honor those whom others look down on. Like Jesus, they can speak up for people who are being judged unfairly and treat them with dignity and kindness, especially in religious settings where such people may feel unwelcome.

### Luke 7:47 — Forgiven Much, She Loved Much

verse: Luke 7:47

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the conclusion of Jesus' response to Simon the Pharisee. A woman known as a sinner had come into Simon's house and shown extravagant love to Jesus by washing His feet with her tears, kissing them, and anointing them with perfume. Simon was offended. Jesus told a parable about two debtors: one owed five hundred denarii and the other fifty, and the lender forgave both. Simon agreed that the one forgiven more would love more. Jesus then contrasted Simon's cold hospitality with the woman's overflowing love.

Now Jesus draws the conclusion: [[v47|Therefore I tell you, her sins, which are many, are forgiven, for she loved much]]. This sentence can be misunderstood. It might sound as if her love earned her forgiveness. But the parable shows the opposite order. In the parable, the debtors were forgiven first, and their love came as a result. The woman's great love was the evidence that she had been forgiven, not the cause. She had likely encountered Jesus or His message before, received His forgiveness, and now came to express her gratitude. Her love showed how much she had been forgiven.

Then Jesus said, [[v47|But one to whom little is forgiven, loves little]]. This was aimed at Simon. Simon did not think he needed much forgiveness, so he showed little love. The problem was not that Simon had fewer sins, but that he did not recognize his need.

Jesus then said directly to the woman, [[v48|Your sins are forgiven]], confirming publicly what had already happened. The guests asked, "Who is this who even forgives sins?" Jesus told her, [[v50|Your faith has saved you. Go in peace]]. Her salvation came through faith in Jesus, and her love was its fruit.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the connection between forgiveness and love. Those who understand how much they have been forgiven respond with deep love and gratitude toward God. Love for God is not a way of earning forgiveness; it is the natural response to forgiveness received. John writes, [[1 John 4:19|We love him, because he first loved us]].

It also shows that everyone needs forgiveness. Simon thought he owed little; the woman knew she owed much. But in reality, both were debtors who could not pay. The difference was in their awareness of their need. Those who think they are good enough often have little love for God, because they do not grasp the depth of His mercy.

Finally, the verse shows that Jesus has authority to forgive sins. The guests' question, "Who is this who even forgives sins?", points to His divine identity. Only God can forgive sins, and Jesus forgave this woman's many sins.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to remember how much they have been forgiven. When love for God grows cold, it is often because believers have forgotten the depth of their sin and the greatness of God's mercy. Reflecting on God's forgiveness can rekindle gratitude and love.

It also warns believers against self-righteousness. Those who think they have little need of forgiveness will love little. Believers can ask God to help them see their sin honestly, not to drive them to despair, but to deepen their appreciation of His grace.

Finally, this verse offers hope to those who feel their sins are too many. The woman's sins were many, yet they were forgiven. No one is beyond Jesus' mercy. Those who come to Him in faith can hear His words: "Your sins are forgiven. Your faith has saved you. Go in peace."

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 7 shows Jesus reaching out to people others overlooked or rejected: a Gentile soldier, a poor widow, a doubting prophet, and a sinful woman. In each case, He responded with compassion, power, and grace. Believers can take comfort that Jesus sees them in their need and responds with love, whatever their background or situation.

The centurion's humble faith teaches believers to trust Jesus' authority and to approach Him with humility rather than a sense of entitlement. The widow of Nain shows that Jesus notices suffering and acts out of compassion, even before being asked. Believers can bring their grief to Him and trust His power over death. They can also follow His example by noticing and caring for those who are grieving and vulnerable.

John's question and Jesus' answer show that honest doubt can be brought to Jesus, and that He responds by pointing to His works. The contrast between the tax collectors who accepted God's way and the Pharisees who rejected it warns believers against pride and the refusal to repent.

Finally, the story of the sinful woman reveals the heart of the gospel. Those who are forgiven much love much. Believers can remember the depth of God's forgiveness and respond with grateful love. They can also learn to see people as Jesus sees them, looking beyond labels and reputations, and refuse the self-righteousness that keeps people from recognizing their own need for grace.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for Your compassion toward people the world overlooks. Like the centurion, I am not worthy for You to come under my roof, yet I trust that You can say the word and bring healing. Thank You for seeing the widow of Nain in her grief and restoring her son. Comfort all who grieve, and help me to notice and care for those who are suffering.

When I have questions and doubts, help me to bring them to You and to look at what You have done. Keep me from the pride that rejects Your ways. Thank You for forgiving my many sins. Help me never to forget how much I have been forgiven, so that I may love You much. Teach me to see people as You see them, beyond their labels and reputations, and to welcome them with Your grace. Thank You that my faith in You has saved me and that I can go in peace. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
