# Luke 4
theme: Anointed by the Spirit: Good News for the Poor and the Outsider
main: Full of the Holy Spirit, Jesus resists the devil's temptations in the wilderness and returns to Galilee in the Spirit's power. In His hometown synagogue He reads Isaiah's prophecy of good news for the poor, freedom for captives, and sight for the blind, and announces that it is fulfilled in Him. When He reminds His neighbors that God's prophets showed mercy to Gentiles, they try to kill Him. In Capernaum He drives out demons and heals the sick, and then moves on, because He was sent to preach the kingdom in other towns too.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 4 marks the beginning of Jesus' public ministry. In chapter 3, Jesus was baptized while praying, the Holy Spirit descended on Him, and the Father declared Him His beloved Son. Luke then traced His genealogy back to Adam, "the son of God." Now Luke shows the Son of God tested in the wilderness and then launching His mission in Galilee. Throughout this chapter, Luke emphasizes the Holy Spirit's role: Jesus is [[v1|full of the Holy Spirit]], [[v1|led by the Spirit]], returns [[v14|in the power of the Spirit]], and declares, [[v18|The Spirit of the Lord is on me]].

### The Temptation in the Wilderness (4:1–13)

Jesus, full of the Holy Spirit, was led by the Spirit into the wilderness, where He was tempted by the devil for forty days. He ate nothing during that time, and afterward He was hungry. Luke's account closely parallels Matthew's (studied at Matthew 4:4 and 4:10), but Luke places the temptations in a different order. Matthew ends with the temptation on the mountain, where the devil offers the kingdoms of the world. Luke ends with the temptation at the temple in Jerusalem. This fits Luke's emphasis on Jerusalem, where his Gospel begins (with Zechariah in the temple) and ends (with the disciples worshiping in the temple), and where Jesus' mission will reach its climax.

The devil's first temptation was to turn a stone into bread. Jesus answered from Deuteronomy 8:3 that people do not live by bread alone. The devil then showed Jesus all the kingdoms of the world and said, [[v6|I will give you all this authority and their glory, for it has been delivered to me]]. Luke alone includes this boast. The devil claimed authority over the world's kingdoms; Scripture elsewhere calls him [[John 12:31|the prince of this world]]. But his offer required Jesus to worship him. Jesus answered from Deuteronomy 6:13 that God alone is to be worshiped and served.

Finally, the devil took Jesus to the highest point of the temple and, quoting Psalm 91, urged Him to throw Himself down and let angels catch Him. Jesus answered from Deuteronomy 6:16, [[v12|You shall not tempt the Lord your God]].

Luke concludes, [[v13|When the devil had completed every temptation, he departed from him until another time]]. The phrase "until another time" (or "until an opportune time") signals that the battle was not over. Satan would return. In Luke 22:3, as the crucifixion approaches, Luke writes that Satan entered Judas. Jesus' victory in the wilderness was the first round in a conflict that would culminate at the cross.

### Return to Galilee (4:14–15)

Jesus returned to Galilee in the power of the Spirit. News about Him spread through the whole region. He taught in their synagogues, and everyone praised Him. These summary verses show His early popularity, which makes the rejection at Nazareth all the more striking.

### Rejected at Nazareth (4:16–30)

Jesus went to Nazareth, where He had grown up. On the Sabbath He went to the synagogue, [[v16|as was his custom]]. Jesus regularly participated in synagogue worship. A synagogue service typically included prayers, readings from the Law and the Prophets, and a sermon or explanation, often given by a visiting teacher.

Jesus stood up to read, and the scroll of the prophet Isaiah was handed to Him. He unrolled it and found the passage from Isaiah 61:1–2 (with a phrase from Isaiah 58:6). He read, [[v18|The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to preach good news to the poor]]. This passage is studied below.

Then He rolled up the scroll, gave it back to the attendant, and sat down, the customary position for teaching. Every eye in the synagogue was fixed on Him. He said, [[v21|Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing]]. Jesus was claiming to be the one Isaiah described, the Spirit-anointed messenger bringing God's salvation.

Notice where Jesus stopped reading. Isaiah 61:2 continues, [[Isaiah 61:2|and the day of vengeance of our God]]. Jesus stopped after "to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord." His first coming was focused on grace and salvation. Judgment would come later.

At first the people were impressed. They [[v22|wondered at the gracious words which proceeded out of his mouth]]. But they also asked, [[v22|Isn’t this Joseph’s son?]] They knew Him as a local carpenter's son. How could He be the fulfillment of Isaiah's prophecy?

Jesus anticipated their thoughts. He quoted a proverb, [[v23|Physician, heal yourself!]], meaning they wanted Him to do the miracles in Nazareth that He had done in Capernaum. He said, [[v24|no prophet is acceptable in his hometown]]. Then He gave two examples from Israel's history, studied below. Elijah was sent to a widow in Sidon, a Gentile, during a famine, rather than to the many widows in Israel. Elisha healed Naaman the Syrian, a Gentile, rather than the many lepers in Israel.

The people's mood changed instantly. They were furious. They drove Jesus out of town and took Him to the edge of the hill on which Nazareth was built, intending to throw Him off the cliff. Nazareth sits on a hillside with steep slopes nearby. Throwing someone off a cliff could also be the first step of a stoning. But Jesus [[v30|passing through the middle of them, went his way]]. Luke does not explain how. Jesus' time had not yet come.

### Authority in Capernaum (4:31–41)

Jesus went down to Capernaum, a town on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee. On the Sabbath, He taught in the synagogue, and the people were amazed because His words had authority.

In the synagogue was a man possessed by an unclean demon, who cried out, [[v34|Ah! what have we to do with you, Jesus of Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are: the Holy One of God!]] Jesus rebuked the demon: [[v35|Be silent and come out of him!]] The demon threw the man down in front of everyone and came out without injuring him. Luke the physician notes that the man was unharmed. The people were amazed at Jesus' authority and power over unclean spirits, and news about Him spread throughout the region.

Jesus then went to Simon's house. Simon's mother-in-law was suffering from a high fever, and they asked Jesus to help her. Luke, possibly drawing on his medical knowledge, specifies "a great fever." Jesus stood over her and [[v39|rebuked the fever]], treating it almost as a hostile force. The fever left her, and she immediately got up and began to serve them.

At sunset, when the Sabbath ended, people brought all their sick to Jesus. Luke notes that He [[v40|laid his hands on every one of them]], giving each person individual attention. Demons came out of many, shouting, [[v41|You are the Christ, the Son of God!]] Jesus rebuked them and would not let them speak, because they knew He was the Messiah. He did not want His identity proclaimed by demons or misunderstood by the crowds.

### Moving On to Other Towns (4:42–44)

At daybreak, Jesus went to a solitary place. The crowds searched for Him and tried to keep Him from leaving. But He said, [[v43|I must preach the good news of God’s Kingdom to the other cities also. For this reason I have been sent]]. This is studied below. He continued preaching in the synagogues of Judea (some manuscripts read "Galilee"; Luke sometimes uses "Judea" for the whole land of the Jews).

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 4:18–19 — The Spirit of the Lord Is on Me

verse: Luke 4:18-19

#### Simple Explanation

These verses contain the words Jesus read in the synagogue at Nazareth, His hometown. It was the Sabbath, and Jesus had returned from the wilderness full of the Spirit's power, with news about Him spreading throughout Galilee. In the synagogue service, He stood to read from the Scriptures, and He was handed the scroll of Isaiah. He found Isaiah 61:1–2 and read it aloud.

[[v18|The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me]]. In the Old Testament, prophets, priests, and kings were anointed with oil to set them apart for God's service. Here, the anointing is with the Spirit. Jesus had just been anointed with the Spirit at His baptism (Luke 3:22). The title "Messiah" or "Christ" means "anointed one."

The passage then describes the anointed one's mission:

[[v18|to preach good news to the poor]]. "The poor" includes people who are economically poor and also those who are humble, oppressed, and dependent on God. Luke's Gospel shows special concern for the poor (Luke 6:20; 14:13; 16:19–31).

[[v18|He has sent me to heal the broken hearted, to proclaim release to the captives]]. "Release" can mean freedom from prison, but it is also the word used for forgiveness of sins. Jesus brings freedom from every kind of bondage, including sin and demonic oppression.

[[v18|recovering of sight to the blind]]. Jesus would heal physically blind people (Luke 18:35–43) and also open spiritually blind eyes.

[[v18|to deliver those who are crushed]]. This phrase from Isaiah 58:6 describes setting free the oppressed and broken.

[[v19|and to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord]]. This refers to the year of the Lord's favor, recalling the Year of Jubilee (Leviticus 25), when every fifty years debts were cancelled, slaves were freed, and land was returned to its original owners. Jesus was announcing a time of God's grace and restoration.

After reading, Jesus sat down and said, "Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing." He was claiming that He was the one Isaiah had described.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses serve as Jesus' mission statement in Luke's Gospel. Everything He does in the following chapters, preaching good news, healing, freeing people from demons, forgiving sins, welcoming outcasts, fulfills this passage. His ministry addresses the whole person: body, soul, and social situation.

The passage also shows that Jesus' mission is empowered by the Holy Spirit. Luke emphasizes the Spirit's role in Jesus' life, and later in Acts, he shows the same Spirit empowering Jesus' followers to continue His mission (Acts 1:8; 2:1–4).

Finally, the phrase "the acceptable year of the Lord" points to a time of grace. Jesus stopped reading before "the day of vengeance of our God." His first coming was focused on salvation, forgiveness, and restoration. Christians understand that judgment will come at His return, but now is the time of God's favor. As Paul writes, [[2 Corinthians 6:2|Behold, now is the acceptable time. Behold, now is the day of salvation]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to receive Jesus' good news personally. Whether they are poor in resources or in spirit, broken-hearted, captive to sin or addiction, blind to God's truth, or crushed by life, Jesus came to bring them freedom and healing. They can come to Him with their needs.

They also show believers what it means to follow Jesus. If Jesus' mission focused on the poor, the broken, the captive, and the oppressed, His followers should care about these people too. Churches and believers can share the good news in words while also showing compassion through practical care, healing, and efforts to free people from bondage.

Finally, these verses remind believers that they serve in the power of the Spirit. Jesus' mission was empowered by the Spirit, and believers' service must be as well. They can depend on the Holy Spirit rather than on their own strength as they seek to share Christ's love with others.

### Luke 4:26–27 — Mercy for the Widow of Zarephath and Naaman

verse: Luke 4:26-27

#### Simple Explanation

These verses come at the turning point of Jesus' sermon in Nazareth. Jesus had read from Isaiah and declared that the prophecy was fulfilled in Him. At first, the people spoke well of Him and were amazed at His gracious words. But they also questioned how Joseph's son could make such claims. Jesus perceived that they wanted Him to perform miracles in Nazareth as He had in Capernaum, and He said, "No prophet is acceptable in his hometown."

Then Jesus gave two examples from the Old Testament. First, during the time of the prophet Elijah, there was a terrible famine in Israel lasting three and a half years. There were many widows in Israel, yet [[v26|Elijah was sent to none of them, except to Zarephath, in the land of Sidon, to a woman who was a widow]]. Zarephath was a town in Phoenicia, Gentile territory. God sent Elijah to a Gentile widow, who cared for him and whose son he raised from the dead (1 Kings 17:8–24).

Second, during the time of the prophet Elisha, [[v27|There were many lepers in Israel]], yet none of them was healed [[v27|except Naaman, the Syrian]]. Naaman was the commander of the army of Aram (Syria), an enemy nation. Through Elisha, God healed Naaman of his leprosy after he washed in the Jordan River (2 Kings 5:1–14).

Jesus' point was that when Israel rejected God's prophets, God showed mercy to Gentiles who responded in faith. The people of Nazareth were rejecting Jesus, and His words implied that God's blessings might go to outsiders instead.

The reaction was immediate and violent. The people were filled with rage, drove Jesus out of town, and tried to throw Him off a cliff. They were angry that Jesus suggested God's favor might extend to Gentiles, and that their own familiarity with Him might lead them to miss what God was doing.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal that God's mercy has always reached beyond Israel's boundaries. Even in the Old Testament, God showed kindness to Gentiles like the widow of Zarephath and Naaman. This anticipates the gospel's spread to all nations, a major theme in Luke and Acts. Simeon had called Jesus [[Luke 2:32|a light for revelation to the nations]].

They also show the danger of assuming special privilege. The people of Nazareth thought they had a special claim on Jesus because He grew up among them. They expected Him to benefit their town. Jesus challenged this attitude. God's grace is not owed to anyone because of their background, nationality, or familiarity with religious things.

Finally, the people's violent reaction foreshadows Jesus' rejection and death. Rejected in His hometown at the beginning of His ministry, He would be rejected in Jerusalem at the end. Yet even rejection could not stop God's purposes; Jesus passed through the crowd and continued His mission.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers to examine whether they assume God's favor belongs only to people like them. Churches can become comfortable with insiders and resistant to outsiders. Jesus' examples remind believers that God delights to show mercy to unexpected people, including those from different cultures, nations, and backgrounds.

They also warn against familiarity breeding contempt. People who have grown up in church or around Christianity may take Jesus for granted, as the people of Nazareth did. Believers can ask God to keep their hearts open and responsive, so that familiarity never blinds them to His work.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to rejoice when God works among people they did not expect. Instead of resenting God's mercy to outsiders, they can celebrate it and participate in sharing God's love with everyone.

### Luke 4:43 — "I Must Preach the Good News to Other Cities Also"

verse: Luke 4:43

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes at the end of an extraordinary day in Capernaum. Jesus had taught with authority in the synagogue, cast out a demon, healed Simon's mother-in-law, and then, after sunset, healed many sick people, laying His hands on each one. Demons came out of many. The whole town was amazed.

At daybreak, Jesus went to a solitary place. The crowds searched for Him and, when they found Him, [[v42|held on to him, so that he wouldn’t go away from them]]. They wanted Him to stay in Capernaum, perhaps to keep healing and teaching them. It would have been natural for Jesus to settle where He was so welcomed.

But Jesus said, [[v43|I must preach the good news of God’s Kingdom to the other cities also. For this reason I have been sent]].

The word "must" expresses divine necessity. Jesus' mission was determined by the Father, not by popular demand. "Preach the good news" connects with the Isaiah passage He read in Nazareth: He was anointed to preach good news. This is the first time Luke uses the phrase "the kingdom of God," which becomes the central theme of Jesus' teaching. The kingdom is God's reign, His saving rule breaking into the world through Jesus. "For this reason I have been sent" shows that Jesus understood Himself as sent by God with a specific purpose.

Notice also the contrast between Nazareth and Capernaum. Nazareth tried to kill Jesus; Capernaum tried to keep Him. Both, in different ways, tried to control Him. But Jesus followed His Father's purpose, not the crowd's wishes.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus' priority was preaching the good news of the kingdom. His healings and miracles were important; they demonstrated the kingdom's power and showed God's compassion. But they served a larger purpose: announcing that God's reign had arrived and calling people to respond. Jesus did not want to be known only as a healer.

It also shows that Jesus' mission was directed by the Father, not by popularity. The crowds wanted Him to stay; the Father had sent Him to other towns. Jesus regularly withdrew to pray (Luke 5:16), and prayer kept Him focused on His mission. Mark's parallel account mentions that Jesus had gone out early to pray before saying these words (Mark 1:35–38).

Finally, the verse reveals the expansive nature of the gospel. Jesus' message was not meant to stay in one place. It was meant to spread to other towns, and eventually, through His followers, to all nations. This anticipates the mission of the church described in Acts.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to keep the good news at the center of their lives and ministries. Churches and Christians do many good things, but proclaiming the good news of God's kingdom should remain central. Practical care and compassion are important, and they are most powerful when connected to the message of Jesus.

It also encourages believers to be guided by God's purposes rather than by the expectations of others. Even good opportunities and popularity can pull people away from what God has called them to do. Through prayer, believers can discern God's direction and have the courage to follow it.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that the gospel is meant to spread. Believers can look beyond their own community, supporting and participating in efforts to share the good news with other towns, cities, and nations.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 4 shows Jesus beginning His ministry in the power of the Holy Spirit. He resisted temptation in the wilderness by relying on God's Word, and He launched His mission by declaring that the Spirit had anointed Him to bring good news to the poor and freedom to the oppressed. Believers can rely on the same Spirit to help them resist temptation and serve others faithfully.

The temptation account reminds believers that spiritual battles are real and ongoing. The devil left Jesus "until another time." Believers should not assume that one victory means the struggle is over. Staying close to God through Scripture and prayer prepares them for future tests.

The sermon at Nazareth calls believers to embrace Jesus' mission. He came for the poor, the broken-hearted, the captive, the blind, and the oppressed. His followers can share His good news in words and show His compassion in action. At the same time, the people of Nazareth's rejection warns against familiarity and against assuming that God's favor belongs only to people like us. God delights to show mercy to outsiders, and believers should rejoice in that.

The healings in Capernaum show Jesus' authority over evil and sickness, and His personal care for each individual. Finally, His decision to leave Capernaum shows the priority of preaching the kingdom and of following God's direction rather than popular demand. Believers can keep the gospel central, seek God's guidance in prayer, and help the good news spread beyond their own community.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for resisting the devil in the wilderness and for relying on God's Word. Help me to stand firm when I am tempted, knowing that the struggle continues, and fill me with the Holy Spirit as You were filled. Thank You for coming to bring good news to the poor, healing to the broken-hearted, freedom to captives, sight to the blind, and liberty to the oppressed. Bring that freedom and healing into my life.

Keep me from the familiarity and pride that made Your hometown reject You. Help me to rejoice when You show mercy to people I would not expect. Give me Your compassion for those who are suffering, and help me to care for them in Your name. Keep the good news of Your kingdom at the center of my life, and guide me by Your Spirit to go where You send me. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
