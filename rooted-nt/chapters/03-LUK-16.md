# Luke 16
theme: Faithful With Money, Rich Toward the Poor, Listening to God's Word
main: Through the puzzling parable of a dishonest manager, Jesus urges His followers to use money shrewdly with eternity in view and to be faithful in small things, since no one can serve both God and wealth. When money-loving Pharisees scoff, He warns that God sees the heart. Then, in the story of a rich man and the beggar Lazarus, He shows the eternal consequences of ignoring the poor and of refusing to listen to the Scriptures God has already given.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 16 continues Jesus' teaching on the way to Jerusalem and follows directly from the three parables of the lost in Luke 15. Those parables answered religious leaders who grumbled that Jesus welcomed sinners. Luke 16 turns to money, a subject Jesus addressed often. It includes two parables, both beginning with the words [[v1|There was a certain rich man]] (v. 1) and [[v19|there was a certain rich man]] (v. 19). The first is addressed to the disciples; the second responds to Pharisees who [[v14|were lovers of money]]. Together they teach that money is a test of faithfulness and that how people use wealth has eternal consequences.

### The Parable of the Shrewd Manager (16:1–8)

A rich man had a manager, or steward, who handled his business affairs. Wealthy landowners often employed managers to oversee their estates, collect rents, and handle contracts. Someone accused the manager of wasting his master's property. The master called him in and said, [[v2|Give an accounting of your management, for you can no longer be manager]].

The manager thought to himself about his options. He was not strong enough to do hard manual labor and was ashamed to beg. Then he came up with a plan. Before word of his dismissal spread, he called in his master's debtors one by one. These were likely tenant farmers or merchants who owed the master large amounts of produce. One owed a hundred measures of olive oil (around 800 gallons); the manager told him to change his bill to fifty. Another owed a hundred measures of wheat (perhaps a thousand bushels); the manager told him to change it to eighty. These were substantial reductions. The debtors would be grateful to the manager, and he hoped they would welcome him into their homes after he lost his job.

Then comes the surprising line: [[v8|His lord commended the dishonest manager because he had done wisely]]. The master did not praise the manager's dishonesty; Jesus Himself calls him "the dishonest manager." He praised his shrewdness, his ability to see his situation clearly and act decisively to secure his future. Some interpreters suggest the manager may have been cancelling his own commission or illegal interest, which would make his actions less dishonest, but the text still calls him dishonest. The point is not his ethics but his foresight.

Jesus drew the lesson: [[v8|for the children of this world are, in their own generation, wiser than the children of the light]]. People of the world are often more clever about securing their earthly future than God's people are about preparing for eternity. This parable is studied below.

### Faithful With Little and Much (16:9–13)

Jesus continued, [[v9|make for yourselves friends by means of unrighteous mammon, so that when you fail, they may receive you into the eternal tents]]. "Mammon" is an Aramaic word for wealth or possessions. Jesus calls it "unrighteous" because money so easily becomes tied up with injustice and greed, and because it belongs to this passing age. "When you fail" means when money runs out or when life ends. Just as the manager used his master's money to make friends who would receive him, believers should use money in ways that bless others and store up treasure in heaven.

Then Jesus gave a principle about faithfulness, studied below: [[v10|He who is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much]]. If people are not trustworthy with worldly wealth, who will trust them with true riches? If they are not faithful with what belongs to someone else (and all possessions ultimately belong to God), who will give them something of their own?

He concluded, [[v13|No servant can serve two masters]]. A person will love one and hate the other. [[v13|You aren’t able to serve God and Mammon]]. Money is a rival master, demanding loyalty that belongs to God alone. (This saying is studied at Matthew 6:21–24.)

### The Pharisees Scoff (16:14–18)

The Pharisees heard all this and [[v14|scoffed at him]]. Luke describes them as [[v14|lovers of money]]. Many people in that time believed that wealth was a sign of God's blessing and righteousness. The Pharisees may have thought Jesus' warnings about money were foolish, especially coming from a poor teacher.

Jesus answered, [[v15|You are those who justify yourselves in the sight of men, but God knows your hearts. For that which is exalted among men is an abomination in the sight of God]]. The Pharisees made themselves look righteous before others, but God saw their hearts. What people admire, such as wealth, status, and religious reputation, can be detestable to God when it springs from pride and greed.

Jesus then made three brief statements. First, [[v16|The law and the prophets were until John]]. John the Baptist marked a turning point; since then, the good news of God's kingdom has been preached, and everyone is pressing urgently into it. Second, this does not mean the law is cancelled: [[v17|it is easier for heaven and earth to pass away than for one tiny stroke of a pen in the law to fall]]. Jesus fulfills the law, and its moral demands remain. Third, as an example of the law's continuing force, He spoke about divorce: whoever divorces his wife and marries another commits adultery. (This teaching is discussed at Matthew 19:6 and Mark 10, including how Christian traditions apply it and the protection of those in danger.) The Pharisees who prided themselves on keeping the law were in fact loosening its moral standards where it suited them, as with the easy divorce practices of the time.

### The Rich Man and Lazarus (16:19–31)

Jesus then told a story found only in Luke. There was a rich man who dressed in purple and fine linen and feasted lavishly every day. Purple cloth was extremely expensive, dyed with a substance taken from shellfish, and it was associated with royalty. Fine linen was often imported from Egypt. This man lived in luxury.

At his gate lay a beggar named Lazarus, covered with sores. Lazarus is the only character in Jesus' parables given a name. The name is a form of Eleazar, meaning "God helps." Lazarus longed to eat the scraps that fell from the rich man's table. Wealthy people sometimes wiped their hands on bread during meals and then threw it on the floor. Even the dogs came and licked his sores; he was too weak to drive them away. The rich man passed Lazarus every day at his gate but did nothing.

Both men died. Lazarus was carried by angels to [[v22|Abraham’s bosom]], a place of honor and comfort, like reclining next to Abraham at a banquet. The rich man was buried, and in Hades he was in torment. He looked up and saw Abraham far away with Lazarus beside him. He called out, [[v24|Father Abraham, have mercy on me, and send Lazarus, that he may dip the tip of his finger in water and cool my tongue!]] Notice that he still thought of Lazarus as a servant to be sent on errands.

Abraham replied, [[v25|Son, remember that you, in your lifetime, received your good things, and Lazarus, in the same way, bad things. But here he is now comforted and you are in anguish]]. The reversal echoes Mary's song (Luke 1:52–53) and Jesus' blessings and woes (Luke 6:20–26). Abraham added that a great chasm separated them, so that no one could cross from one side to the other.

The rich man then begged Abraham to send Lazarus to warn his five brothers. Abraham answered, [[v29|They have Moses and the prophets. Let them listen to them]]. The rich man insisted that if someone came from the dead, they would repent. Abraham's reply is studied below.

A note on interpretation: this story uses imagery common in Jewish thinking of the time about the afterlife, such as Abraham's side, Hades, and a great gulf. Christians differ on how literally to take the details of the afterlife in this story, since it functions as a parable. But its central lessons are clear: choices in this life have eternal consequences, the way people treat the poor matters to God, and the Scriptures are sufficient to lead people to repentance.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 16:8–9 — Using Wealth Wisely With Eternity in View

verse: Luke 16:8-9

#### Simple Explanation

These verses contain the lesson of the parable of the shrewd manager, one of the most puzzling parables Jesus told. A manager was about to be fired for wasting his master's money. Thinking quickly about his future, he called in his master's debtors and reduced their bills significantly, making them grateful to him so that they would welcome him into their homes after he lost his job.

Then [[v8|His lord commended the dishonest manager because he had done wisely]]. Many readers are troubled by this. Was Jesus praising dishonesty? No. The parable calls him "the dishonest manager," and Jesus does not approve of his cheating. What the master commended was his shrewdness: he recognized that his time was short, understood his situation, and acted decisively to prepare for the future. The word translated "wisely" means shrewdly or prudently.

Jesus then drew the lesson: [[v8|for the children of this world are, in their own generation, wiser than the children of the light]]. "Children of this world" are people whose concerns are limited to this life. "Children of the light" are God's people. Jesus observed that worldly people are often more thoughtful and energetic about securing their earthly futures than God's people are about preparing for eternity.

[[v9|I tell you, make for yourselves friends by means of unrighteous mammon]]. "Mammon" means money or wealth. Jesus calls it "unrighteous" because wealth is so often gained or used unjustly and because it belongs to this passing world. Yet it can be used for good. Believers should use money to "make friends," to help people, especially the poor, and to support God's work.

[[v9|so that when you fail, they may receive you into the eternal tents]]. "When you fail" means when money runs out, or when life ends. "Eternal tents" refers to eternal dwellings in God's kingdom. The picture may be of people who have been helped by believers' generosity welcoming them into eternity, or of God Himself receiving them. The point is that money used generously in this life has eternal significance.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This parable teaches that this life is a time of stewardship, and it will end. Like the manager, every person will one day give an account. The wise response is to use what God has entrusted, especially money, with that day in view.

It also teaches that money can be used for eternal purposes. Money itself is temporary, but the good done with it can have lasting effects: people fed, the gospel shared, the poor helped, relationships built. Jesus taught elsewhere that giving to the needy stores up treasure in heaven (Luke 12:33).

Finally, the parable challenges believers' priorities. Worldly people often plan carefully for retirement, careers, and comfort. Believers should be at least as thoughtful about eternal matters. This does not mean neglecting responsible planning, but it means placing eternal priorities first.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to think strategically about how they use money. Rather than spending everything on personal comfort or saving everything for themselves, they can ask how their resources might bless others and advance God's kingdom.

Practical applications include generous giving to those in need, supporting ministries and missions, helping neighbors and friends in crisis, and using hospitality to build relationships. These are ways of "making friends" with money that will matter in eternity.

Finally, these verses remind believers that life is short. Just as the manager knew his time was limited, believers can live with an awareness that their opportunity to use earthly resources for God's purposes will one day end. Acting wisely now has eternal value.

### Luke 16:10 — Faithful in Little, Faithful in Much

verse: Luke 16:10

#### Simple Explanation

This verse follows the parable of the shrewd manager, in which a manager about to lose his job used his master's money to secure his future. Jesus had just told His disciples to use money wisely, with eternity in view. Now He states a principle about faithfulness: [[v10|He who is faithful in a very little is faithful also in much. He who is dishonest in a very little is also dishonest in much]].

"Faithful" means trustworthy, reliable, and dependable. "A very little" refers to small things, which in this context especially means money and earthly possessions. Jesus says that faithfulness in small things reveals character. A person who is trustworthy in small matters is likely to be trustworthy in larger ones. Similarly, a person who is dishonest in small things will be dishonest in larger things too.

In the following verses, Jesus applies this to money and spiritual life. [[v11|If therefore you have not been faithful in the unrighteous mammon, who will commit to your trust the true riches?]] Worldly wealth is the "very little"; "true riches" are spiritual blessings and responsibilities in God's kingdom. If people cannot be trusted with money, God will not entrust them with greater spiritual treasures.

[[v12|If you have not been faithful in that which is another’s, who will give you that which is your own?]] Everything people possess on earth ultimately belongs to God; they are stewards of His property. If they are not faithful with what belongs to God, they cannot expect to receive the eternal inheritance He promises.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that character is revealed in small things. Many people think that small compromises do not matter, such as a small lie, a little cheating, or careless use of money. Jesus teaches that these small things reveal and shape character. Faithfulness in daily, ordinary matters prepares a person for greater responsibility.

It also shows that money is a spiritual test. How people handle money reveals their hearts. Generosity, honesty, and responsible stewardship show faithfulness to God. Greed, dishonesty, and waste show that money has become a master. Jesus' parables about talents and minas make a similar point (Matthew 25:21; Luke 19:17).

Finally, the verse points to the generosity of God's rewards. Those who are faithful with "very little" will be entrusted with "much." God rewards faithfulness far beyond what is earned.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to be faithful in small things. Honesty in small financial matters, keeping small promises, doing small tasks well, and treating people kindly in everyday interactions all matter to God. These small faithfulnesses build a character that can be trusted with more.

It also encourages believers to see their finances as a matter of spiritual faithfulness. Budgeting responsibly, paying debts, giving generously, avoiding dishonest gain, and caring for what they have are ways of being faithful with what belongs to God.

Finally, this verse warns against excusing small sins. Believers can examine whether they have been dishonest in small ways, such as exaggerating, cutting corners, or taking what isn't theirs, and repent, knowing that small dishonesty can grow into larger unfaithfulness.

### Luke 16:31 — [[Luke 16:31|Neither Will They Be Persuaded if One Rises From the Dead]]

verse: Luke 16:31

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the final line of the story of the rich man and Lazarus. In the story, a rich man lived in luxury while a poor beggar named Lazarus lay at his gate, hungry and covered with sores. Both died. Lazarus was carried to Abraham's side, while the rich man found himself in torment in Hades. He begged Abraham to send Lazarus to cool his tongue, but Abraham said that a great chasm separated them.

Then the rich man made another request. He asked Abraham to send Lazarus to his father's house to warn his five brothers so they would not end up in the same place of torment. Abraham answered, [[v29|They have Moses and the prophets. Let them listen to them]]. "Moses and the prophets" refers to the Scriptures, the Old Testament. The Scriptures already taught God's will clearly, including care for the poor (Deuteronomy 15:7–11; Isaiah 58:6–7; Amos 4:1–2).

The rich man protested, [[v30|No, father Abraham, but if one goes to them from the dead, they will repent]]. He believed that a miraculous sign would convince his brothers where Scripture had not.

Abraham's final answer: [[v31|If they don’t listen to Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded if one rises from the dead]].

The problem was not a lack of evidence but a lack of willingness to listen. If people refuse to obey what God has already clearly revealed, a miracle will not change their hearts. They will find a way to explain it away.

This verse carries powerful irony. Jesus Himself would soon rise from the dead, yet many would still refuse to believe. In John 11, Jesus raised another man named Lazarus from the dead, and instead of believing, the chief priests plotted to kill Lazarus too (John 12:10–11).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that Scripture is sufficient to lead people to repentance and faith. God has revealed His will clearly in His Word. Paul writes that the Scriptures are [[2 Timothy 3:15|able to make you wise for salvation through faith, which is in Christ Jesus]]. People do not need more spectacular signs; they need to listen to what God has already said.

It also reveals that unbelief is often a matter of the will rather than the mind. People sometimes say they would believe if they had more evidence. But Jesus' story suggests that hearts closed to God's Word will remain closed even to miracles. Faith requires a willingness to listen and obey.

Finally, the verse points toward Jesus' resurrection. One did rise from the dead. The question for every reader is whether they will listen to Moses, the prophets, and the risen Jesus, or whether they will continue to refuse.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to take Scripture seriously. They do not need to wait for a dramatic sign to know God's will. Much of what God wants is already clear in His Word, including caring for the poor, loving neighbors, and repenting of sin. The question is whether they will obey.

It also helps believers understand people who refuse to believe. Sometimes believers think that if they could just present the right evidence, others would believe. While evidence and reasoning are valuable, faith also requires an open heart. Believers can pray for God to soften hearts while faithfully sharing His Word.

Finally, this verse, together with the whole story, calls believers to notice the "Lazarus at their gate." The rich man's sin was not his wealth alone, but his indifference to the suffering person right outside his door. Believers can open their eyes to the needy around them and respond with compassion, obeying what Moses and the prophets, and Jesus, have clearly taught.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 16 teaches that money is a test of the heart and that how people use it has eternal consequences. The parable of the shrewd manager challenges believers to use their resources wisely with eternity in view: helping others, giving generously, and investing in what lasts. Worldly people often plan carefully for their earthly futures; believers should be even more thoughtful about their eternal futures.

Jesus' principle about faithfulness in little things reminds believers that character is revealed in everyday choices. Honesty in small matters, careful stewardship, and generosity with modest resources prepare believers for greater responsibilities in God's kingdom. And no one can serve two masters. Believers must decide whether God or money will rule their hearts.

The Pharisees' scoffing reveals how easily religious people can love money while appearing righteous. God sees the heart, and what people admire may be detestable to Him. Believers can examine their hearts for love of money and pride in appearances.

Finally, the story of the rich man and Lazarus shows the eternal seriousness of ignoring the poor. The rich man did not harm Lazarus directly; he simply ignored him. Believers can open their eyes to the needy around them and respond with compassion. The story also teaches that Scripture is sufficient to lead people to God. Believers can listen to God's Word, obey it, and share it, trusting that it has the power to change hearts, while praying for those whose hearts remain closed.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, everything I have belongs to You. Teach me to be a faithful and wise steward, using my money and possessions with eternity in view. Help me to be faithful in small things, honest in every detail, and generous toward others. Free me from serving money, and help me to serve You alone.

Search my heart, Lord, and show me where I have loved money or sought the approval of others more than Your approval. Open my eyes to the Lazarus at my gate, the people in need whom I may have overlooked, and give me compassion and courage to help them. Thank You for the Scriptures that show me Your will. Help me to listen to Your Word and obey it, and to trust that it has power to lead people to repentance and life. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
