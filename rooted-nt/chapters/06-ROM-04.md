# Romans 4
theme: Abraham, the Father of All Who Believe
main: Paul shows that justification by faith is not a new idea by pointing to Abraham, the father of Israel. Abraham believed God, and his faith was credited to him as righteousness, not because he earned it, but as a gift from the God who justifies the ungodly. David, too, celebrates the blessing of those whose sins are forgiven apart from works. Abraham was declared righteous before he was circumcised, so he is the father of all who believe, whether Jew or Gentile. Though he and Sarah were far past the age of having children, Abraham trusted God's promise, giving glory to God, and his faith is a pattern for those who believe in the God who raised Jesus from the dead.

## Chapter Background and Story

In Romans 3, Paul declared that a person is justified by faith apart from the works of the Law (3:28). A Jewish reader might object: "Isn't this a new teaching? What about Abraham, the father of our nation? Wasn't he accepted by God because of his obedience and circumcision?" Abraham was the great example of righteousness in Jewish tradition. Some Jewish writings of the time praised Abraham for keeping the Law even before it was given.

Paul answers by turning to the Scriptures themselves. He shows that Abraham was justified by faith, not by works, and that this was true before he was circumcised. Abraham therefore becomes the father of all believers, both Jews and Gentiles. Paul's argument in this chapter is careful and rooted in the text of Genesis.

### Abraham Justified by Faith (4:1–8)

Paul asks what Abraham discovered. [[v2|For if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about, but not toward God]]. If Abraham had earned God's acceptance, he could boast. But Scripture says otherwise, as studied below.

Paul then brings in a second witness: David, Israel's great king. David [[v6|pronounces blessing on the man to whom God counts righteousness apart from works]]. Paul quotes Psalm 32:1–2, studied below. Having two witnesses was important in Jewish law (Deuteronomy 19:15). Abraham and David, the two most honored figures in Israel's history, both testify to justification by faith.

### Before Circumcision (4:9–12)

Paul then asks whether this blessing is only for the circumcised. When was Abraham declared righteous? [[v10|Not in circumcision, but in uncircumcision]]. In Genesis, Abraham was declared righteous by faith in Genesis 15, but he was not circumcised until Genesis 17, at least fourteen years later. Circumcision came after his justification.

What, then, was circumcision? [[v11|He received the sign of circumcision, a seal of the righteousness of the faith which he had while he was in uncircumcision]]. Circumcision was a sign and a seal, a visible confirmation of the righteousness Abraham already had by faith. It did not create his righteousness.

This means that Abraham is [[v11|the father of all those who believe, though they might be in uncircumcision]]. Gentile believers are Abraham's spiritual children. And he is also [[v12|the father of circumcision to those who not only are of the circumcision, but who also walk in the steps of that faith of our father Abraham]]. Jewish believers who share Abraham's faith are his children in the fullest sense.

### The Promise through Faith (4:13–17)

God's promise to Abraham was that he and his descendants would inherit the world. [[v13|For the promise to Abraham and to his offspring that he would be heir of the world wasn’t through the law, but through the righteousness of faith]]. The promise was given about 430 years before the Law of Moses (Galatians 3:17). It could not depend on the Law.

If the promise depended on keeping the Law, no one would receive it, because [[v15|the law produces wrath]]. The Law reveals sin and brings judgment. [[v16|For this cause it is of faith, that it may be according to grace, to the end that the promise may be sure to all the offspring]]. Because the promise rests on grace received by faith, it is certain. If it depended on human performance, it would always be uncertain.

God had told Abraham, [[v17|I have made you a father of many nations]] (Genesis 17:5). Abraham believed God, [[v17|who gives life to the dead, and calls the things that are not, as though they were]]. God can speak of things that do not yet exist as if they already did, because His word brings them into being.

### Abraham's Faith (4:18–22)

Paul describes Abraham's faith in vivid terms: [[v18|Against hope, Abraham in hope believed]]. From a human perspective, there was no hope. But Abraham's hope was in God. [[v19|Without being weakened in faith, he didn’t consider his own body, already having been worn out, (he being about a hundred years old), and the deadness of Sarah’s womb]]. Abraham faced the facts honestly. He knew he and Sarah were too old to have children. But he did not let those facts weaken his faith. The rest of this description is studied below.

### Written for Us (4:23–25)

Paul concludes by applying Abraham's story to his readers, studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Romans 4:3–5 — Faith Credited as Righteousness

verse: Romans 4:3-5

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had argued that people are justified by faith, not by works. He knew that his Jewish readers would think of Abraham, the father of their nation, whom they regarded as the greatest example of righteousness. So Paul turned to Abraham's story in Genesis.

[[v3|For what does the Scripture say? “Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.”]] Paul quoted Genesis 15:6. God had promised Abraham, who was old and childless, that his descendants would be as numerous as the stars. Abraham believed God's promise. And God "accounted" his faith as righteousness.

The word "accounted" (or "credited") is a bookkeeping term. It means to put something into someone's account. God credited righteousness to Abraham's account because of his faith. Abraham did not earn it; God gave it.

Paul then explained the difference between earning and receiving: [[v4|Now to him who works, the reward is not counted as grace, but as something owed]]. When people work for an employer, their wages are not a gift; they are owed. If salvation were earned by works, God would owe it to people.

[[v5|But to him who doesn’t work, but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is accounted for righteousness]]. Salvation is not like wages. It is a gift received by faith. Notice the remarkable phrase: God "justifies the ungodly." God does not justify people because they are already godly; He justifies those who are ungodly and trust in Him. This is the wonder of grace.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that justification by faith has always been God's way. Abraham lived long before the Law of Moses, yet he was declared righteous by faith. The New Testament gospel is not a departure from the Old Testament but its fulfillment.

They also show the difference between grace and wages. Paul writes elsewhere, [[Romans 6:23|For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord]]. Humanity has earned only death. Eternal life comes as a free gift.

Finally, the phrase "justifies the ungodly" shows the depth of God's grace. Many people think God accepts good people and rejects bad ones. The gospel teaches that God justifies those who know they are ungodly and trust in Him. Jesus said, [[Luke 5:32|I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses free believers from trying to earn God's approval. Many people treat their relationship with God like a job, hoping that if they work hard enough, God will reward them. Paul teaches that salvation is not wages but a gift. Believers can receive it with gratitude.

They also bring hope to those who feel too sinful for God. God justifies the ungodly. No one is too far gone to receive His grace. Believers can share this hope with anyone.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to follow Abraham's example of faith. Abraham believed God's promise even when it seemed impossible. Believers can trust God's promises in their own lives, knowing that faith is what God honors.

### Romans 4:7–8 — Blessed Are Those Whose Sins Are Forgiven

verse: Romans 4:7-8

#### Simple Explanation

After showing that Abraham was justified by faith, Paul brought in a second witness: King David. David was the greatest king in Israel's history, a man after God's own heart. But David had also committed terrible sins, including adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of her husband Uriah (2 Samuel 11).

Paul said that David [[v6|pronounces blessing on the man to whom God counts righteousness apart from works]]. Then he quoted Psalm 32:1–2.

[[v7|Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven, whose sins are covered]]. The word "blessed" means happy, fortunate, deeply favored by God. The happiest people are not those who have never sinned, since no one fits that description, but those whose sins are forgiven. "Forgiven" means released, sent away. "Covered" means hidden from God's judgment, as if under a covering. In the Old Testament, the word for atonement carries the idea of covering.

[[v8|Blessed is the man whom the Lord will by no means charge with sin]]. The Lord does not "charge" or "count" sin against this person. The same bookkeeping language is used here as in verse 3. Just as God credited righteousness to Abraham, He does not credit sin to those He forgives.

Notice that David speaks of this blessing apart from works. David did not earn forgiveness by doing good deeds. He received it by confessing his sin and trusting God's mercy. Psalm 32 describes how David felt crushed while he kept silent about his sin, and how he found relief when he confessed it: "I acknowledged my sin to you... and you forgave the iniquity of my sin" (Psalm 32:5).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that justification includes forgiveness. Being declared righteous means that God no longer counts a person's sins against them. This is the heart of the good news. Paul writes, [[2 Corinthians 5:19|namely, that God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not reckoning to them their trespasses]].

They also show that the happiest life is a forgiven life. Many people search for happiness in success, pleasure, or possessions. David discovered that true blessedness comes from having one's sins forgiven and being right with God.

Finally, these verses show that even great sinners can be forgiven. David committed adultery and murder, yet God forgave him when he repented. No sin is beyond God's mercy for those who turn to Him.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to experience the joy of forgiveness. Believers who carry guilt can bring their sins to God, confess them honestly as David did, and receive His forgiveness. There is great freedom in knowing that God does not count their sins against them.

They also remind believers that God's forgiveness is complete. When God forgives, He does not keep a record of sins. Believers do not need to keep punishing themselves for sins God has forgiven.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to extend forgiveness to others. Those who have been forgiven much are called to forgive others (Colossians 3:13). The joy of being forgiven can overflow into mercy toward those who have wronged them.

### Romans 4:20–21 — Fully Assured That God Is Able

verse: Romans 4:20-21

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was describing Abraham's faith. God had promised Abraham that he would be the father of many nations and that his descendants would be as numerous as the stars. But Abraham was about a hundred years old, and Sarah was about ninety and had never been able to have children. From a human point of view, the promise was impossible.

Paul said that Abraham did not ignore these facts. He considered his own body, which was "as good as dead," and Sarah's barrenness. He faced reality honestly. Yet he believed.

[[v20|Yet, looking to the promise of God, he didn’t waver through unbelief, but grew strong through faith, giving glory to God]]. Abraham kept his eyes on God's promise. He did not waver between belief and unbelief. Instead, his faith grew stronger. And in trusting God, he gave glory to God.

Paul's description is generous. Genesis shows that Abraham sometimes struggled; he laughed at the promise (Genesis 17:17) and tried to fulfill it through Hagar (Genesis 16). Yet over the whole course of his life, Abraham's faith held firm and grew stronger. Paul focuses on the overall direction of his faith.

[[v21|and being fully assured that what he had promised, he was also able to perform]]. This is the heart of Abraham's faith. He was "fully assured," completely convinced, that God was able to do what He had promised. Abraham's faith rested not on his own ability but on God's power and faithfulness.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the nature of true faith. Faith is not denying reality or pretending that difficulties do not exist. Abraham faced the facts. But faith looks beyond the facts to the God who is greater than any circumstance. Hebrews says, [[Hebrews 11:1|Now faith is assurance of things hoped for, proof of things not seen]].

They also show that faith gives glory to God. When people trust God, they honor Him as faithful and powerful. Unbelief, by contrast, suggests that God cannot be trusted. Faith is an act of worship.

Finally, these verses show that faith can grow. Abraham "grew strong through faith." Faith is not static; it can be strengthened through trusting God over time, especially in difficult circumstances.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers who face impossible situations. Believers may face illness, financial hardship, broken relationships, or long-delayed hopes. Like Abraham, they can acknowledge the difficulties honestly while trusting that God is able to do what He has promised.

They also encourage believers to focus on God's promises. Abraham looked to "the promise of God." Believers can strengthen their faith by meditating on God's promises in Scripture and remembering His faithfulness.

Finally, these verses encourage believers who struggle with doubt. Abraham was not perfect; he had moments of weakness. Yet God honored his faith. Believers who struggle can bring their doubts to God and ask Him to strengthen their faith.

### Romans 4:24–25 — Delivered for Our Trespasses, Raised for Our Justification

verse: Romans 4:24-25

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been telling the story of Abraham's faith. Abraham believed God's promise, and his faith was credited to him as righteousness. Now Paul applied this story to his readers.

[[v23|Now it was not written that it was accounted to him for his sake alone]]. The story of Abraham was not recorded only for Abraham's benefit. [[v24|but for our sake also, to whom it will be accounted, who believe in him who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead]]. The same thing that happened to Abraham happens to all who believe. Abraham believed in the God who could bring life from his and Sarah's "dead" bodies. Believers today trust in the God who raised Jesus from the dead. Both kinds of faith trust God's power to bring life out of death.

Then Paul summarized the gospel in one verse: [[v25|who was delivered up for our trespasses, and was raised for our justification]]. Jesus was "delivered up," handed over to death, because of our sins. His death dealt with the problem of sin. This echoes Isaiah 53:12, which says the servant "bore the sin of many."

And Jesus "was raised for our justification." His resurrection showed that God accepted His sacrifice. It is God's declaration that the work is complete. Because Jesus was raised, believers can be declared righteous. The death and resurrection of Jesus belong together; both are necessary for salvation.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the Scriptures were written for believers in every generation. Paul says the same elsewhere: [[Romans 15:4|For whatever things were written before were written for our learning]]. The stories of the Old Testament are not merely ancient history; they speak to believers today.

They also summarize the heart of the gospel: Christ's death and resurrection. Paul writes that the gospel is [[1 Corinthians 15:3|that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures]], [[1 Corinthians 15:4|that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures]]. Salvation rests entirely on what Christ has done.

Finally, these verses show the connection between faith in the resurrection and justification. Believers are justified by trusting in the God who raised Jesus. The resurrection is not an optional extra in the Christian faith; it is central to salvation.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to read the Old Testament as God's word for them. The stories of Abraham, Moses, David, and others were written to teach and encourage believers today.

They also invite believers to rest in what Christ has done. Jesus was delivered for their sins and raised for their justification. Believers can trust that their sins have been dealt with and that God has declared them righteous.

Finally, these verses call believers to place their faith in the God who raises the dead. The same God who brought life from Abraham and Sarah and who raised Jesus can bring life into situations that seem hopeless. Believers can trust Him with every part of their lives.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Romans 4 shows that justification by faith is God's ancient way. Abraham, the father of Israel, was declared righteous because he believed God, not because of his works. David, too, celebrated the blessing of forgiveness apart from works. Believers today are justified in the same way: by trusting God, who justifies the ungodly.

The chapter also shows that Abraham is the father of all who believe. He was declared righteous before he was circumcised, so Gentile believers are his true children, along with Jewish believers who share his faith. Believers can see themselves as part of one family of faith that stretches back to Abraham and includes people of every nation.

Romans 4 holds up Abraham as a model of faith. Facing impossible circumstances, he did not deny reality, but he looked to God's promise, grew strong in faith, and gave glory to God, fully convinced that God was able to do what He had promised. Believers can follow his example, trusting God's promises in their own difficulties and asking Him to strengthen their faith.

Finally, the chapter points to Jesus, who was delivered up for our trespasses and raised for our justification. Believers can rest in His finished work, trusting in the God who raises the dead, and can rejoice in the blessing of sins forgiven and righteousness credited to them as a gift.

## Chapter Prayer

Faithful God, thank You for Abraham, who believed You and was counted righteous. Thank You that You justify the ungodly, and that I can be made right with You not by my works but by trusting You. Thank You for the joy David knew, the blessing of sins forgiven and no longer counted against me. Make me a true child of Abraham, walking in the steps of his faith. When I face situations that seem impossible, help me to look to Your promises, to grow strong in faith, and to give You glory, fully assured that You are able to do what You have promised. Lord Jesus, thank You for being delivered up for my sins and raised for my justification. I trust in the God who raises the dead. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
