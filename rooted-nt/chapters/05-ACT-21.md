# Acts 21
theme: Ready to Die for the Name of the Lord Jesus: Paul Arrives in Jerusalem
main: As Paul sails toward Jerusalem, believers in Tyre and Caesarea warn him, and the prophet Agabus binds his own hands and feet with Paul's belt to show that Paul will be arrested. Paul answers that he is ready not only to be bound but to die for the name of the Lord Jesus, and his friends say, "The Lord's will be done." In Jerusalem, James and the elders rejoice at what God has done among the Gentiles, and they ask Paul to join some men in a purification rite to show Jewish believers that he respects the Law. In the temple, Jews from Asia falsely accuse him of bringing a Gentile inside, a mob tries to kill him, and Roman soldiers rescue and arrest him.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 21 marks a turning point in Paul's life. The missionary journeys are over. From here to the end of Acts, Paul is a prisoner, first in Jerusalem, then in Caesarea, and finally in Rome. Yet even in chains, he continues to bear witness to Jesus before crowds, councils, governors, and kings, just as Jesus had said he would (9:15).

The chapter also echoes the journey of Jesus to Jerusalem in Luke's Gospel. Like Jesus, Paul set his face to go to Jerusalem despite knowing that suffering awaited him (Luke 9:51). Like Jesus, he was warned by friends, handed over to the Gentiles, and accused by a crowd that shouted, "Away with him!" (compare Luke 23:18).

### Warnings Along the Way (21:1–14)

Paul and his companions sailed from Miletus past Cos and Rhodes to Patara, then crossed to Tyre in Phoenicia. There they found disciples and stayed seven days. These believers [[v4|said to Paul through the Spirit that he should not go up to Jerusalem]]. This raises a question: was Paul disobeying the Spirit by going? Most interpreters understand that the Spirit revealed to these believers that Paul would suffer in Jerusalem, and out of love they urged him not to go. Paul, however, had already been told that he was "bound by the Spirit" to go to Jerusalem (20:22). The Spirit revealed the danger; the believers drew their own conclusion about what Paul should do.

When it was time to leave, the believers, [[v5|with wives and children]], walked them out of the city. [[v5|Kneeling down on the beach, we prayed]]. This tender scene shows the love that united believers who had just met.

They sailed on to Ptolemais and then to Caesarea, where they stayed at the house of [[v8|Philip the evangelist, who was one of the seven]] (6:5). Philip had preached in Samaria and to the Ethiopian official (Acts 8). Nearly twenty years later, he was still serving the Lord in Caesarea. Luke notes that [[v9|this man had four virgin daughters who prophesied]]. The fulfillment of Joel's prophecy, "your sons and your daughters will prophesy" (2:17), continued in the life of the church.

While they were there, the prophet Agabus came from Judea. This was the same prophet who had predicted the famine (11:28). Agabus took Paul's belt, tied his own feet and hands with it, and said, [[v11|The Holy Spirit says: ‘So the Jews at Jerusalem will bind the man who owns this belt, and will deliver him into the hands of the Gentiles.’]] Like Old Testament prophets who acted out their messages (Isaiah 20:2–4; Jeremiah 13:1–11), Agabus dramatized the prophecy. Paul's response is studied below.

### Arrival in Jerusalem (21:15–26)

Paul and his companions traveled to Jerusalem, staying with Mnason of Cyprus, [[v16|an early disciple]]. In Jerusalem, [[v17|the brothers received us gladly]]. The next day, Paul met with James and the elders. Their conversation is studied below. Their request, and Paul's response, are also studied below.

James and the elders also reminded Paul of the decision of the Jerusalem Council (15:20, 29): Gentile believers were not required to follow the Law of Moses, except to avoid certain practices. The concern here was not about Gentiles but about Jewish believers and Paul's reputation among them.

### Riot in the Temple (21:27–36)

Near the end of the seven days of purification, Jews from the province of Asia, perhaps from Ephesus, saw Paul in the temple. Their accusation and its effects are studied below.

The crowd dragged Paul out of the temple, and the gates were shut. As they were trying to kill him, the commander of the Roman garrison heard that all Jerusalem was in an uproar. The Roman garrison was stationed in the Fortress of Antonia, which overlooked the temple courts, with stairs leading down into the courtyard. The commander immediately ran down with soldiers and centurions. When the crowd saw the soldiers, they stopped beating Paul.

The commander arrested Paul and ordered him to be bound with two chains, fulfilling Agabus's prophecy. He asked who Paul was and what he had done, but the crowd was shouting different things, so he ordered Paul taken into the barracks. The violence was so great that the soldiers had to carry Paul up the stairs. The crowd followed, shouting, [[v36|Away with him!]], the same cry the crowd had shouted at Jesus' trial (Luke 23:18).

### Paul Asks to Speak (21:37–40)

As Paul was about to be taken into the barracks, he asked the commander in Greek, [[v37|May I speak to you?]] The commander was surprised: [[v37|Do you know Greek?]] He had assumed Paul was [[v38|the Egyptian who before these days stirred up to sedition and led out into the wilderness the four thousand men of the Assassins]]. The Jewish historian Josephus also tells of an Egyptian false prophet who led a large group to the Mount of Olives, promising that Jerusalem's walls would fall at his command; the Roman governor Felix attacked them, and the Egyptian escaped.

Paul answered, [[v39|I am a Jew from Tarsus in Cilicia, a citizen of no insignificant city. I beg you, allow me to speak to the people]]. The commander gave permission. Paul, bruised and bound, stood on the stairs, motioned to the crowd, and when there was a great hush, he spoke to them [[v40|in the Hebrew language]], probably Aramaic, the everyday language of Jews in Judea. His speech follows in chapter 22.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 21:13–14 — Ready to Die for the Name of the Lord Jesus

verse: Acts 21:13-14

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was staying in Caesarea at the house of Philip the evangelist. The prophet Agabus had just bound his own hands and feet with Paul's belt, declaring that the Jews in Jerusalem would bind Paul and hand him over to the Gentiles. When Paul's companions, including Luke, and the local believers heard this, they [[v12|begged him not to go up to Jerusalem]]. They loved Paul and did not want him to suffer.

Paul's response was emotional but firm: [[v13|What are you doing, weeping and breaking my heart?]] Their tears made the decision harder, not because Paul was unsure, but because he loved them and their grief pained him. The word "breaking" suggests pounding or crushing, as if their pleas were weakening his resolve.

[[v13|For I am ready not only to be bound, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus]]. Paul was willing not only to be arrested, as Agabus had prophesied, but even to die. He was motivated by "the name of the Lord Jesus," his love and loyalty to Christ.

[[v14|When he would not be persuaded, we ceased, saying, “The Lord’s will be done.”]] When Paul could not be persuaded, his friends stopped pleading. They submitted to God's will, echoing the prayer of Jesus in Gethsemane: [[Luke 22:42|Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done]].

This scene shows that both Paul and his friends acted out of love. His friends loved Paul and wanted to protect him. Paul loved Christ and was willing to suffer for Him. In the end, they all trusted God's will.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the cost of discipleship. Jesus called His followers to take up their cross and follow Him (Luke 9:23). Paul was willing to do exactly that. He had told the Ephesian elders that he did not count his life as precious to himself (20:24). Now that conviction was tested.

They also show how believers can respond when they disagree about God's will. Paul's friends sincerely believed he should not go; Paul sincerely believed he must. They expressed their concerns honestly, but in the end they trusted God. Believers can hold their own views while submitting to God's sovereign will.

Finally, the phrase "The Lord's will be done" is a powerful expression of faith. It is not resignation but trust. It acknowledges that God's plans are wiser than human plans, even when they involve suffering.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers to consider their willingness to suffer for Christ. Most believers will not face death for their faith, though many around the world do. But all believers may face costs: loss of reputation, relationships, opportunities, or comfort. Paul's example encourages believers to be ready to follow Jesus whatever the cost.

They also encourage believers to support those who make costly decisions for Christ. Paul's friends, though they disagreed, eventually supported him with the words, "The Lord's will be done." Believers can stand with fellow believers who follow God's call, even when it is difficult.

Finally, these verses invite believers to pray, "The Lord's will be done," in their own lives. In times of uncertainty, illness, or difficult decisions, believers can entrust their lives to God's will, trusting that He is good and wise.

### Acts 21:19–20 — Glorifying God for What He Has Done

verse: Acts 21:19-20

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had arrived in Jerusalem with representatives of the Gentile churches, bringing a generous gift for the poor believers there. The next day, he met with James, the leader of the Jerusalem church, and all the elders.

[[v19|When he had greeted them, he reported one by one the things which God had worked among the Gentiles through his ministry]]. Paul did not boast about his own achievements. He reported what "God had worked" through him. He described in detail how Gentiles had come to faith, churches had been planted, and lives had been changed.

[[v20|They, when they heard it, glorified God]]. The Jerusalem leaders responded with praise. They rejoiced that God was saving Gentiles. This was a beautiful moment of unity between the Jewish church in Jerusalem and the Gentile mission.

But then they raised a concern: [[v20|You see, brother, how many thousands there are among the Jews of those who have believed, and they are all zealous for the law]]. Notice the word "brother." The leaders affirmed their fellowship with Paul. They also pointed out that thousands of Jewish believers in Jerusalem were devoted to the Law of Moses. They still circumcised their sons, kept the food laws, and observed the festivals, as expressions of their Jewish identity and faithfulness.

These Jewish believers had heard rumors that Paul taught Jews living among Gentiles to abandon Moses, stop circumcising their children, and forsake Jewish customs. This was not true. Paul taught that Gentiles did not need to keep the Law to be saved, but he did not forbid Jewish believers from keeping their customs.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the joy of the church when God works among the nations. The Jerusalem leaders glorified God for the Gentiles' salvation. Believers can rejoice whenever God works, wherever and through whomever He works.

They also show the challenge of unity in a diverse church. Jewish believers and Gentile believers had different practices and sensitivities. Rumors and misunderstandings threatened to divide them. Unity required honest conversation, mutual respect, and a willingness to address concerns.

Finally, these verses show the importance of giving God the credit. Paul reported what God had done, and the leaders glorified God. In the church, all fruitfulness comes from God, and He deserves the praise.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to celebrate what God is doing in other places and through other people. Hearing reports of God's work around the world, in other churches, or in other people's lives can lead believers to praise God.

They also encourage believers to address misunderstandings honestly. Rumors and false reports can damage relationships in the church. Believers can speak directly and graciously with one another to clear up misunderstandings.

Finally, these verses remind believers to respect differences within the church. Believers from different cultures, traditions, and backgrounds may practice their faith in different ways. As long as these practices do not contradict the gospel, believers can respect and honor one another's convictions.

### Acts 21:26 — Becoming as a Jew to the Jews

verse: Acts 21:26

#### Simple Explanation

James and the elders were concerned that Jewish believers in Jerusalem had heard false rumors that Paul taught Jews to abandon the Law of Moses. To address this, they made a suggestion. Four Jewish believers had taken a vow, probably a Nazirite vow, which involved abstaining from wine and not cutting one's hair for a period of time, ending with offerings at the temple and the shaving of the head (Numbers 6). The leaders asked Paul to join these men in their purification rites and pay for their offerings. [[v24|Then all will know that there is no truth in the things that they have been informed about you, but that you yourself also walk keeping the law]].

[[v26|Then Paul took the men, and the next day purified himself and went with them into the temple, declaring the fulfillment of the days of purification, until the offering was offered for every one of them]]. Paul agreed. He went through the purification ritual and supported the four men, showing publicly that he respected the Law and Jewish customs.

Some readers wonder whether Paul compromised his convictions. But this action was consistent with his principles. Paul taught that keeping the Law could not save anyone. But he never taught that Jewish believers had to stop observing their customs. He wrote, [[1 Corinthians 9:20|To the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might gain Jews; to those who are under the law, as under the law, that I might gain those who are under the law]]. Paul was willing to set aside his own freedom for the sake of unity and the gospel.

Paul had also previously taken a vow of his own (18:18), showing that he continued to value certain Jewish practices as expressions of devotion.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows Paul's flexibility for the sake of the gospel. Paul was firm on the essentials of the gospel: salvation by grace through faith, not by works of the Law. But he was flexible on matters of practice that did not compromise the gospel. He was willing to adapt his behavior to remove obstacles for others.

It also shows the importance of peace in the church. Paul cooperated with James and the elders to maintain unity between Jewish and Gentile believers. Paul wrote, [[Romans 12:18|If it is possible, as much as it is up to you, be at peace with all men]].

Finally, the verse shows that good intentions do not always prevent conflict. Paul's action was meant to calm tensions, but it ended up leading to his arrest. Believers can do what is right and wise, yet still face opposition. Faithfulness is not measured by outcomes but by obedience.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to be flexible in non-essential matters for the sake of others. Believers may have freedom in many areas, such as food, customs, or styles of worship. They can choose to set aside their preferences to build relationships and avoid unnecessary offense.

It also encourages believers to work for peace and unity in the church. Paul cooperated with the Jerusalem leaders to address misunderstanding. Believers can seek practical ways to build bridges between different groups in the church.

Finally, the verse reminds believers to distinguish between essential and non-essential matters. Believers must stand firm on the gospel itself, while being gracious and flexible on secondary issues.

### Acts 21:28–29 — A False Accusation

verse: Acts 21:28-29

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was in the temple completing the days of purification with the four men who had taken a vow. He was doing exactly what James and the elders had suggested, showing his respect for the Law and the temple. But some Jews from the province of Asia saw him there. They may have been from Ephesus, where Paul had ministered for nearly three years and faced opposition.

They stirred up the crowd and seized Paul, [[v28|crying out, “Men of Israel, help! This is the man who teaches all men everywhere against the people, and the law, and this place.]] These were serious charges: that Paul taught against the Jewish people, the Law of Moses, and the temple. They were similar to the charges against Stephen (6:13) and Jesus (Mark 14:58).

[[v28|Moreover, he also brought Greeks into the temple and has defiled this holy place!]] This was the most inflammatory charge. Gentiles were allowed in the outer Court of the Gentiles, but a stone barrier separated it from the inner courts. Inscriptions in Greek and Latin warned that any Gentile who passed beyond the barrier would be responsible for his own death. Two of these warning inscriptions have been found by archaeologists.

Luke explains the reason for the accusation: [[v29|For they had seen Trophimus the Ephesian with him in the city, and they supposed that Paul had brought him into the temple]]. Trophimus was a Gentile believer from Ephesus who had traveled with Paul (20:4). The accusers had seen Paul with Trophimus in the city and assumed, without evidence, that Paul had brought him into the temple. It was false, but it was enough to start a riot.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the danger of false assumptions and accusations. The accusers jumped to conclusions without checking the facts. Scripture warns against spreading false reports (Exodus 23:1) and calls believers to judge rightly (John 7:24). Rumors and assumptions can do tremendous harm.

They also show that faithful believers may face unjust accusations, as Jesus did. Jesus warned, [[Matthew 5:11|Blessed are you when people reproach you, persecute you, and say all kinds of evil against you falsely, for my sake]].

Finally, there is a deep irony in the accusation. Paul was accused of bringing a Gentile past the barrier that separated Jews and Gentiles in the temple. Paul later wrote that Christ [[Ephesians 2:14|broke down the middle wall of separation]] between Jews and Gentiles. Spiritually, Paul did proclaim that in Christ the barrier was removed, not by defiling the temple, but through the cross.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses warn believers against making assumptions about others. It is easy to jump to conclusions based on partial information. Believers can be careful to seek the truth before speaking or acting, and to refuse to spread rumors.

They also encourage believers who face false accusations. When believers are misunderstood or falsely accused, they can trust that God knows the truth and will vindicate them. Paul faced false accusations repeatedly, yet God used his trials to advance the gospel.

Finally, these verses remind believers of the unity that Christ brings. The barriers that once divided people have been broken down in Christ. Believers can live out this unity by welcoming people of every background into the fellowship of the church.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 21 shows a disciple who was ready to suffer and even die for Jesus. Despite warnings and the tears of his friends, Paul went to Jerusalem. Believers today can ask God for the same readiness to follow Him whatever the cost, and can support one another with the prayer, "The Lord's will be done."

The chapter also shows the beauty of Christian fellowship. Believers in Tyre knelt and prayed with Paul on the beach. Philip welcomed him into his home. The Jerusalem church received him gladly. Believers can show the same love and hospitality to fellow believers, even those they have just met.

Acts 21 models wisdom in maintaining unity. Paul reported what God had done, the Jerusalem leaders glorified God, and Paul willingly joined in a purification rite to address misunderstandings. Believers can celebrate God's work, address concerns honestly, and be flexible in non-essential matters for the sake of peace.

Finally, the chapter shows that faithful believers may face unjust accusations. Paul was falsely accused and nearly killed, yet God protected him through the Roman soldiers. Believers can refuse to spread rumors, seek the truth, and trust God when they themselves are misunderstood, knowing that He can use even injustice to advance His purposes.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, give me the courage of Paul, who was ready not only to be bound but to die for Your name. When following You is costly, help me to trust You and to say with Your people, "The Lord's will be done." Thank You for the love of believers who pray together, open their homes, and welcome one another. Help me to show that same love. Teach me to rejoice whenever You are at work, and to give You the glory. Give me wisdom to be flexible in things that do not matter and firm in the truth of the gospel. Guard my tongue from spreading rumors or making false assumptions about others. When I am misunderstood or falsely accused, help me to trust You to bring the truth to light. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
