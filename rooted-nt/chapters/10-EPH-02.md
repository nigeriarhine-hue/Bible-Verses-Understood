# Ephesians 2
theme: Saved by Grace and Made One in Christ
main: Paul describes the human condition apart from God: dead in sins, following the ways of the world and the evil one, and by nature under God's wrath. But God, rich in mercy and great in love, made believers alive with Christ, raised them up, and seated them with Him. Salvation is by grace through faith, a gift of God and not the result of works, and believers are God's workmanship created for good works. Paul then shows that Christ has broken down the dividing wall between Jews and Gentiles, making peace through the cross and creating one new people. Gentile believers are no longer strangers but fellow citizens and members of God's household, being built together into a holy temple where God lives by His Spirit.

## Chapter Background and Story

In chapter 1, Paul celebrated God's eternal plan and prayed that believers would know the immense power that raised Christ from the dead. In chapter 2, he shows that same power at work in two ways. First, God raised individual believers from spiritual death to life (2:1–10). Second, God brought together two groups that had been deeply divided, Jews and Gentiles, into one new people (2:11–22).

The division between Jews and Gentiles was one of the deepest in the ancient world. It was religious, cultural, and social. In the temple in Jerusalem, a stone barrier separated the Court of the Gentiles from the inner courts, with inscriptions warning that any Gentile who passed beyond it would be put to death. Paul himself had been arrested after being falsely accused of bringing a Gentile, Trophimus the Ephesian, past that barrier (Acts 21:27–29). When Paul writes of Christ breaking down "the middle wall of separation," his readers in Ephesus would have known exactly the kind of wall he meant.

This chapter contains one of the most famous summaries of the gospel in the Bible (2:8–9) and one of the most powerful visions of reconciliation between peoples.

### Dead in Sins (2:1–3)

[[v1|You were made alive when you were dead in transgressions and sins]]. Paul's description of life apart from God is stark. People were not merely sick or weak; they were spiritually dead, unable to respond to God on their own.

[[v2|in which you once walked according to the course of this world, according to the prince of the power of the air, the spirit who now works in the children of disobedience]]. Three forces held people captive. "The course of this world" refers to the values and patterns of a society that has turned from God. "The prince of the power of the air" refers to Satan, whom ancient people pictured as ruling the realm between earth and heaven. He is "the spirit who now works" in those who disobey God.

[[v3|We also all once lived among them in the lusts of our flesh, doing the desires of the flesh and of the mind, and were by nature children of wrath, even as the rest]]. The third force is the flesh, the sinful human nature turned in on itself. Paul includes himself and other Jewish believers ("we also all"). Everyone, Jew and Gentile alike, was under God's righteous judgment. "Children of wrath" means people who, by their sinful condition, deserved God's judgment against sin.

### But God (2:4–7)

Two of the most hopeful words in Scripture begin verse 4. God's merciful action is studied below. [[v6|and raised us up with him, and made us to sit with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus]]. Believers are united with Christ not only in His resurrection but also in His ascension. In a spiritual sense, they already share His victory and His place of authority. [[v7|that in the ages to come he might show the exceeding riches of his grace in kindness toward us in Christ Jesus]]. Throughout eternity, God's people will be living displays of His grace and kindness.

### By Grace Through Faith (2:8–10)

These central verses are studied below.

### Strangers Brought Near (2:11–13)

[[v11|Therefore remember that once you, the Gentiles in the flesh, who are called “uncircumcision” by that which is called “circumcision” (in the flesh, made by hands)]]. Paul asks the Gentile believers to remember where they came from. Jews often referred to Gentiles dismissively as "the uncircumcision."

[[v12|that you were at that time separate from Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of the promise, having no hope and without God in the world]]. Paul lists five losses. Gentiles had been without the Messiah, outside God's covenant people, strangers to His promises, without hope, and without God. They had many gods, but not the true God.

[[v13|But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off are made near in the blood of Christ]]. Another great turning point. Through Christ's death, those who were far from God have been brought near.

### Christ Our Peace (2:14–18)

How Christ made peace between Jew and Gentile is studied below. [[v17|He came and preached peace to you who were far off and to those who were near]]. Paul echoes Isaiah 57:19, where God promises peace to those far and near. Through the apostles' preaching, the risen Christ continued to proclaim peace. [[v18|For through him we both have our access in one Spirit to the Father]]. Jew and Gentile now approach God on the same basis, through the same Son, by the same Spirit, to the same Father.

### God's Household and Temple (2:19–22)

[[v19|So then you are no longer strangers and foreigners, but you are fellow citizens with the saints and of the household of God]]. Gentile believers are no longer outsiders or resident aliens with limited rights. They are full citizens and family members.

[[v20|being built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone]]. The church rests on the teaching of the apostles and prophets. "Prophets" here probably refers to New Testament prophets, since they are listed after the apostles (see Ephesians 3:5; 4:11). Christ is the cornerstone, the stone that determined the position and alignment of the whole building (Psalm 118:22; 1 Peter 2:6).

[[v21|in whom the whole building, fitted together, grows into a holy temple in the Lord]]; [[v22|in whom you also are built together for a habitation of God in the Spirit]]. Ephesus had the magnificent temple of Artemis. Jerusalem had God's temple, with its barrier that excluded Gentiles. Paul announces a new temple, made not of stone but of people, Jew and Gentile together. God Himself lives in it by His Spirit. And it is still growing, as more people are added and the church matures.

## Important Verses Explained

### Ephesians 2:4–5 — But God, Rich in Mercy

verse: Ephesians 2:4-5

#### Simple Explanation

After describing humanity's desperate condition, dead in sins, enslaved, and under judgment, Paul turns with two powerful words: "But God." Everything changes because of God's action.

Paul describes God's character in three ways. He is "rich in mercy." Mercy is compassion toward those in misery, withholding the punishment they deserve. God has an abundance of it. His action flows "for his great love with which he loved us." God's love was the motive. And this love reached people "even when we were dead through our trespasses." God did not wait for people to improve or reach out to Him. He loved them while they were still dead in sin.

What did God do? He "made us alive together with Christ." Just as God raised Jesus from physical death, He raised believers from spiritual death. They were given new life, the very life of the risen Christ.

Paul cannot resist adding a parenthetical exclamation: "by grace you have been saved." He will repeat it in verse 8. Grace means God's undeserved favor. Dead people cannot raise themselves. Salvation must be entirely God's gift.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The phrase "But God" appears at turning points throughout Scripture. Joseph told his brothers, [[Genesis 50:20|you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good]]. Paul wrote in Romans 5:8, [[Romans 5:8|But God commends his own love toward us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us]]. These words mark the moment when human hopelessness meets divine intervention.

The description of humanity as "dead" is significant for understanding grace. If people were merely sick, they might cooperate with a doctor. If they were drowning, they might reach for a rope. But the dead can do nothing. Christian traditions have discussed how this truth relates to human response to the gospel, but all agree that new life begins with God's initiative. Faith itself is a response made possible by His grace.

That God [[v5|made us alive together with Christ]] also points to union with Christ. What happened to Christ, death, resurrection, and ascension, happens to believers in Him. They share His life now and will share His glory in the future.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

For anyone who feels spiritually lifeless, trapped in patterns of sin, or hopeless about change, these verses proclaim good news. God specializes in bringing the dead to life. No one is too far gone for His mercy.

These verses also humble believers. Their new life is entirely a gift. They did not raise themselves. That truth guards against pride and cultivates gratitude and compassion toward those who have not yet experienced God's grace. Every believer was once dead in sin; the difference is God's mercy.

### Ephesians 2:8–9 — Saved by Grace Through Faith

verse: Ephesians 2:8-9

#### Simple Explanation

These are among the most well-known verses in the Bible. Paul repeats what he said in verse 5, "by grace you have been saved," and now he explains it more fully.

"By grace": salvation comes from God's free, undeserved favor. It is not earned, purchased, or achieved.

"You have been saved": in Greek this verb describes a completed action with lasting results. Believers have been rescued and remain saved.

"Through faith": faith is the means by which people receive God's gift. Faith is not a work that earns salvation; it is the empty hand that receives it, trusting in Christ.

"And that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God." The word "that" likely refers to the whole package of salvation by grace through faith. None of it originates from human effort. It is entirely God's gift.

"Not of works, that no one would boast." If salvation came by works, people could take credit for it. But because it is a gift, no one can boast before God. Everyone stands on the same ground, receiving mercy they did not deserve.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses capture the heart of the gospel that Paul preached everywhere. They echo his teaching in Romans 3:23–28 and Galatians 2:16. Salvation is not a reward for good behavior or religious devotion. It is a rescue from God, received through trust in Christ.

Different Christian traditions have explored how faith itself relates to God's grace. Some emphasize that even faith is God's gift, produced by the Spirit. Others emphasize that God enables people to respond freely in faith. Both agree that faith is not a meritorious work and that the credit for salvation belongs to God alone.

The phrase "that no one would boast" shows the purpose of grace. God designed salvation in a way that removes all human pride. This is fitting, because pride was at the root of humanity's original rebellion. Grace levels everyone. The most moral person and the most notorious sinner come to God the same way: as recipients of mercy.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people, including many who attend church, believe deep down that they must earn God's approval. They try to balance their bad deeds with good ones or measure their standing by how religious they feel. These verses bring freedom: salvation is a gift to be received, not a wage to be earned.

This truth also brings stability. If salvation depended on human performance, it would rise and fall with each success and failure. But because it rests on God's grace, believers can have assurance even on their worst days.

Finally, these verses shape how believers see others. No one has grounds for spiritual superiority. Those who have received grace are called to extend it humbly to others, remembering where they came from.

### Ephesians 2:10 — Created for Good Works

verse: Ephesians 2:10

#### Simple Explanation

Right after saying that salvation is "not of works," Paul explains where good works fit in. "For we are his workmanship." The word for "workmanship" can mean something made, a creation, or even a work of art. It is the root of the English word "poem." Believers are God's masterpiece, carefully crafted by Him.

"Created in Christ Jesus." Salvation is a new creation. God has not just patched up the old life; He has created something new in Christ (2 Corinthians 5:17).

"For good works." Good works are not the root of salvation, but they are its fruit. God saved people so that they would do good. Salvation and good works go together, but in the right order.

"Which God prepared before that we would walk in them." God has already prepared good works for His people to do. Like a path laid out ahead of time, these works are part of His plan. Believers simply need to walk in them.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Verses 8–10 hold together two truths that are sometimes separated. Salvation is entirely by grace, not by works. And salvation results in good works. Those who emphasize only the first may neglect holiness and service. Those who emphasize only the second may fall into trying to earn salvation. Paul keeps both together. James makes a complementary point when he says that faith without works is dead (James 2:17). Genuine faith always produces a changed life.

The idea that God has prepared good works in advance is deeply encouraging. It means that believers' lives have purpose. Every act of kindness, faithfulness, service, and love can be part of what God planned. This does not mean every detail is scripted, but it does mean that God has a purpose for each believer's life and equips them to fulfill it.

Being God's "workmanship" also speaks of dignity. Each believer is a work of the divine Artist. God is shaping them, sometimes through difficult seasons, into something beautiful that displays His glory.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people wonder whether their lives matter. This verse declares that believers are God's handiwork, created for a purpose. Their everyday lives, at home, at work, in their neighborhoods, can be filled with good works God has prepared for them.

This verse also offers freedom. Believers do not need to strive anxiously to find significant things to do. They can walk day by day in the path God prepares, paying attention to the opportunities He places before them, such as a neighbor in need, a coworker who is struggling, or a chance to show kindness.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that they are works in progress. God is still shaping them. When they feel unfinished or flawed, they can trust the skill and patience of the Artist.

### Ephesians 2:14–16 — He Is Our Peace

verse: Ephesians 2:14-16

#### Simple Explanation

Paul now explains how Jew and Gentile were brought together. "For he is our peace." Christ does not merely bring peace; He Himself is peace. In His person and work, He reconciles people to God and to one another.

He "made both one, and broke down the middle wall of separation." The two groups, Jews and Gentiles, have become one. The "middle wall of separation" was the barrier of hostility between them, symbolized by the literal wall in the temple courts that kept Gentiles out.

Verse 15 explains how: He abolished "in his flesh the hostility, the law of commandments contained in ordinances." Through His death, Christ set aside the Law as a system of regulations that separated Jews from Gentiles, such as circumcision, food laws, and purity rules. These had marked Israel as distinct, but they had also become a source of division and hostility.

The purpose: "that he might create in himself one new man of the two, making peace." Christ did not make Gentiles into Jews or Jews into Gentiles. He created a new humanity, a new people that includes both.

Verse 16 adds that He reconciled "them both in one body to God through the cross, having killed the hostility through it." The cross reconciles people to God and to each other at the same time. On the cross, hostility itself was put to death.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage shows that the gospel has both vertical and horizontal dimensions. Reconciliation with God leads to reconciliation among people. Those who are at peace with God through Christ are called to live at peace with one another. The church is meant to be a community where ancient divisions are overcome.

Paul's words about the Law need careful reading. He is not saying that God's moral commands have been abolished. Elsewhere, he affirms that the Law is holy and that love fulfills it (Romans 7:12; 13:8–10). What Christ abolished was the Law as a dividing wall, the system of ceremonial regulations that separated Jews and Gentiles and could not make anyone right with God.

"One new man" is a remarkable phrase. The church is not just an organization or a mixture of groups; it is a new humanity created in Christ. This new humanity reflects God's original purpose for the human race, now restored. Ethnic and cultural identities remain, but they no longer divide or determine standing before God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Divisions between ethnic groups, nations, classes, and political tribes remain deep in today's world, and sometimes they run through churches. This passage calls believers to see these divisions in the light of the cross. Christ has broken down the dividing walls. Churches that welcome people of every background into one family display the power of the gospel.

This calling is practical. It may involve building friendships across cultural lines, listening to the experiences of believers from different backgrounds, repenting of prejudice, and working for justice and reconciliation. It also involves humility, recognizing that every group stands equally in need of grace.

On a personal level, Christ is also the source of peace in strained relationships. Those who have been reconciled to God through the cross can seek reconciliation with others, as far as it is possible and safe, trusting that Christ is able to bring peace where hostility once ruled.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Ephesians 2 tells the story of salvation in two great movements. The first is personal. Every human being, apart from God, was spiritually dead, enslaved to the world, the devil, and the flesh, and under judgment. "But God," rich in mercy and great in love, made believers alive with Christ and raised them up with Him. This is entirely a gift of grace, received through faith, not earned by works. No one can boast; everyone stands in need of mercy. Believers can rest in the security of this gift, and they can extend the same grace humbly to others.

At the same time, grace is not idle. Believers are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works that God has prepared. Their daily lives become the setting for acts of love and service that reflect His character. Salvation by grace leads naturally to a life of good works.

The second movement is communal. Christ has broken down the wall of hostility between Jews and Gentiles, creating one new people and reconciling both to God through the cross. Those who were once strangers are now fellow citizens and members of God's household. The church is a living temple, built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, with Christ as the cornerstone, and God dwells within it by His Spirit.

This vision calls the church to be a place where every dividing wall comes down. Believers are invited to welcome one another across lines of ethnicity, culture, class, and background, to pursue reconciliation, and to display to the world the peace that only Christ can bring.

## Chapter Prayer

Gracious God, I was dead in my sins, but You, rich in mercy and great in love, made me alive with Christ. Thank You that I have been saved by grace through faith, not by my own works, so that I have nothing to boast of but You. Thank You that I am Your workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works You prepared beforehand. Help me to walk in them each day. Lord Jesus, You are our peace. You broke down the dividing wall and made us one new people through Your cross. Tear down every wall of hostility in my heart. Help me to welcome all whom You have welcomed, and to seek peace and reconciliation wherever I can. Build Your church into a holy temple where You are pleased to live by Your Spirit. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
