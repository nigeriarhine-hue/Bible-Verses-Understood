# Luke 22
theme: The Last Supper, the Garden, and the Look That Broke Peter's Heart
main: As Passover arrives, Satan enters Judas, who agrees to betray Jesus. At the Last Supper, Jesus gives the bread and cup as signs of His body given and His blood poured out for His disciples, and when they argue about greatness, He reminds them that He is among them as one who serves. He tells Peter that He has prayed for him in the coming test, prays in agony on the Mount of Olives, heals a servant's ear at His arrest, and, after Peter's denials, turns and looks at Peter, who goes out and weeps bitterly.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 22 begins the account of Jesus' suffering and death. Throughout his Gospel, Luke has shown Jesus traveling toward Jerusalem with determination, knowing what awaited Him there (Luke 9:51). Now the time has come. Luke's account parallels Matthew 26 and Mark 14, but it includes many distinctive details: Satan entering Judas, Jesus' longing to eat the Passover, His teaching about service at the table, His prayer for Peter, the angel strengthening Him in the garden, His healing of the servant's ear, and His look at Peter. (The anointing, the institution of the Lord's Supper, Gethsemane, and Peter's denials are studied in Mark 14:24, 14:36, 14:38, 14:72 and Matthew 26:28, 26:39, 26:52.)

### The Plot and Judas's Betrayal (22:1–6)

The Feast of Unleavened Bread, called the Passover, was approaching. Jerusalem was crowded with pilgrims. The chief priests and scribes were looking for a way to kill Jesus, but they feared the people.

Luke writes, [[v3|Satan entered into Judas, who was also called Iscariot, who was counted with the twelve]]. This recalls Luke 4:13, where the devil left Jesus after the temptations [[Luke 4:13|until another time]]. That time had now come. Luke does not excuse Judas; he chose to go to the chief priests and temple officers and discuss how to hand Jesus over. But Luke shows that the betrayal was part of a larger spiritual battle. The leaders were glad and agreed to give Judas money. He looked for an opportunity to betray Jesus when no crowd was present.

### Preparing the Passover (22:7–13)

On the day when the Passover lamb had to be sacrificed, Jesus sent Peter and John to prepare the meal. He told them they would meet a man carrying a jar of water, which would have been unusual since women usually carried water. They were to follow him to a house and ask the owner for the guest room. The owner would show them a large, furnished upper room. They found everything as Jesus had said and prepared the Passover.

### The Last Supper (22:14–23)

When the hour came, Jesus reclined at the table with the apostles. He said, [[v15|I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer]]. The Greek phrase expresses deep longing: "With desire I have desired." Jesus had looked forward to this final meal with His friends. He said He would not eat it again until it was fulfilled in God's kingdom.

Luke's account mentions two cups. The Passover meal included several cups of wine. Jesus took a cup, gave thanks, and told them to share it, saying He would not drink of the fruit of the vine again until God's kingdom came. Then He took bread, gave thanks, broke it, and gave it to them, saying, [[v19|This is my body which is given for you. Do this in memory of me]]. This is studied below. After supper, He took the cup and said, [[v20|This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you]]. (Some early manuscripts omit part of verses 19–20, but most include them, and Paul's account in 1 Corinthians 11:23–25 closely matches Luke's.)

Then Jesus said that the hand of His betrayer was with Him on the table. The Son of Man would go as God had determined, but woe to the one who betrayed Him. The disciples began to ask each other which of them it could be.

### The Dispute About Greatness (22:24–30)

Shockingly, right after the Last Supper, an argument arose among the disciples about which of them was the greatest. Jesus answered that the kings of the Gentiles lord it over their people, and those in authority call themselves [[v25|benefactors]], a title rulers used to advertise their generosity. [[v26|But not so with you]]. The greatest should be like the youngest, and the leader like one who serves. This is studied below.

Then Jesus spoke kindly to them: [[v28|But you are those who have continued with me in my trials]]. Despite their failures, they had stayed with Him. He promised them a kingdom, that they would eat and drink at His table and sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel.

### Jesus Prays for Peter (22:31–38)

Then Jesus spoke directly to Peter: [[v31|Simon, Simon, behold, Satan asked to have all of you, that he might sift you as wheat]]. This verse and the next are studied below. Peter declared, [[v33|Lord, I am ready to go with you both to prison and to death!]] Jesus told him that before the rooster crowed that day, he would deny three times that he knew Him.

Jesus then reminded them that when He sent them out without purse, bag, or sandals, they lacked nothing (Luke 9:3; 10:4). [[v36|But now]], He said, things would be different. They should take a purse and bag, and whoever had no sword should sell his cloak and buy one. He explained that the Scripture [[v37|He was counted with transgressors]] (Isaiah 53:12) must be fulfilled in Him. The disciples said, "Lord, here are two swords," and He replied, [[v38|That is enough]].

This passage is often misunderstood. Jesus was not instructing His disciples to take up arms; a few hours later He rebuked the use of the sword and healed the man who was struck (vv. 49–51). Many interpreters understand His words as a vivid way of saying that times of hostility were coming, when His followers would no longer be welcomed and would need to provide for themselves. When the disciples took Him literally and produced two swords, His reply, "That is enough," may mean "Enough of this talk," ending a conversation they had misunderstood.

### Prayer on the Mount of Olives (22:39–46)

Jesus went out, [[v39|as his custom was]], to the Mount of Olives, and His disciples followed. Luke does not name Gethsemane but notes that Jesus regularly went there. He told them, [[v40|Pray that you don’t enter into temptation]]. He withdrew about a stone's throw, knelt down, and prayed, [[v42|Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done]]. (This prayer is studied at Mark 14:36 and Matthew 26:39.)

Luke alone records, [[v43|An angel from heaven appeared to him, strengthening him]]. [[v44|Being in agony, he prayed more earnestly. His sweat became like great drops of blood falling down on the ground]]. (Some early manuscripts do not include verses 43–44, but many do, and they were known to early Christian writers in the second century.) Luke the physician describes Jesus' intense distress. The phrase "like great drops of blood" may mean that His sweat fell like drops of blood, or it may describe a rare medical condition in which extreme stress causes blood vessels to rupture into sweat glands. Either way, Luke shows the depth of Jesus' anguish.

When Jesus rose from prayer, He found the disciples asleep, [[v45|because of grief]]. Luke gently explains their sleep as a result of sorrow. Jesus said, [[v46|Why do you sleep? Rise and pray that you may not enter into temptation]].

### The Arrest (22:47–53)

While He was still speaking, a crowd arrived, led by Judas. He approached Jesus to kiss Him. Jesus said, [[v48|Judas, do you betray the Son of Man with a kiss?]] A kiss was a sign of friendship and respect; Judas used it as a signal for betrayal.

The disciples asked, [[v49|Lord, shall we strike with the sword?]] Before Jesus could answer, one of them struck the high priest's servant and cut off his right ear. Jesus said, [[v51|Let me at least do this]] (or "No more of this!"), and He touched the man's ear and healed him. Luke alone records this healing. Even while being arrested, Jesus showed mercy to one of those arresting Him.

Jesus said to the chief priests, temple officers, and elders who had come for Him that they had not arrested Him when He taught daily in the temple. [[v53|But this is your hour, and the power of darkness]]. For a short time, evil would seem to triumph.

### Peter's Denials and Jesus' Look (22:54–62)

They seized Jesus and took Him to the high priest's house. Peter followed at a distance. In the courtyard, people lit a fire and sat around it, and Peter sat among them. A servant girl looked closely at him in the firelight and said, [[v56|This man also was with him]]. Peter denied it: [[v57|Woman, I don’t know him]]. A little later someone else said he was one of them, and Peter said, [[v58|Man, I am not!]] About an hour later another insisted that he was a Galilean. Peter said, [[v60|Man, I don’t know what you are talking about!]] Immediately, while he was still speaking, a rooster crowed.

Then comes a detail found only in Luke: [[v61|The Lord turned and looked at Peter]]. This is studied below. Peter remembered Jesus' words, went out, and wept bitterly.

### Jesus Before the Council (22:63–71)

The men guarding Jesus mocked and beat Him. They blindfolded Him, struck Him, and demanded, [[v64|Prophesy! Who is the one who struck you?]] At daybreak, the council of elders, chief priests, and scribes gathered and questioned Him. They asked whether He was the Christ. He said that if He told them, they would not believe. Then He declared, [[v69|From now on, the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of the power of God]], echoing Psalm 110:1 and Daniel 7:13. They asked, "Are you then the Son of God?" He answered, [[v70|You say it, because I am]]. They concluded they needed no more witnesses.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 22:19 — "Do This in Memory of Me"

verse: Luke 22:19

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records the moment Jesus gave the bread to His disciples during the Last Supper. It was Passover, the annual festival when Jews remembered God's deliverance of Israel from slavery in Egypt. The Passover meal included unleavened bread, bitter herbs, wine, and the Passover lamb, each with symbolic meaning, and the father or leader explained them as part of retelling the story of the exodus.

Jesus had told His disciples that He had eagerly desired to eat this Passover with them before He suffered. During the meal, [[v19|He took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke and gave it to them]]. These actions, taking, giving thanks, breaking, and giving, echo how Jesus fed the five thousand (Luke 9:16) and how He would break bread with the two disciples at Emmaus after His resurrection (Luke 24:30).

Then He said, [[v19|This is my body which is given for you]]. Jesus gave new meaning to the Passover bread. It now represented His own body, which would be given in death the next day. "For you" emphasizes that His death would be on behalf of His disciples, for their benefit. Luke alone records the words "which is given for you" in connection with the bread.

[[v19|Do this in memory of me]]. Jesus commanded His disciples to continue this practice. Just as Passover helped Israel remember the exodus, this meal would help Jesus' followers remember His death and the new deliverance He accomplished. "Memory" in the Bible is more than mental recall. It means bringing a past event into the present so that it shapes life now. When believers eat the bread, they remember and proclaim His saving death.

Paul's account of the Lord's Supper, written in the 50s A.D., includes these same words (1 Corinthians 11:24), showing that the early church practiced this meal from the beginning.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that Jesus' death was a voluntary gift. His body was "given," not merely taken. He gave Himself for His people. Isaiah 53 describes the Servant who poured out His life for others, and Jesus saw Himself fulfilling that role.

It also shows that the Lord's Supper is central to Christian worship. From the earliest days, believers gathered to break bread (Acts 2:42). Christians have understood the Lord's Supper in different ways: some believe Christ is truly present in the bread and wine, some emphasize a spiritual presence received by faith, and some see it primarily as a memorial. All agree that it calls believers to remember Jesus' sacrifice and to receive it with gratitude.

Finally, the command "Do this in memory of me" shows that remembering Jesus' death is essential to the life of faith. Believers are prone to forget. Regular participation in the Lord's Supper keeps the cross at the center of their faith.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to participate in the Lord's Supper with reverence and gratitude. Each time they take the bread, they can remember that Jesus gave His body for them personally. This remembrance can deepen love for Him and renew commitment to follow Him.

It also encourages believers to make remembrance of Jesus' sacrifice a regular part of their lives, not only in church but in daily reflection. Remembering the cross can bring comfort in guilt, humility in pride, and courage in difficulty.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that the Lord's Supper is shared. Jesus gave the bread to all His disciples, including those who would fail Him that very night. It is a meal of grace for imperfect people, uniting believers as one body in Christ.

### Luke 22:27 — "I Am Among You as One Who Serves"

verse: Luke 22:27

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes in a surprising moment. Jesus had just shared the Last Supper with His disciples, giving them the bread and cup as signs of His body and blood. He had told them that one of them would betray Him. And immediately afterward, [[v24|A dispute also arose among them, which of them was considered to be greatest]]. While Jesus was facing death, His disciples were arguing about rank.

Jesus responded by contrasting the world's way of leadership with His kingdom's way. The kings of the Gentiles lord it over others, and those in authority call themselves "benefactors," taking credit for their supposed generosity. [[v26|But not so with you]]. The greatest among them should become like the youngest, who in that culture had the least status, and the leader should be like one who serves.

Then Jesus asked a question: [[v27|For who is greater, one who sits at the table, or one who serves?]] In that culture, the answer was obvious: the one reclining at the table, being served, was greater than the servant waiting on him. Jesus acknowledged this: [[v27|Isn’t it he who sits at the table?]]

Then He pointed to Himself: [[v27|But I am among you as one who serves]]. Jesus, the Lord and Teacher, the King who would be seated at the right hand of God, had taken the role of a servant. John's Gospel tells us that at this same meal, Jesus washed His disciples' feet, a task assigned to the lowest servant (John 13:1–17). And within hours, He would give His life for them.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of Jesus' mission. He came not to be served but to serve and to give His life (Mark 10:45, studied at Matthew 20:28). His whole life was service, culminating in the cross. Paul describes Jesus as taking the form of a servant and humbling Himself to death on a cross (Philippians 2:7–8).

It also reveals how slow the disciples were to understand. Even at the Last Supper, they were competing for status. Jesus did not reject them; He patiently taught them again, and He commended them for having stayed with Him in His trials. Grace meets people in their failures.

Finally, this verse redefines greatness for all believers. In God's kingdom, greatness is measured by service. Leaders are servants. This turns upside down the world's understanding of power and status.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to follow Jesus' example of service. In families, workplaces, churches, and communities, they can look for ways to serve others rather than seeking recognition or status. This may mean doing humble tasks, putting others' needs first, or using authority to help rather than to dominate.

It especially challenges leaders. Pastors, parents, managers, and others in authority are called to lead as servants, not as people who expect to be served. Christian leadership is measured by how well leaders care for others.

Finally, this verse invites believers to receive Jesus' service. He is still "among you as one who serves." Believers can come to Him with their needs, trusting that He gives Himself for their good.

### Luke 22:31–32 — "I Prayed for You That Your Faith Wouldn't Fail"

verse: Luke 22:31-32

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to Peter during the Last Supper, shortly before His arrest. The disciples had just been arguing about who was the greatest. Jesus had taught them about servant leadership and promised them a place in His kingdom. Now He turned to Peter with a warning and a promise.

[[v31|Simon, Simon]]. Jesus repeated his name, a sign of deep concern and affection. He used his original name, Simon, rather than Peter, "rock," perhaps hinting at the weakness Peter was about to show.

[[v31|behold, Satan asked to have all of you, that he might sift you as wheat]]. The word "you" here is plural in Greek, meaning all the disciples. Satan had demanded permission to test them severely. This recalls the book of Job, where Satan asked permission to test Job (Job 1:6–12). "Sift as wheat" pictures grain being shaken violently in a sieve to separate it from chaff. The disciples were about to be shaken by fear, confusion, and failure.

[[v32|but I prayed for you, that your faith wouldn’t fail]]. Here "you" is singular, referring to Peter specifically. Jesus had prayed for Peter personally. He did not pray that Peter would avoid the test or never fail. He prayed that Peter's faith would not fail completely, that even after falling, he would not abandon his faith altogether.

[[v32|You, when once you have turned again, establish your brothers]]. Jesus knew Peter would fall, but He also knew Peter would return. "Turned again" means repent and come back. Then Peter would have a responsibility: to strengthen the other disciples. His experience of failure and restoration would equip him to help others.

Peter responded with confidence: "Lord, I am ready to go with you both to prison and to death!" Jesus told him that before the rooster crowed, he would deny Him three times. Within hours, it happened.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal that believers face real spiritual opposition. Satan seeks to shake and destroy faith. Peter writes later, [[1 Peter 5:8|Be sober and self-controlled. Be watchful. Your adversary, the devil, walks around like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour]]. Peter knew from experience what it meant to be sifted.

They also reveal Jesus' intercession for His people. Jesus prayed for Peter before Peter failed. His prayer was the reason Peter's faith did not ultimately collapse. The New Testament teaches that the risen Jesus continues to intercede for believers (Romans 8:34; Hebrews 7:25). Believers' perseverance depends not only on their own strength but on Christ's prayers for them.

Finally, the verses show that failure is not the end of usefulness. Jesus expected Peter to fail, but He also expected him to return and to strengthen others. Peter became a leader of the early church, preaching at Pentecost and caring for believers. His failure made him humble and compassionate.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers facing spiritual testing. Trials, temptations, and doubts can shake faith severely. Believers can take comfort that Jesus is praying for them, that their faith may not fail.

They also offer hope to those who have failed. Like Peter, believers may have denied Jesus in word or action. Jesus' words show that failure does not have to be final. They can turn back to Him, receive forgiveness, and continue following Him.

Finally, these verses call restored believers to strengthen others. Those who have experienced failure and God's restoring grace are often best equipped to help others who are struggling. Believers can use their own stories to encourage, support, and strengthen fellow believers.

### Luke 22:61 — The Lord Turned and Looked at Peter

verse: Luke 22:61

#### Simple Explanation

This verse, found only in Luke, describes the moment immediately after Peter's third denial. Jesus had been arrested on the Mount of Olives and taken to the high priest's house. Peter followed at a distance and sat with others around a fire in the courtyard. A servant girl recognized him and said he had been with Jesus. Peter denied it. A little later, someone else accused him, and he denied it again. About an hour later, another person insisted that Peter was a Galilean and had been with Jesus. Peter said, [[v60|Man, I don’t know what you are talking about!]] Immediately, while he was still speaking, a rooster crowed.

Then [[v61|The Lord turned and looked at Peter]]. Jesus was apparently being held somewhere within sight of the courtyard, perhaps being led from one place to another in the high priest's house. At the very moment of Peter's third denial and the rooster's crow, Jesus turned and looked at him.

Luke does not describe Jesus' expression. But the look was enough. [[v61|Then Peter remembered the Lord’s word, how he said to him, “Before the rooster crows you will deny me three times.”]] Only a few hours earlier, Peter had boldly declared that he was ready to go with Jesus to prison and death. Jesus had warned him he would deny Him three times. Now it had happened, exactly as Jesus said.

[[v62|He went out, and wept bitterly]]. Peter left the courtyard and broke down in tears. His weeping showed genuine sorrow and grief over what he had done.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that Jesus knows His followers' failures. He saw Peter at the very moment of his denial. Nothing is hidden from Him. Yet His look seems to have been one of sorrow and love rather than anger. Jesus had already prayed for Peter (v. 32) and had already told him that he would turn back. The look was an invitation to remember Jesus' words, both the warning and the promise.

It also shows how conviction can lead to repentance. Peter's tears were the beginning of his restoration. Unlike Judas, whose remorse led to despair (Matthew 27:3–5), Peter's grief led him back to Jesus. After the resurrection, the risen Lord appeared to Peter (Luke 24:34) and restored him (John 21:15–19).

Finally, the verse shows the contrast between Jesus and Peter on that night. Jesus faced His accusers with courage and truth; Peter denied Him out of fear. Yet Jesus' faithfulness was not only an example for Peter but the very means of his salvation. Jesus went to the cross for the one who denied Him.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to let Jesus' gaze bring them to repentance. When they fail Him, whether through denial, compromise, or sin, they can remember that He sees them with sorrowful love. His look is not one of rejection but of invitation to return.

It also encourages believers to allow godly sorrow to lead them back to Christ. Tears over sin are not the end; they are the beginning of restoration when they lead to repentance and trust in Jesus' forgiveness.

Finally, this verse reminds believers to be humble about their own strength. Peter was sure he would never deny Jesus. Believers can learn from his story to depend on Christ's prayers and grace rather than on their own confidence, and to pray, as Jesus urged, that they will not enter into temptation.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 22 shows the night before Jesus' death, and it reveals both His faithfulness and human weakness. Judas, influenced by Satan and his own greed, betrayed Jesus. The disciples argued about greatness at the Last Supper. Peter denied Him three times. Yet through it all, Jesus remained faithful, loving, and in control.

The Last Supper calls believers to remember Jesus' sacrifice regularly. His body was given and His blood poured out "for you." Participating in the Lord's Supper with reverence and gratitude keeps the cross at the center of faith and unites believers as one body.

Jesus' response to the disciples' argument calls believers to servant leadership. In God's kingdom, greatness is measured by service, and Jesus Himself is among His people as one who serves. Believers can look for ways to serve others humbly and to use any authority they have for others' good.

Jesus' words to Peter remind believers that spiritual testing is real, but Jesus intercedes for His people. Failure need not be final. Those who fall can turn back to Him and then strengthen others. His prayer in the garden shows the importance of watchful prayer in times of temptation and distress.

Finally, Jesus' healing of the servant's ear and His look at Peter reveal His mercy even toward those who opposed or failed Him. Believers can receive His mercy when they fail and extend it to others, including their enemies.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for eagerly desiring to share the Passover with Your disciples, and for giving Your body and pouring out Your blood for me. Help me to remember Your sacrifice with gratitude every time I share the bread and cup. Forgive me for seeking greatness and recognition, and teach me to serve as You served.

Thank You for praying for Peter, and for praying for me, that my faith may not fail. When I am tested, keep me watchful and prayerful. When I fail, as Peter did, let Your look of sorrowful love bring me to repentance and back to You, and then use my story to strengthen others. Thank You for healing even those who came to arrest You. Make me merciful, as You are merciful. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
