# Matthew 1
theme: The Promised King Enters a Real Family
main: Matthew opens his Gospel by tracing Jesus' family line through David and Abraham, showing that God's long history of promises with Israel is now reaching its goal. He then tells how Joseph, a righteous man faced with a painful dilemma, obeys God's word and receives Mary's child as his own. The child is named Jesus because He will save His people from their sins, and He is Immanuel, "God with us."

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew was written for readers who knew the Scriptures of Israel well, and its opening chapter is built to speak directly to them. For centuries Israel had been waiting. God had promised Abraham that through his family all the nations of the earth would be blessed (Genesis 12:3). He had promised David that a son of his would reign on a throne that would last forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16). Yet by the time of Jesus, no descendant of David had ruled for nearly six hundred years. The nation had been conquered by Babylon, had returned from exile in small numbers, and now lived under Roman power and the rule of Herod, a king appointed by Rome who was not from David's line. Many Jews longed for the Messiah, the anointed king God had promised. Matthew's first sentence answers that longing.

### The Genealogy of Jesus the Messiah (1:1–17)

Matthew begins, [[v1|The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham]]. "Christ" is not a surname; it is the Greek form of the Hebrew title "Messiah," meaning "anointed one." Calling Jesus "the son of David" identifies Him as the rightful heir to David's throne. Calling Him "the son of Abraham" ties Him to the very first promise God made to Israel's ancestor. The phrase "the book of the genealogy" also echoes the way Genesis introduces family histories (Genesis 2:4; 5:1), hinting that something as significant as a new beginning is taking place.

Modern readers often skip genealogies, but in the ancient world a family record established identity, inheritance, and legitimacy. Matthew's list moves from Abraham to David, from David to the exile in Babylon, and from the exile to Jesus. He arranges it into three groups of fourteen generations (v. 17). He has selected and arranged the names deliberately, which was a common and accepted practice; his purpose is not to list every ancestor but to show the shape of Israel's history. It rose to its height with King David, fell to its lowest point in the exile, and now arrives at the Messiah. Some scholars also note that the numerical value of David's name in Hebrew letters is fourteen, which would make the whole list a subtle emphasis on David's royal line.

The genealogy contains some surprises. Ancient genealogies usually listed only fathers, but Matthew names four women before Mary: Tamar (v. 3), Rahab (v. 5), Ruth (v. 5), and [[v6|her who had been Uriah’s wife]], that is, Bathsheba. Each one has an unusual story. Tamar was involved in a scandalous episode with her father-in-law Judah (Genesis 38). Rahab was a Canaanite woman of Jericho who sheltered Israel's spies (Joshua 2). Ruth was a Moabite, from a people often hostile to Israel. Bathsheba is not even named; she is called the wife of Uriah, a Hittite, which reminds readers of David's adultery and his murder of her husband (2 Samuel 11). By including these women, Matthew shows that God's saving purpose has always worked through outsiders, through broken family stories, and even through serious sin. The Messiah's family tree is not polished, and that is part of the good news.

The list also includes faithful kings like Hezekiah and Josiah alongside wicked ones like Manasseh and Ahaz. It passes through the shame of the exile (vv. 11–12). Matthew is telling Israel's whole story, failures included, and saying that God has been faithful through all of it.

At the end, the pattern changes. For forty generations the wording has been "A became the father of B." But when Matthew reaches Joseph, he writes that Joseph was [[v16|the husband of Mary, from whom was born Jesus, who is called Christ]]. In the Greek, "from whom" is feminine, referring to Mary. Matthew is carefully preparing for what he explains next: Joseph is Jesus' legal father, giving Him the royal line of David, but Joseph is not His biological father.

### Joseph's Dilemma and the Angel's Message (1:18–25)

Matthew now tells how the birth came about. Mary was [[v18|engaged to Joseph]]. Jewish betrothal was much more binding than a modern engagement. It was a legal commitment, usually arranged between families, that could only be ended by a formal divorce, and the couple could be called husband and wife even though they did not yet live together or have sexual relations. The wedding, when the bride moved into the husband's home, typically came about a year later. During this period Mary was found to be pregnant. Matthew immediately tells the reader that the child was [[v18|by the Holy Spirit]], but Joseph did not yet know this.

From Joseph's point of view, the only explanation was that Mary had been unfaithful. The law of Moses treated this very seriously. Joseph had options: he could expose her publicly, bringing great shame on her and possibly a legal trial, or he could divorce her quietly before a couple of witnesses. Matthew describes Joseph as a [[v19|righteous man]] who was [[v19|not willing to make her a public example]], so he decided to end the betrothal privately. His righteousness included both respect for God's law and mercy toward Mary.

While he was considering this, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream. The angel addressed him as [[v20|Joseph, son of David]], reminding him of his royal ancestry and the role he was about to play. He told him not to be afraid to take Mary as his wife, because the child had been conceived by the Holy Spirit. He told Joseph that Mary would give birth to a son and that Joseph was to name Him Jesus. In that culture, naming a child was the father's right and an act of legally acknowledging the child as his own. By naming Jesus, Joseph would adopt Him into the line of David.

Matthew then explains that this fulfilled the words of the prophet Isaiah: the virgin would conceive and bear a son, called Immanuel, which means [[v23|God with us]]. This is the first of many times Matthew says that something happened "that it might be fulfilled," showing how Jesus completes the story and hopes of the Old Testament.

Joseph's response is immediate and complete. He [[v24|did as the angel of the Lord commanded him]]. He took Mary home as his wife, accepting whatever gossip might follow, and he did not have sexual relations with her until after Jesus was born, which confirms that Jesus' conception was entirely the work of God. Then [[v25|He named him Jesus]]. Joseph never speaks a word in Matthew's Gospel, but his obedience speaks loudly. Without fanfare, he protected Mary, received her child, and gave Jesus a home and a family name.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 1:19 — Joseph's Righteousness and Mercy

verse: Matthew 1:19

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes Joseph's response when he learned that Mary, his betrothed wife, was pregnant. Since he knew he was not the father, he would naturally have concluded that she had been unfaithful. Matthew has already told the reader that the child was from the Holy Spirit (v. 18), but Joseph had not yet been told. This verse captures him at the moment of greatest heartbreak and confusion, before the angel appears.

Matthew calls Joseph "her husband" even though they were not yet married in the full sense, because Jewish betrothal was a legally binding union. Ending it required a formal divorce. That is why Matthew says Joseph "intended to put her away," which means to divorce her.

Two qualities are mentioned. First, Joseph was "a righteous man." In Jewish thought, a righteous person was someone who lived faithfully according to God's law. Joseph took the commandments seriously, and he believed he could not simply go ahead with the marriage as if nothing had happened. Second, he was [[v19|not willing to make her a public example]]. He could have brought the matter before the community and shamed Mary publicly. Instead he chose to act quietly, with only the minimum witnesses required, to protect her as much as possible.

This tells us that Joseph's righteousness was not harsh. He did not use the law as a weapon to punish someone who, as far as he knew, had wronged him deeply. He combined faithfulness to God with compassion toward Mary. This prepares the way for the angel's message, which will ask him to go further still: to trust God's explanation and take Mary as his wife.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Joseph's story shows that true righteousness and mercy belong together. Later in Matthew, Jesus will criticize religious leaders who were careful about small rules but neglected [[Matthew 23:23|justice, mercy, and faith]], and He will quote God's words through Hosea, [[Matthew 9:13|I desire mercy, and not sacrifice]]. Joseph, the man who raised Jesus, already embodied that kind of righteousness. He cared about God's law, but he did not separate obedience from kindness.

The verse also shows a man making a hard decision with incomplete information. Joseph acted as wisely and as kindly as he could with what he knew, and then God gave him more light. Faithfulness often works this way. God does not always explain everything at once, but He guides those who are seeking to do what is right. When the angel spoke, Joseph was ready to change his plan, which shows that his commitment was to God's will rather than to his own sense of what was respectable.

It is worth noticing how much Joseph was asked to give up. Taking Mary as his wife meant sharing in whatever suspicion and gossip surrounded her pregnancy. He laid aside his reputation in order to obey God and protect a vulnerable woman and her child. In this he foreshadows the self-giving love that Jesus Himself would show.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Joseph's example teaches believers to hold firm convictions with a merciful heart. When someone has wronged us, or appears to have done so, we often feel entitled to expose them or make sure everyone knows. Joseph shows another way. It is possible to take right and wrong seriously while still refusing to humiliate another person. This applies to conflicts in families, workplaces, and churches: deal with problems honestly, but do not use them to shame others or to make ourselves look better.

His example also encourages patience before reaching final conclusions. Joseph's first judgment, while understandable, turned out to be mistaken. Many painful situations have explanations we do not yet know. Taking time, praying, and remaining open to God's direction can keep us from decisions we would later regret.

Finally, Joseph shows the kind of obedience that is willing to bear misunderstanding. Doing what God asks may sometimes cost us comfort or reputation. Believers can trust that quiet, faithful obedience matters to God even when no one else notices it.

### Matthew 1:21 — The Name That Means Salvation

verse: Matthew 1:21

#### Simple Explanation

These words are part of the angel's message to Joseph in his dream. Joseph has been told not to be afraid to take Mary as his wife because her child has been conceived by the Holy Spirit. Now he is told two things about the child: Mary will give birth to a son, and Joseph is to give Him the name Jesus.

The instruction "You shall name him" is significant. In Jewish custom, the father named his son, and by doing so he publicly claimed the child as his own. By naming Jesus, Joseph would make Him his legal son and heir, placing Him in the royal line of David that Matthew traced in verses 1–16.

The name itself carries the meaning of the verse. "Jesus" is the Greek form of the Hebrew name Yeshua (Joshua), which means "the LORD saves" or "the LORD is salvation." It was a common name at the time, but the angel explains why it fits this child in a unique way: [[v21|for it is he who shall save his people from their sins]].

Many Jews of that time hoped the Messiah would save Israel from its political enemies, especially Rome. The angel defines salvation more deeply. Israel's greatest problem was not foreign rule but sin, the rebellion against God that had led to the exile in the first place and that separates every human being from God. Jesus will deal with that root problem. "His people" refers first to Israel, but as Matthew's Gospel unfolds, Jesus' people will include all who follow Him from every nation (Matthew 28:19).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse summarizes the purpose of Jesus' life before He is even born. Matthew's Gospel will show Jesus teaching, healing, and confronting evil, but all of it moves toward the cross, where He gives His life [[Matthew 20:28|as a ransom for many]] and where His blood is poured out [[Matthew 26:28|for the remission of sins]]. The name Jesus already points to that goal.

The verse also says something important about who Jesus is. In the Old Testament, it is the LORD Himself who promises to redeem Israel from all their iniquities (Psalm 130:8). Here the angel says that Jesus Himself will save His people from their sins. Matthew is quietly preparing readers for the truth announced two verses later: in Jesus, God has come to be with His people.

Salvation from sin includes forgiveness, but it is broader than that. It means being rescued from sin's guilt and also from its power, being restored to God and gradually transformed to live as God intends. The New Testament presents this salvation as God's gift, received through faith in Jesus, not as a reward earned by good behavior.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites readers to see their deepest need clearly. Many people want help with circumstances, relationships, health, or finances, and God cares about all of these. But the angel names sin as the problem Jesus came to address at the root. Being honest about our own wrongdoing, rather than excusing it or blaming others, is the first step to receiving what Jesus offers.

It also calls believers to trust Jesus as Savior rather than trying to save themselves. When guilt weighs heavily, the answer is not to try harder to make up for it but to come to the One whose very name means "the LORD saves." Confessing sin to Him and receiving His forgiveness brings real freedom.

Finally, because Jesus saves people from sin and not only its penalty, His followers can expect and seek real change. Habits, attitudes, and patterns that once seemed fixed can be confronted with His help. Growth may be gradual, but the One who saves is also at work to make His people new.

### Matthew 1:23 — Immanuel, God With Us

verse: Matthew 1:23

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is Matthew's comment on the events he has just described. After telling how the angel instructed Joseph, Matthew explains that all of this happened to fulfill what the Lord had spoken through the prophet. The words come from Isaiah 7:14, and Matthew quotes them in the form found in the Greek translation of the Old Testament, which used the word "virgin."

In Isaiah's own day, the prophecy was given to King Ahaz of Judah around 735 B.C., when Judah was threatened by an alliance between Syria and the northern kingdom of Israel. Ahaz was afraid and refused to trust God. Isaiah told him that God would give a sign: a young woman would conceive and bear a son named Immanuel, and before the child grew old enough to know right from wrong, the threatening kings would be gone. Many interpreters believe this sign had a first fulfillment in Isaiah's time. But Isaiah's prophecy continues in chapters 8–11 to speak of a child who would be called [[Isaiah 9:6|Mighty God]] and who would sit on David's throne forever. Matthew sees the fullest meaning of Isaiah's words in Jesus, whose birth from a virgin is far more than a sign about a temporary political crisis.

The name "Immanuel" comes from two Hebrew words: "immanu" (with us) and "El" (God). Matthew translates it for his readers: "God with us." Jesus was not literally called Immanuel as His everyday name; His name was Jesus. "Immanuel" describes who He is. In Him, God is present with His people.

Matthew emphasizes that Jesus' conception happened without a human father. Mary was a virgin, and the child was conceived by the Holy Spirit (vv. 18, 20). This is not a small detail. It shows that Jesus' coming was a unique act of God, not simply the birth of a gifted human being.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

"God with us" is one of the central themes of Matthew's Gospel. It appears at the beginning of the book in this verse, in the middle when Jesus promises to be present wherever two or three gather in His name (Matthew 18:20), and at the very end when the risen Jesus tells His disciples, [[Matthew 28:20|I am with you always, even to the end of the age]]. Matthew frames his entire Gospel with the truth that, in Jesus, God has come near and remains near.

Throughout the Old Testament, God's presence among His people was precious and holy. He dwelt in the tabernacle and later in the temple, and the prophets promised a day when God would dwell among His people in a new way. Christians confess that in Jesus this promise was fulfilled in the most personal way possible: God took on human nature. The doctrine called the incarnation teaches that Jesus is fully God and fully human, one person who can truly represent humanity before God and truly reveal God to humanity.

Christians have long understood the virgin birth as a sign of this mystery. Jesus did not begin as an ordinary man who later became divine. From His conception, He was the Son of God entering human life. This is why His presence, His teaching, and His saving work carry God's own authority.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

"God with us" means believers are never truly alone. Jesus did not save from a distance; He entered human life, with its weakness, sorrow, and pain. When believers face loneliness, fear, or suffering, they can bring it to a God who understands from the inside and who has promised His continuing presence through His Spirit.

The truth of Immanuel also shapes how believers live. If God is with His people, then every part of life, including work, family, rest, and relationships, is lived in His presence. That encourages integrity in private as well as in public, and gratitude in ordinary moments as well as special ones.

Finally, Isaiah's original prophecy was given to a frightened king who refused to trust God. The sign of Immanuel was God's answer to fear. Believers facing threatening situations today can remember that God's presence is the deepest answer to fear, and they can choose to trust Him rather than rely only on their own plans and alliances, as Ahaz did.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 1 reminds readers that God keeps His promises across long stretches of time. Generations lived and died between Abraham and Jesus, and between David and Jesus. Many of them saw little evidence that God's promises were moving forward. Yet God was faithful through every generation, including the dark years of exile. Believers who are waiting on God, whether for answered prayer, for change in a difficult situation, or for the fulfillment of His purposes, can take courage from this. God's timing is often slower than ours, but His faithfulness does not fail.

The genealogy also speaks to anyone who feels their family history or personal past disqualifies them from God's purposes. The Messiah's family line included foreigners, people with scandalous stories, and kings who did great evil. God did not hide these names. He worked through and beyond them. No one's background places them beyond the reach of God's grace. This does not excuse sin, but it means that the past does not have to have the last word.

Joseph's example gives a picture of quiet, practical faithfulness. He was asked to do something that would cost him reputation and comfort, and he obeyed without delay. His righteousness included mercy, and his obedience was expressed in protecting and caring for others. Many of the most important acts of faith are like this: unseen, costly, and steady. Believers can ask God for the same kind of integrity in their families and responsibilities, choosing compassion over humiliation and obedience over self-protection.

Above all, the chapter presents Jesus as the Savior from sin and as God with us. These two truths belong together. Because Jesus is God with us, He is able to save; and because He saves His people from their sins, His presence brings forgiveness rather than fear. The right response is to trust Him, to bring honest confession of sin to Him, and to live each day aware that He is present and at work.

## Chapter Prayer

Faithful God, thank You for keeping Your promises through all the generations, through times of strength and times of failure, until You sent Jesus, the Son of David and the Son of Abraham. Thank You that His family line includes people with broken and surprising stories, and that Your grace reaches people no one else would have chosen.

Give me a heart like Joseph's, that holds firmly to what is right and still refuses to shame others, and that obeys You promptly even when it costs me something. Thank You that Jesus came to save His people from their sins. Help me to be honest about my sin and to trust Him rather than my own efforts. Thank You that He is Immanuel, God with us. When I feel afraid or alone, remind me that You are near, and teach me to live every part of my life in Your presence. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
