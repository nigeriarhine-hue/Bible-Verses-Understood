# Matthew 3
theme: The Call to Repent and the Beloved Son Revealed
main: John the Baptist prepares the way for the Messiah by calling Israel to repentance and warning that genuine change must show itself in a changed life. Jesus then comes to be baptized, identifying Himself with the people He came to save, and the Father publicly declares Him to be His beloved Son as the Spirit comes upon Him.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 3 jumps forward about thirty years from the end of chapter 2. Jesus has grown up in Nazareth, and now a new figure appears on the scene: John the Baptist. Luke's Gospel tells us that John was a relative of Jesus, the son of an elderly priest named Zechariah and his wife Elizabeth, born just a few months before Jesus (Luke 1). Matthew introduces him simply as the one who came [[v1|preaching in the wilderness of Judea]].

To understand why John's ministry caused such a stir, it helps to know that many Jews believed God had not sent a true prophet for centuries. The last of the Old Testament prophets, Malachi, had promised that God would send a messenger to prepare His way, and that Elijah would come before the great day of the LORD (Malachi 3:1; 4:5). The people lived with a sense that God was silent, waiting for Him to act again. Then suddenly, in the barren region near the Jordan River, a man dressed like Elijah began calling the whole nation to prepare for God's coming.

### John the Baptist's Message and Lifestyle (3:1–6)

John's message was short and urgent: [[v2|Repent, for the Kingdom of Heaven is at hand!]] Matthew usually says "Kingdom of Heaven" where Mark and Luke say "Kingdom of God." Many Jews avoided saying God's name out of reverence, sometimes replacing it with "Heaven," so the two phrases mean the same thing. The kingdom is God's rule, His reign breaking into the world to set things right, as the prophets had promised.

Matthew identifies John with the voice of Isaiah 40:3, crying out in the wilderness to prepare the way of the Lord. In Isaiah, that voice announced the end of Israel's exile and God's return to rescue His people. Ancient roads were prepared for the arrival of a king by leveling and straightening them. John's work was to prepare people's hearts in the same way, removing obstacles so they would be ready for the Lord.

John's appearance reinforced his message. He wore clothing made of camel's hair and a leather belt, which deliberately recalls Elijah, who is described in 2 Kings 1:8 as wearing a garment of hair with a leather belt. His diet of locusts (permitted as food under Leviticus 11:22) and wild honey was the simple fare of the desert. He lived outside the comforts and structures of society, and his whole life said that something was wrong and needed to change.

People came to him in great numbers from Jerusalem, Judea, and the Jordan region. They were baptized by him in the river, confessing their sins. Jewish people knew of ritual washings for purification, and Gentiles who converted to Judaism were sometimes immersed as part of their entry into Israel. John's baptism was striking because he called Jews, God's covenant people, to undergo a washing of repentance, as if they needed to begin again. The location was meaningful too. Israel had entered the promised land by crossing the Jordan; now the people were returning to the river to prepare for a new act of God.

### John's Warning to the Religious Leaders (3:7–12)

Among the crowds came many Pharisees and Sadducees. The Pharisees were a popular movement devoted to careful observance of the law of Moses, including many traditions about how to apply it. The Sadducees were a smaller, wealthier group associated with the priestly aristocracy and the temple, who rejected beliefs such as the resurrection of the dead. These groups often disagreed with each other, but both held positions of religious influence.

John greeted them with shocking words: [[v7|You offspring of vipers, who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?]] The image is of snakes fleeing a brush fire. John suspected they were coming for baptism as a kind of insurance against judgment, without any real change of heart. He told them to [[v8|produce fruit worthy of repentance]], meaning that genuine repentance would show itself in changed behavior.

He also warned them not to rely on being descendants of Abraham. Many believed that belonging to Abraham's family guaranteed a place among God's people. John said that God could raise up children for Abraham from the stones lying on the ground. (In Hebrew and Aramaic, the words for "children" and "stones" sound similar, which may have made the saying memorable.) Family heritage could not replace a living relationship with God.

John used two agricultural images of judgment. The ax was already lying at the root of the trees, ready to cut down any tree that did not bear good fruit. And the one coming after him would hold a winnowing fork. At harvest, farmers tossed threshed grain into the air with a fork; the wind blew the light chaff away while the heavier wheat fell back to the threshing floor. The wheat was gathered and stored, and the chaff was burned. John was saying that the coming one would separate the genuine from the false.

John insisted that this coming one was far greater than he was. John was not even worthy to carry His sandals, a task done by the lowest servants. John baptized with water, but the coming one would [[v11|baptize you in the Holy Spirit]] and with fire. The prophets had promised that in the last days God would pour out His Spirit on His people (Joel 2:28; Ezekiel 36:26–27). Fire could represent both purification and judgment, and in John's words both seem to be in view: the Messiah would purify those who repent and judge those who refuse.

### The Baptism of Jesus (3:13–17)

Then Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan to be baptized by John. John tried to stop Him, saying, [[v14|I need to be baptized by you, and you come to me?]] John recognized that Jesus was greater and had no sins to confess. Why should the one who would baptize with the Spirit be baptized by a mere prophet?

Jesus answered, [[v15|Allow it now, for this is the fitting way for us to fulfill all righteousness]]. These are the first words Jesus speaks in Matthew's Gospel. He did not need to repent, but He chose to stand with sinful people in their act of repentance, identifying fully with the people He came to save. By doing what God was calling Israel to do through John, He was carrying out God's righteous plan in every respect. His baptism also anticipates the cross, where the sinless one would take the place of sinners. Some interpreters also see in it His public dedication to His mission, much as priests and kings in Israel were consecrated before beginning their service.

As Jesus came up out of the water, the heavens were opened, and He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and resting on Him. Then a voice from heaven declared, [[v17|This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased]]. In this scene the Father speaks, the Son is baptized, and the Spirit descends. Christians have long seen here a revelation of the one God as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, a truth that Matthew will make explicit at the end of his Gospel in the command to baptize [[Matthew 28:19|in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit]].

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 3:2 — Repent, for the Kingdom Is Near

verse: Matthew 3:2

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is John the Baptist's summary message. He preached it in the wilderness of Judea, a dry and rugged region between Jerusalem and the Dead Sea, to the crowds who came out to hear him. Matthew will later show Jesus beginning His own ministry with exactly the same words (Matthew 4:17), which tells us how central this call is.

"Repent" translates a Greek word meaning to change one's mind, but in the Bible it carries the fuller meaning of the Hebrew idea of turning around. To repent is to turn away from sin and turn back to God. It involves a change of thinking, attitude, and direction. It is not simply feeling sorry; it is a reorientation of one's life. That is why John later insists on fruit that shows repentance is real.

The reason for repentance is that [[v2|the Kingdom of Heaven is at hand]]. "At hand" means it has come near; it is about to arrive or is already arriving. The Jews hoped for the day when God would act decisively to establish His rule, defeat evil, and restore His people. John announced that this day was upon them. But he gave the announcement a surprising twist: the coming of God's kingdom is not automatically good news for everyone. It means God will set things right, which includes judging sin. To be ready, people needed to turn from their sins.

The crowds who heard John understood his message as urgent. Many responded by confessing their sins and being baptized. The call was addressed not only to obvious sinners but to the whole nation, including the religious and respectable.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The kingdom of heaven is the central theme of Jesus' teaching in Matthew. It refers to God's reign: His saving and ruling power coming into the world through Jesus. The kingdom has a present dimension, seen when Jesus forgives sins, heals the sick, and drives out demons, and a future dimension, which will be complete when Christ returns. John stood at the threshold of this new era, announcing that it was about to begin.

Repentance is the doorway into the kingdom. Throughout Scripture, God calls His people to return to Him (for example, Isaiah 55:7 and Joel 2:12–13), always with the promise that He is gracious and ready to forgive. Repentance is not a way of earning God's favor; it is the response of someone who realizes they have been going the wrong way and turns back to the God who welcomes them. In the New Testament, repentance and faith go together, as two sides of turning from sin to God.

It is important that John's message was given to religious people. Repentance is not only for those whose sins are obvious. Pride, self-righteousness, and religious complacency also need to be repented of. Everyone needs to turn to God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Repentance is not a one-time event but a lasting posture for believers. Martin Luther began his famous Ninety-Five Theses with the observation that when Jesus called people to repent, He meant that the entire life of believers should be one of repentance. Followers of Christ continually recognize areas where they have turned from God and choose to turn back to Him.

Practically, repentance means naming sin honestly rather than minimizing it, confessing it to God, and making specific changes. If we have wronged someone, repentance may include apologizing and making things right. If a habit is pulling us away from God, it means taking concrete steps away from it. Feeling regret without changing direction is not yet repentance.

The nearness of God's kingdom also gives repentance hope. We do not turn from sin into emptiness; we turn toward the God whose rule brings forgiveness, healing, and new life. Repentance is the doorway to joy, not just to sorrow.

### Matthew 3:8 — Fruit That Shows Repentance Is Real

verse: Matthew 3:8

#### Simple Explanation

John speaks these words directly to the Pharisees and Sadducees who came to the place where he was baptizing. He had just called them [[v7|offspring of vipers]] and asked who had warned them to flee from the coming judgment. John perceived that many of them were not coming in genuine repentance. They may have wanted to appear righteous before the crowds, or to protect themselves from judgment, without truly changing.

"Fruit" is a common biblical image for the visible results of what is inside a person. Just as a tree is known by the fruit it produces, a person's repentance is shown by the way they live. John was saying that baptism, as an outward act, was not enough. If their repentance was real, it would produce evidence: changed behavior, humility, justice, and mercy.

Luke's Gospel records examples of the kind of fruit John meant. He told those with two coats to share with someone who had none; he told tax collectors to collect no more than they were authorized; and he told soldiers not to extort money or accuse people falsely (Luke 3:10–14). Repentance touched practical matters like money, honesty, and the treatment of others.

John then warned them not to rely on their status as descendants of Abraham. Being part of God's chosen people did not exempt them from the need for genuine repentance. He followed this with the image of the ax at the root of the trees, ready to cut down every tree that did not produce good fruit.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that true repentance cannot be separated from transformed living. Scripture consistently connects inner faith and outward obedience. Jesus will later say, [[Matthew 7:20|by their fruits you will know them]], and James will insist that faith without works is dead (James 2:17). Christians believe that people are saved by God's grace, not by their good works, but they also believe that genuine faith and repentance always produce a changed life.

The warning about relying on Abraham as father addresses a danger that every religious community faces: assuming that belonging to the right group, family, or church guarantees a right standing with God. Heritage and community are gifts, but they are not a substitute for personal turning to God. Paul later explains that Abraham's true children are those who share his faith (Galatians 3:7).

At the same time, the image of fruit is hopeful. Fruit grows gradually. God does not expect immediate perfection from those who repent, but He does expect real growth. Where there is genuine life, fruit will appear.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to examine whether their faith is making a difference in how they live. It is possible to attend church, use the right words, and participate in religious practices without real change. Genuine repentance will eventually show itself in how we spend money, how we speak, how we treat those with less power, and whether we keep our commitments.

It also warns against relying on spiritual background. Growing up in a Christian family, attending a particular church, or having been baptized years ago are not substitutes for a present, living faith. Each person must respond to God personally.

Finally, the call to bear fruit encourages specific, practical steps. If there is someone we have wronged, fruit might mean seeking reconciliation. If we have been dishonest, it might mean making restitution. If we have neglected those in need, it might mean beginning to give generously. Small, concrete changes are the evidence of a heart that has truly turned to God.

### Matthew 3:17 — "This Is My Beloved Son"

verse: Matthew 3:17

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records the voice of God the Father speaking from heaven immediately after Jesus' baptism. Jesus had come to John to be baptized, and when John hesitated, Jesus insisted that it was right [[v15|to fulfill all righteousness]]. As Jesus came up out of the water, the heavens opened, and He saw the Spirit of God descending on Him like a dove. Then the Father spoke.

The Father's words echo two important Old Testament passages. The first is Psalm 2:7, where God says to His anointed king, [[Psalm 2:7|You are my son]]. This psalm was understood as describing the Messiah, the king of David's line. The second is Isaiah 42:1, where God introduces His Servant: [[Isaiah 42:1|my chosen, in whom my soul delights]]. In Isaiah, the Servant is the one who will bring justice to the nations and, later in the book, suffer for the sins of others (Isaiah 53). The Father's words combine these two figures: Jesus is the royal Son and the suffering Servant.

The word "beloved" emphasizes the deep love between the Father and the Son. Some also hear an echo of Genesis 22:2, where God speaks of Isaac as Abraham's son, [[Genesis 22:2|your only son, Isaac, whom you love]], the son Abraham was asked to offer. The phrase [[v17|with whom I am well pleased]] expresses the Father's approval of Jesus. At this point Jesus has not yet preached a sermon or performed a miracle. The Father's pleasure rests on who Jesus is and on His willingness to take up His mission.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is one of the clearest revelations in Scripture of Jesus' identity. He is not merely a prophet like John or a good teacher. He is the beloved Son of God. Matthew's Gospel will continue to build on this truth, and the same words will be spoken again at the transfiguration, with the added command, [[Matthew 17:5|Listen to him]].

The scene also reveals the relationship within the one God. The Father speaks, the Son is baptized, and the Spirit descends. This does not explain the mystery of the Trinity, but it shows that the Father, Son, and Spirit are distinct and act together in the work of salvation.

The timing of these words is significant. They come right after Jesus identifies with sinners in baptism and right before He goes into the wilderness to be tempted. The Father's affirmation strengthens Jesus for the trial ahead. In chapter 4 the devil will twice say, "If you are the Son of God," trying to make Jesus prove His identity on the devil's terms. Jesus does not need to prove it. He has already heard His Father's voice.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls readers first to recognize who Jesus is. The Father Himself has declared Jesus to be His beloved Son. That means His words carry God's authority, His life reveals God's character, and His mission accomplishes God's purposes. The appropriate response is to listen to Him, trust Him, and follow Him.

It also offers a picture of the identity God gives to those who belong to Christ. The New Testament teaches that believers are adopted as God's children through Jesus (Galatians 4:4–7). Jesus' sonship is unique, but believers share in the Father's love through Him. This means believers can draw their sense of worth from God's love rather than from achievement, approval, or performance.

Finally, the order of events is a lesson. The Father's love and approval came before Jesus' public ministry and before His time of testing. In the same way, believers do not serve God in order to earn His love; they serve from the security of being loved. When temptation or hardship comes, remembering that we belong to God can steady us, just as Jesus faced the devil secure in His Father's words.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 3 brings together two great themes: the call to repentance and the revelation of Jesus as God's beloved Son. They belong together. John prepared people for Jesus by calling them to turn from sin, and Jesus Himself stepped into the water alongside repentant sinners. The chapter invites readers to come to God honestly, admitting their need, and to discover that the one who is coming in power to judge is also the one who stands with sinners to save them.

The chapter warns against empty religion. John was severe with religious leaders who came for baptism without real repentance and who trusted in their heritage. This warning still applies to anyone who relies on church attendance, religious upbringing, or outward practices instead of a living, obedient relationship with God. The remedy is not to abandon spiritual practices but to let them be expressions of a heart truly turned to God, producing fruit in everyday life.

John's humility offers another lesson. He had large crowds, great influence, and a powerful message, but he insisted that he was not worthy even to carry Jesus' sandals. He understood that his role was to point to someone greater. Believers who serve in any capacity, whether in families, churches, or communities, can learn from John to keep attention on Christ rather than on themselves.

Finally, Jesus' willingness to be baptized shows the humility of God's Son. He did not hold Himself apart from sinful people; He joined them. His followers are called to the same kind of humble solidarity with others, refusing to look down on those who are struggling, and remembering that every believer stands before God only because Jesus chose to stand with us. Living from the Father's love, rather than striving to earn it, allows believers to face temptation and serve others with steady confidence.

## Chapter Prayer

Heavenly Father, thank You for sending John to prepare the way for Your Son, and for the clear call to repent because Your kingdom has come near. Search my heart and show me where I have turned away from You. Give me grace to turn back fully, not only with words or outward actions but with a changed life that bears real fruit.

Keep me from trusting in my background, my reputation, or my religious habits instead of in You. Thank You that Jesus, though He had no sin, stepped into the water with sinners and identified Himself with people like me. Thank You for declaring Him Your beloved Son. Help me to listen to Him, to follow Him, and to live in the security of being loved by You through Him, so that I may face temptation and serve others with humility and confidence. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
