# Romans 1
theme: The Gospel of God's Power and Humanity's Need
main: Paul introduces himself as a servant of Christ Jesus, set apart for the gospel God promised through the prophets concerning His Son, descended from David and declared Son of God in power by the resurrection. He expresses his longing to visit the believers in Rome and states his theme: he is not ashamed of the gospel, because it is God's power for the salvation of everyone who believes, revealing a righteousness that is received by faith. Paul then begins to explain why everyone needs this gospel: God's wrath is revealed against human ungodliness, because people have known God through creation yet refused to honor Him, exchanging the Creator for created things and falling into every kind of sin.

## Chapter Background and Story

Romans 1 opens the most systematic letter Paul ever wrote. In ancient letters, the writer usually began with his name, the recipients, and a greeting. Paul follows this pattern, but he expands it dramatically, packing his greeting with a summary of the gospel. Then, after a personal section about his plans, he announces the theme of the whole letter (1:16–17) and begins his argument that all people need God's saving righteousness (1:18–3:20).

### Paul's Greeting and the Gospel of God's Son (1:1–7)

Paul describes himself in three ways. He is [[v1|a servant of Jesus Christ]]; the word means "slave" or "bondservant," someone wholly owned by and devoted to his master. He is [[v1|called to be an apostle]], appointed by Christ Himself. And he is [[v1|set apart for the Good News of God]]. As a Pharisee, which means "separated one," Paul had once been set apart for the Law; now he was set apart for the gospel.

This gospel is not new. God [[v2|promised before through his prophets in the holy Scriptures]]. Its subject is God's Son, described in two ways. According to His human nature, He [[v3|was born of the offspring of David according to the flesh]], fulfilling the promise of a Messiah from David's line. And He [[v4|was declared to be the Son of God with power according to the Spirit of holiness, by the resurrection from the dead]]. Jesus did not become God's Son at the resurrection; He was always the Son. But the resurrection publicly declared and displayed His identity in power.

Through Christ, Paul received grace and apostleship [[v5|for obedience of faith among all the nations]]. The phrase "obedience of faith" sums up the goal of Paul's mission: faith that expresses itself in obedience to Christ. Paul's greeting ends with his characteristic blessing: [[v7|Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ]]. Notice that Paul places Jesus alongside the Father as the source of grace and peace.

### Paul's Longing to Visit Rome (1:8–15)

Paul thanked God that the Roman believers' [[v8|faith is proclaimed throughout the whole world]]. Rome was the capital, and news of a church there spread widely. He prayed for them constantly and longed to visit, [[v11|that I may impart to you some spiritual gift, to the end that you may be established]]. Then he humbly added that the blessing would be mutual: [[v12|that is, that I with you may be encouraged in you, each of us by the other’s faith, both yours and mine]].

He had often planned to come but had been prevented. He felt a debt to all people: [[v14|I am debtor both to Greeks and to foreigners, both to the wise and to the foolish]]. Having received the gospel, he owed it to everyone. So [[v15|I am eager to preach the Good News to you also who are in Rome]].

### The Theme of the Letter (1:16–17)

Paul then announced the theme of the entire letter, studied below in two parts.

### God's Wrath against Ungodliness (1:18–32)

Paul now began to explain why the gospel is necessary. Before describing the good news, he describes the bad news. The first part of this explanation, about God's revelation in creation, is studied below, as is the "great exchange" of truth for a lie.

Paul then describes how God "gave them up" three times (1:24, 26, 28). This phrase describes God's judgment in the present: when people persistently reject Him, God allows them to experience the consequences of their choices. Sin becomes its own punishment.

In verses 26–27, Paul speaks of same-sex sexual relations as an example of humanity's turning away from God's created design, describing them as contrary to "nature," meaning God's design in creation. These verses are part of Paul's broader argument that all humanity has turned from the Creator. Christians in most traditions throughout history have understood these verses as teaching that sexual relations are intended for marriage between a man and a woman, while some modern interpreters have proposed other readings. Whatever one's view, Paul's argument does not single out one group as uniquely sinful. In the very next verses, he lists envy, deceit, gossip, arrogance, disobedience to parents, and unmercifulness, and in chapter 2 he turns to those who judge others, showing that all people stand in need of God's grace. These verses must never be used to justify contempt, mockery, or mistreatment of anyone. Every person is made in God's image and loved by Him.

Paul's list of sins in verses 29–31 is sobering: [[v29|being filled with all unrighteousness, sexual immorality, wickedness, covetousness, malice; full of envy, murder, strife, deceit, evil habits, secret slanderers]], [[v30|backbiters, hateful to God, insolent, arrogant, boastful, inventors of evil things, disobedient to parents]], [[v31|without understanding, covenant breakers, without natural affection, unforgiving, unmerciful]]. The list includes sins that many people consider minor, such as gossip and boasting, alongside violent ones like murder. No one can read this list without finding themselves in it.

The chapter ends with a final observation: people know that God's judgment is right, yet [[v32|not only do the same, but also approve of those who practice them]]. Approving and celebrating sin shows how deeply the human heart has been darkened.

This dark picture is not the end of the story. It prepares the way for the good news of chapter 3: [[Romans 3:23|for all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God]], [[Romans 3:24|being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus]].

## Important Verses Explained

### Romans 1:16 — Not Ashamed of the Gospel

verse: Romans 1:16

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had just told the believers in Rome how much he longed to visit them and preach the gospel there. Rome was the capital of the most powerful empire in the world, a city of wealth, culture, and military might. To many in Rome, the message of a crucified Jewish teacher who rose from the dead would seem foolish or even shameful. Crucifixion was the most humiliating form of execution, reserved for slaves and rebels.

Yet Paul declared, [[v16|For I am not ashamed of the Good News of Christ]]. He had been mocked in Athens, beaten in Philippi, and stoned in Lystra for this message. He was not embarrassed by it. He was proud to proclaim it.

Why? [[v16|because it is the power of God for salvation]]. The gospel is not merely good advice or interesting information. It is "the power of God." The Greek word for power (dynamis) is related to the English word "dynamite." Through the gospel, God acts with power to rescue people from sin and death.

[[v16|for everyone who believes]]. The gospel's power is available to everyone, without exception. It is received by believing, by trusting in Jesus. No one is excluded because of nationality, background, or past.

[[v16|for the Jew first, and also for the Greek]]. The gospel came first to the Jewish people, because God had made His promises to them and the Messiah came from them. Then it went to the Gentiles ("Greeks" here meaning all non-Jews). Paul's own ministry followed this pattern: he usually preached first in the synagogue, then to Gentiles. Both Jews and Gentiles are saved in the same way, by faith.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that the gospel is God's power, not human wisdom. Paul wrote to the Corinthians that the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, [[1 Corinthians 1:18|but to us who are being saved it is the power of God]]. Lives are transformed not by clever arguments or programs but by the message of Christ crucified and risen.

It also shows the universal scope of salvation. "Everyone who believes" includes every nation, culture, and class. The gospel breaks down barriers between Jew and Gentile, rich and poor, educated and uneducated.

Finally, the verse shows that there is a temptation to be ashamed of the gospel. Jesus warned, [[Mark 8:38|For whoever will be ashamed of me and of my words in this adulterous and sinful generation, the Son of Man also will be ashamed of him]]. Paul's declaration is a model of courageous faith.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse challenges believers to examine whether they are ashamed of the gospel. Many believers feel embarrassed to speak about Jesus at work, at school, or among friends, fearing ridicule or rejection. Paul's example encourages them to speak with confidence, because the gospel is God's power.

It also encourages believers to trust the gospel's power. Believers do not need to make the message more impressive or acceptable. The simple message of Christ's death and resurrection has power to save. Believers can share it with confidence that God will work through it.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that the gospel is for everyone. No one is too far gone, too different, or too sinful to be saved. Believers can share the gospel with all people, trusting that it is for "everyone who believes."

### Romans 1:17 — The Righteous Shall Live by Faith

verse: Romans 1:17

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had just declared that he was not ashamed of the gospel, because it is the power of God for salvation for everyone who believes. Now he explained why the gospel has this power.

[[v17|For in it is revealed God’s righteousness]]. The gospel reveals "God's righteousness." This phrase has a rich meaning. It refers to God's own righteousness, His faithfulness, justice, and goodness. It also refers to the righteousness God gives to sinners, the right standing before Him that He grants as a gift. In the gospel, God shows that He is righteous and makes sinners righteous through faith in Christ. Paul will explain this more fully in Romans 3:21–26.

[[v17|from faith to faith]]. This phrase has been understood in several ways. It may mean "by faith from first to last," that is, entirely by faith. It may mean from God's faithfulness to human faith. Or it may mean from the faith of one person to the faith of another, as the gospel spreads. The main point is clear: this righteousness is received by faith, not by works.

[[v17|As it is written, “But the righteous shall live by faith.”]] Paul quoted Habakkuk 2:4. In its original setting, Habakkuk was struggling to understand why God would allow the cruel Babylonians to conquer Judah. God answered that the righteous person would live by trusting Him, remaining faithful even when circumstances were confusing. Paul applies this verse to the gospel: those who are made righteous by faith will live, receiving eternal life, through faith.

This verse changed the course of history. Martin Luther, a sixteenth-century monk who was tormented by the fear that he could never be righteous enough for God, studied this verse and came to understand that God's righteousness is a gift received by faith. He later wrote that he felt as if he had been born again and had entered paradise itself through open gates.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse introduces the central theme of Romans: justification by faith. People cannot make themselves righteous before God by their own efforts. But God gives righteousness as a gift to all who trust in Christ. Paul writes later, [[Romans 3:22|even the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ to all and on all those who believe]].

It also shows that faith is not only the entrance to the Christian life but its whole character. "The righteous shall live by faith." Believers begin by faith, continue by faith, and will finish by faith. Faith means trusting God and His promises, even when circumstances are difficult, as Habakkuk learned.

Finally, the verse shows the continuity between the Old and New Testaments. Paul grounds his message in Habakkuk. Justification by faith is not a new idea; it was always God's way. Abraham himself was declared righteous by faith (Genesis 15:6; Romans 4:3).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse brings freedom to those who feel they can never be good enough for God. Righteousness is not earned; it is received as a gift through faith in Christ. Believers can rest in this gift rather than striving anxiously to earn God's approval.

It also calls believers to live by faith every day. Faith is not only for the moment of conversion. Believers are called to trust God in every circumstance, especially in times of confusion or suffering, as Habakkuk did.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to share this good news. Many people around them are burdened by guilt or by the fear that they are not good enough. The gospel reveals that God gives righteousness freely to all who believe.

### Romans 1:20–21 — Without Excuse

verse: Romans 1:20-21

#### Simple Explanation

After announcing the good news of the gospel, Paul began to explain why everyone needs it. He started with people who do not have the written Scriptures, the Gentile world. Did they know anything about God?

[[v20|For the invisible things of him since the creation of the world are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made]]. God is invisible; no one can see Him directly. But His creation reveals Him. When people look at the vastness of the sky, the beauty of the earth, the complexity of living things, and the order of the universe, they can perceive something of the God who made them.

[[v20|even his everlasting power and divinity]]. Creation reveals two things about God in particular: His "everlasting power," the power needed to create and sustain the universe, and His "divinity," that He is God, a being above and beyond creation.

[[v20|that they may be without excuse]]. Because God has revealed Himself in creation, no one can say they had no knowledge of Him at all. Everyone has received some light.

Then Paul described humanity's response: [[v21|Because knowing God, they didn’t glorify him as God, and didn’t give thanks]]. People knew God through creation, but they did not honor Him as God or thank Him for His gifts. Ingratitude and refusal to worship are at the root of human sin.

[[v21|but became vain in their reasoning, and their senseless heart was darkened]]. When people turn from God, their thinking becomes empty and confused, and their hearts become darkened. Rejecting the light leads to deeper darkness.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses teach what is often called "general revelation": God reveals Himself to all people through creation. Psalm 19 says, [[Psalm 19:1|The heavens declare the glory of God. The expanse shows his handiwork]]. This revelation is not enough to show people the way of salvation through Christ, but it is enough to show them that God exists and deserves their worship.

They also reveal the root of sin. The fundamental human problem is not ignorance but rebellion: refusing to honor God and give Him thanks. Sin begins in the heart's refusal to acknowledge God as God.

Finally, these verses show that rejecting God affects the mind. When people refuse the truth, their reasoning becomes futile. This helps explain why intelligent people can believe foolish things when they reject God. Paul writes that people [[Romans 1:22|Professing themselves to be wise, they became fools]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to see God's glory in creation. Nature is not merely a collection of random processes; it is the handiwork of God. Believers can grow in worship by paying attention to the beauty and wonder of the world He made.

They also call believers to gratitude. Paul identifies ingratitude as one of the first steps away from God. Believers can cultivate thankfulness, regularly recognizing and thanking God for His gifts.

Finally, these verses help believers understand those who reject God. Many people know, at some level, that God exists, but they resist honoring Him. Believers can pray for them and gently point them to the God revealed in creation and, more fully, in Jesus Christ.

### Romans 1:24–25 — Exchanging the Truth of God for a Lie

verse: Romans 1:24-25

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had explained that people knew God through creation but refused to honor Him or give Him thanks. Instead, they [[v23|traded the glory of the incorruptible God for the likeness of an image of corruptible man, and of birds, four-footed animals, and creeping things]]. They made idols and worshiped them instead of the true God.

Then Paul described God's response: [[v24|Therefore God also gave them up in the lusts of their hearts to uncleanness, that their bodies should be dishonored among themselves]]. The phrase "God gave them up" is repeated three times in this chapter (1:24, 26, 28). It describes God's judgment. When people persistently reject God, He allows them to go their own way and experience the consequences of their choices. He removes His restraining hand, and their desires lead them into deeper sin.

[[v25|who exchanged the truth of God for a lie]]. The "truth of God" is the truth about who God is and who people are in relation to Him. The "lie" is the idea that something other than God deserves worship. Every idol is a lie, promising what only God can give.

[[v25|and worshiped and served the creature rather than the Creator]]. This is the heart of idolatry: giving the worship that belongs to God to something He has made. In the ancient world, this meant statues and images. But it can include anything people put in God's place: money, pleasure, power, success, relationships, or even oneself.

[[v25|who is blessed forever. Amen.]] Even while describing humanity's rebellion, Paul cannot help but praise God. The Creator remains worthy of eternal praise, whether or not people give it to Him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that idolatry is the root of all other sins. When people stop worshiping God, they will worship something else. And what they worship shapes who they become. The psalmist writes of idols, [[Psalm 115:8|Those who make them will be like them]].

They also show that God's judgment can take the form of allowing people to have what they want. "God gave them up" means that He let them follow their own desires. This is a sobering truth: one of the most serious judgments is for God to let people go their own way. Yet even this judgment is meant to show people the emptiness of life without God and lead them back to Him.

Finally, these verses show that sin distorts human relationships and even the human body. When people reject the Creator, they misuse His good gifts. The rest of the chapter traces how turning from God leads to the breakdown of relationships and moral life.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to examine their own hearts for idols. Idols today are rarely statues, but they can be anything that takes God's place in one's affections, trust, or priorities. Believers can ask God to reveal their idols and help them turn back to Him.

They also warn against believing the lie that anything other than God can truly satisfy. Every idol eventually disappoints. Believers can find their deepest satisfaction in worshiping and serving the Creator.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to respond to a broken world with compassion. Paul's description of humanity applies to everyone, including believers before they knew Christ. Believers are not called to look down on others but to share the gospel, which has the power to rescue people from the lie and bring them back to the truth.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Romans 1 calls believers to be unashamed of the gospel. The message of Christ crucified and risen may seem foolish to the world, but it is the power of God for the salvation of everyone who believes. Believers can share it confidently, knowing that it is good news for every person, regardless of background.

The chapter also introduces the heart of Paul's message: God's righteousness is revealed in the gospel and received by faith. People cannot make themselves right with God; He gives righteousness as a gift. Believers can rest in this grace and live each day by faith, trusting God in every circumstance.

Romans 1 also shows that God reveals Himself through creation, so that all people have some knowledge of Him. Yet humanity has refused to honor Him and give Him thanks, turning instead to idols. Believers can respond by cultivating gratitude and worship, seeing God's glory in the world He made, and examining their own hearts for anything that has taken His place.

Finally, the chapter paints an honest picture of human sinfulness. Its list of sins includes things like envy, gossip, and arrogance alongside more obvious wrongs. No one escapes this description. This should lead believers not to pride or condemnation of others, but to humility and compassion, and to a deeper gratitude for the gospel, which is God's answer to the brokenness of every human heart.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord God, I praise You as the Creator, who is blessed forever. Thank You for revealing Your eternal power and divine nature in the world You made. Forgive me for the times I have failed to honor You or give You thanks, and for the idols I have allowed to take Your place in my heart. Thank You for the gospel of Your Son, born of David's line and declared to be the Son of God with power by His resurrection. Help me never to be ashamed of this good news, for it is Your power for the salvation of everyone who believes. Thank You for the righteousness You give by faith. Teach me to live by faith every day. Keep me from pride and from looking down on others, and fill me with compassion and a desire to share the gospel with all. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
