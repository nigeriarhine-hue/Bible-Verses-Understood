# Matthew 22
theme: Invited to the Feast, Tested in the Temple
main: Jesus tells a parable of a royal wedding feast to show that God's invitation, rejected by many who were first invited, goes out to everyone, while still requiring a fitting response. In the temple courts, His opponents try to trap Him with questions about taxes, the resurrection, and the greatest commandment. Jesus answers each with wisdom that exposes their errors, summarizes the law as love for God and neighbor, and finally asks a question about the Messiah that no one can answer.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 22 continues the confrontation in the temple courts during the final week before Jesus' death. In chapter 21, the chief priests and elders challenged Jesus' authority, and He responded with parables about two sons and wicked tenants that exposed their rejection of God's messengers. They wanted to arrest Him but feared the crowds. In chapter 22, Jesus tells one more parable, and then different groups of opponents come with questions designed to trap Him. Each attempt fails, and by the end of the chapter, no one dares ask Him anything more.

### The Parable of the Wedding Feast (22:1–14)

Jesus compared the kingdom of heaven to a king who prepared a wedding feast for his son. In the ancient Near East, a royal wedding feast was a grand occasion that could last for days. Invitations were typically sent twice: once well in advance, and then again when everything was ready. Refusing a king's invitation after initially accepting was a serious insult.

The king sent his servants to call the invited guests, but they would not come. He sent more servants with a warm appeal: the dinner was prepared, the cattle were slaughtered, everything was ready. But the guests [[v5|made light of it]] and went off to their farms and businesses. Others seized the servants, mistreated them, and killed them. The king was furious, sent his army, destroyed the murderers, and burned their city.

Then he told his servants to go to the main roads and invite everyone they found. They gathered [[v10|both bad and good]], and the wedding hall was filled with guests. But when the king came in, he saw a man without wedding clothes. He asked, [[v12|Friend, how did you come in here not wearing wedding clothing?]] The man was speechless. The king ordered him bound and thrown into the outer darkness. Jesus concluded, [[v14|For many are called, but few chosen]].

The parable continues the themes of chapter 21. The king is God; the son is Jesus; the wedding feast represents the joy of God's kingdom, a common image in Jewish hope (Isaiah 25:6–9). The first invited guests represent the religious leaders and those in Israel who rejected God's call through the prophets, John, and Jesus. The destruction of the city may point to the destruction of Jerusalem by the Romans in A.D. 70, which many early Christians understood as a judgment connected with the rejection of Jesus. The invitation to everyone on the roads represents the gospel going out to all people, including sinners and Gentiles.

The man without wedding clothes shows that accepting the invitation requires a fitting response. Some interpreters suggest that hosts sometimes provided garments for guests, so refusing to wear one was an insult. Whether or not that custom applied, the point is that those who come to God's feast must be transformed by His grace. Many interpreters connect the wedding garment with the righteousness that comes from God and shows itself in a changed life (compare Revelation 19:8). Being "called" means receiving the invitation; being "chosen" refers to those who truly respond and belong to God.

### Paying Taxes to Caesar (22:15–22)

The Pharisees then plotted to trap Jesus in His words. They sent their disciples together with the Herodians, supporters of Herod's family who cooperated with Rome. These two groups normally opposed each other, but they united against Jesus. They began with flattery, praising His honesty and impartiality. Then they asked, [[v17|Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?]]

This was a carefully designed trap. The tax in question was the poll tax paid directly to the Roman emperor, which had sparked a revolt led by Judas the Galilean in A.D. 6. Many Jews hated it as a sign of foreign domination. If Jesus said "pay it," He would lose the support of the crowds who resented Rome. If He said "don't pay it," the Herodians could report Him to the Romans as a rebel.

Jesus saw through their scheme and called them hypocrites. He asked to see the coin used for the tax. They brought a denarius, which showed it was in their possession. Jesus asked whose image and inscription were on it. They said, "Caesar's." The denarius of that time likely bore the image of Emperor Tiberius and an inscription calling him "son of the divine Augustus." Jesus then said, [[v21|Give therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s]]. His opponents were amazed and left. This saying is studied below.

### The Question About the Resurrection (22:23–33)

That same day, the Sadducees came. Matthew explains that they [[v23|say that there is no resurrection]]. The Sadducees accepted only the first five books of Moses as authoritative and believed that those books did not teach resurrection. They presented a scenario based on the law of levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5–6), which required a man to marry his brother's widow if the brother died childless, to continue the family line. Seven brothers married the same woman in turn, each dying without children. In the resurrection, whose wife would she be? The Sadducees intended to show that the idea of resurrection was absurd.

Jesus answered, [[v29|You are mistaken, not knowing the Scriptures, nor the power of God]]. First, they misunderstood God's power. In the resurrection, people will not marry, but will be like the angels. Life in the age to come is not simply a continuation of earthly life with the same arrangements; God will transform it. Second, they misunderstood the Scriptures. Jesus quoted from the books the Sadducees accepted, Exodus 3:6, where God says to Moses at the burning bush, "I am the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob." God spoke these words centuries after the patriarchs died, yet He said "I am," not "I was." [[v32|God is not the God of the dead, but of the living]]. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are still alive to God, and their relationship with Him guarantees their future resurrection. The crowds were astonished.

### The Greatest Commandment (22:34–40)

When the Pharisees heard that Jesus had silenced the Sadducees, they gathered together. One of them, an expert in the law, asked Him a question to test Him: [[v36|Teacher, which is the greatest commandment in the law?]] Jewish teachers counted 613 commandments in the law of Moses and often debated which were "heavy" and which were "light."

Jesus answered by quoting Deuteronomy 6:5, the heart of the Shema, the confession of faith that devout Jews recited daily: [[v37|You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind]]. This is the first and greatest commandment. Then He added a second, like it, from Leviticus 19:18: [[v39|You shall love your neighbor as yourself]]. He concluded that all the Law and the Prophets depend on these two commandments. This is studied below.

### Whose Son Is the Messiah? (22:41–46)

While the Pharisees were still gathered, Jesus asked them a question: [[v42|What do you think of the Christ? Whose son is he?]] They answered, "Of David," which was the standard expectation. The Messiah would be a descendant of King David.

Jesus then asked how David, speaking by the Spirit, could call the Messiah "Lord" in Psalm 110:1: [[v44|The Lord said to my Lord, sit on my right hand, until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet]]. In that psalm, "The LORD" (God) speaks to "my Lord" (the Messiah). In that culture, a father would not call his son "lord"; a son honored his father. If David called the Messiah "Lord," then the Messiah must be more than David's son. He must be greater than David.

The Pharisees could not answer. Jesus was pointing to His own identity: He is David's son by human descent, but He is also David's Lord, the Son of God who would sit at God's right hand. Psalm 110:1 became one of the most quoted Old Testament verses in the New Testament, used to describe Jesus' exaltation after His resurrection (Acts 2:34–36; Hebrews 1:13). After this, no one dared ask Him any more questions.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 22:21 — Caesar's and God's

verse: Matthew 22:21

#### Simple Explanation

This verse contains Jesus' answer to one of the most carefully planned traps of His ministry. The Pharisees and Herodians came together to ask whether it was lawful to pay taxes to Caesar. These groups had different views on Roman rule, but they united to test Jesus. The question seemed to have no safe answer. Saying "yes" would make Jesus appear to support foreign oppression and lose the crowd's support. Saying "no" would make Him guilty of rebellion against Rome.

Jesus asked them to show Him the coin used to pay the tax. They brought a denarius, a Roman silver coin. Jesus asked whose image and inscription were on it. They answered, "Caesar's." The coin bore the image of Emperor Tiberius and likely an inscription calling him the son of the divine Augustus. Many devout Jews were troubled by such coins because of their claims of divinity and because images could be seen as violating the second commandment. The fact that Jesus' questioners had one in their possession, even in the temple courts, was telling.

Jesus then said, [[v21|Give therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s]]. The word "give" here means "give back" or "pay what is owed." Since the coin bears Caesar's image and was produced by his government, it belongs to his sphere. Paying taxes is a legitimate obligation. But Jesus added that people must also give to God what belongs to Him.

This answer avoided both traps. Jesus affirmed that taxes should be paid, so He could not be accused of rebellion. But He also placed Caesar under God. Caesar's coin was Caesar's, but Caesar was not divine, and he had no claim on what belongs only to God. His opponents were amazed and went away.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that believers have real obligations to governing authorities, but those obligations are limited. Paul later explains that governments are established by God to maintain order and that Christians should pay taxes and give honor to those in authority (Romans 13:1–7). Peter also urges believers to honor the emperor (1 Peter 2:17). Yet both apostles also insisted on obeying God rather than human authorities when the two conflict (Acts 5:29).

The deeper point lies in the word "image." The coin bore Caesar's image, so it belonged to him. Genesis 1:27 teaches that human beings are made in the image of God. If what bears Caesar's image belongs to Caesar, then what bears God's image, human beings themselves, belongs to God. Caesar can claim taxes, but God claims the whole person: heart, soul, mind, and strength.

This saying does not create two completely separate spheres, as if God has no interest in public life. Everything ultimately belongs to God (Psalm 24:1), and governments are accountable to Him. But it does mean that believers can participate in civic life and fulfill their responsibilities while recognizing that their ultimate loyalty belongs to God alone.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to be responsible citizens. Paying taxes honestly, obeying just laws, and respecting those in authority are part of faithful living. Christian faith does not exempt believers from civic duties.

It also calls believers to keep their ultimate loyalty for God. No government, political party, nation, or leader deserves the devotion that belongs only to God. When political loyalties become all-consuming, or when governments demand what conflicts with God's commands, believers must remember that they belong to God first.

Finally, this verse invites each believer to consider what they are giving to God. If they bear God's image, then their lives belong to Him. That includes time, abilities, relationships, and resources. Jesus' words challenge believers not only to pay what they owe to the state, but to give themselves wholly to God.

### Matthew 22:32 — The God of the Living

verse: Matthew 22:32

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to the Sadducees, a group of religious leaders who denied the resurrection of the dead. They had tried to make the resurrection look ridiculous with a story about a woman who married seven brothers in turn, according to the law of levirate marriage. They asked whose wife she would be in the resurrection.

Jesus told them they were mistaken because they did not know the Scriptures or the power of God. He first explained that resurrection life is different from earthly life; people will not marry then. Then He turned to the Scriptures. The Sadducees accepted the five books of Moses as their highest authority, so Jesus quoted from Exodus 3:6, where God spoke to Moses from the burning bush: [[v32|I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob]].

By the time God spoke these words to Moses, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob had been dead for centuries. Yet God did not say "I was their God" but "I am their God." Jesus drew the conclusion: [[v32|God is not the God of the dead, but of the living]]. If God still identifies Himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, then they must still be alive in relationship with Him. And God's covenant relationship with them, which included promises for their bodies and their descendants, requires that He will raise them.

The crowds who heard this were astonished. Jesus had answered the Sadducees from the very books they claimed to honor.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that God's relationship with His people does not end at death. The covenant God makes with those who belong to Him is stronger than death. Those who die in faith are still alive to God, and they await the resurrection of the body. Luke's account adds, [[Luke 20:38|for all are alive to him]].

The verse also shows how Jesus read Scripture. He paid attention to the precise wording and to the character of God revealed in it. The God who calls Himself the God of Abraham would not abandon Abraham to permanent death. God's faithfulness guarantees resurrection.

Jesus' own resurrection, a few days after this conversation, would be the decisive proof. Paul says Christ [[1 Corinthians 15:20|became the first fruit of those who are asleep]], the beginning of the great resurrection harvest. The Christian hope is not merely that the soul survives death, but that God will raise His people bodily, transforming them to share in His eternal life.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse brings comfort to those who have lost loved ones who trusted in God. Death is real and painful, but it does not end the relationship between God and His people. Believers who have died are alive to God, and they will be raised. Grief can be held together with hope.

It also shapes how believers face their own mortality. Because God is the God of the living, death is not the final word. Believers can live with courage and purpose, knowing that their future is secure in God's faithfulness.

Finally, this verse encourages careful reading of Scripture. Jesus rebuked the Sadducees for not knowing the Scriptures or the power of God. Believers can study the Bible attentively and humbly, allowing it to correct their assumptions, and they can trust that God's power is greater than their limited understanding.

### Matthew 22:37–39 — Love God, Love Your Neighbor

verse: Matthew 22:37-39

#### Simple Explanation

These verses contain Jesus' answer to a question from an expert in the law. The Pharisees had gathered after Jesus silenced the Sadducees, and one of them asked Him which commandment in the law was the greatest. Jewish teachers often debated how to rank the many commandments of the law. The question may have been a test to see whether Jesus would downplay some part of the law.

Jesus answered by quoting Deuteronomy 6:5: [[v37|You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind]]. This verse was part of the Shema, the daily confession of faith that begins, "Hear, Israel: the LORD is our God. The LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4). Every devout Jew knew these words. "Heart, soul, and mind" together mean the whole person: emotions, will, life, and thoughts. Love for God must involve every part of a person's being. (Deuteronomy says "heart, soul, and might"; the other Gospels include both "mind" and "strength," showing that the point is total devotion.)

Jesus said this is [[v38|the first and great commandment]]. Then He added a second that is [[v39|likewise]], meaning similar in nature and importance: [[v39|You shall love your neighbor as yourself]], from Leviticus 19:18. In Leviticus, "neighbor" referred to fellow Israelites, but the same chapter also commands love for foreigners living among them (Leviticus 19:34), and Jesus expanded "neighbor" to include anyone in need, even enemies (Luke 10:25–37; Matthew 5:44).

In the next verse, Jesus says that all the Law and the Prophets depend on these two commandments, the way a door hangs on its hinges. Every other commandment in Scripture is an expression of love for God or love for neighbor.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses summarize the heart of God's will for human life. God is not primarily interested in rule-keeping for its own sake; He desires love. The Ten Commandments themselves are divided into duties toward God and duties toward others. Paul writes, [[Romans 13:10|Love therefore is the fulfillment of the law]].

The order matters. Love for God comes first. When God is loved above all, love for others flows from that relationship and is shaped by it. But the two cannot be separated. John writes that anyone who says he loves God but hates his brother is a liar (1 John 4:20). Love for God is shown in love for those made in His image.

These commands also reveal humanity's need for grace. No one has loved God with all their heart, soul, and mind, or loved their neighbor perfectly as themselves. The commandments show the standard, and they reveal how far everyone falls short. Jesus alone fulfilled them perfectly, and through Him believers receive forgiveness and the Holy Spirit, who pours God's love into their hearts (Romans 5:5) and enables them to love.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses offer a simple but demanding guide for daily life. Believers can ask, in any situation, "How can I love God here?" and "How can I love my neighbor here?" These questions apply to decisions about time, money, work, relationships, speech, and priorities.

Loving God with the whole heart, soul, and mind means worship that engages emotions, will, and intellect. It includes prayer, obedience, trusting Him in hardship, and thinking carefully about His Word. It means giving Him first place in every area of life.

Loving one's neighbor as oneself means caring for others' well-being with the same attention we naturally give our own. It includes kindness, honesty, generosity, patience, and forgiveness, and extends to people we find difficult, people different from us, and people in need. This love is not merely a feeling; it is expressed in practical action.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 22 begins with an invitation. God invites people to the joy of His kingdom, pictured as a royal wedding feast. Many reject the invitation because they are busy with other concerns, and some respond with hostility. But God extends the invitation to everyone, good and bad alike. Believers can receive this invitation with gratitude and share it widely, knowing that God welcomes people from every background. At the same time, the man without wedding clothes reminds them that accepting God's invitation means being transformed by His grace, not continuing as though nothing has changed.

The questions in the temple courts show Jesus' wisdom and challenge believers in practical ways. The question about taxes teaches that believers have real responsibilities to governing authorities, but their ultimate loyalty belongs to God, in whose image they are made. Believers can be good citizens without giving any government or political cause the devotion that belongs to God alone.

The question about the resurrection reminds believers that God is the God of the living. Death does not end the relationship between God and His people, and the hope of resurrection can sustain believers in grief and in the face of their own mortality. It also challenges believers to know the Scriptures and trust God's power.

At the center of the chapter is the greatest commandment: love God with all your heart, soul, and mind, and love your neighbor as yourself. This summary of God's will can guide every part of life. Believers can measure their priorities, decisions, and relationships by these two commandments, and when they fall short, they can turn to Jesus, who fulfilled them perfectly, and ask for His Spirit to fill them with love.

## Chapter Prayer

Gracious King, thank You for inviting me to the feast of Your kingdom. Forgive me for the times I have been too busy or distracted to respond to Your call. Clothe me with the righteousness You provide, and change my life so that it fits the grace I have received. Help me to share Your invitation with everyone, knowing that You welcome people from every road.

Teach me to fulfill my responsibilities to those in authority while giving my whole self to You, because I bear Your image. Thank You that You are the God of the living, and that those who trust in You are alive to You even in death. Strengthen my hope in the resurrection. Above all, help me to love You with all my heart, soul, and mind, and to love my neighbor as myself. Where I fall short, fill me with Your Spirit and Your love. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
