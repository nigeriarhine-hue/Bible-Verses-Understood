# Mark 2
theme: New Wine That Old Religion Cannot Contain
main: Mark 2 brings together five scenes in which Jesus' words and actions provoke questions from religious leaders. He forgives a paralyzed man before healing him, calls a tax collector and eats with sinners, explains why His disciples do not fast, and defends them for picking grain on the Sabbath. In each scene Jesus reveals that something new has arrived: the Son of Man who forgives sins, welcomes sinners, brings joy like a bridegroom, and is Lord of the Sabbath.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 1 showed Jesus' authority and growing popularity in Galilee. Mark 2:1–3:6 presents a series of five controversy stories, carefully arranged, in which Jesus' actions are challenged by scribes and Pharisees. The stories move from silent objection (2:6–7), to questions directed at the disciples (2:16), to questions addressed to Jesus (2:18, 24), and finally, in Mark 3:6, to a plot to kill Him. Mark shows how opposition to Jesus grew step by step.

The scribes were experts in the law of Moses who taught and interpreted it. The Pharisees were a popular movement devoted to careful observance of the law and the traditions that applied it to daily life, especially regarding purity, tithing, fasting, and the Sabbath. Many of them were sincere, but their system of rules often left little room for mercy toward people who did not measure up. Jesus' teaching and actions challenged their assumptions about who could be forgiven, who could be welcomed, and what God truly required.

### The Paralyzed Man Lowered Through the Roof (2:1–12)

Jesus returned to Capernaum, and word spread that He was [[v1|at home]], probably at Peter's house. So many people gathered that there was no room left, not even outside the door. While Jesus was speaking, four men arrived carrying a paralyzed man on a mat. Unable to reach Jesus through the crowd, they climbed onto the roof, dug through it, and lowered the man down in front of Jesus.

Homes in Galilee typically had flat roofs made of wooden beams covered with branches, reeds, and packed clay. An outside staircase often led to the roof, which was used for storage, work, and sleeping in hot weather. Digging through it would have been messy and disruptive, with dirt falling on the crowd below. But the men were determined.

Jesus, [[v5|seeing their faith]], said to the paralyzed man, [[v5|Son, your sins are forgiven you]]. This verse is studied below. Some scribes sitting there thought to themselves, [[v7|Who can forgive sins but God alone?]] They believed Jesus was committing blasphemy by claiming God's authority. Jesus knew their thoughts and asked which was easier to say: "Your sins are forgiven" or "Get up and walk." To demonstrate that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins, He told the man to get up, take his mat, and go home. The man did so in front of everyone, and they were all amazed and praised God, saying, [[v12|We never saw anything like this!]] (Matthew's account of this event is studied at Matthew 9:6.)

### The Call of Levi and the Meal With Sinners (2:13–17)

Jesus went out again beside the lake and taught the crowds. As He walked along, He saw Levi, the son of Alphaeus, sitting at the tax booth. Levi is also called Matthew (Matthew 9:9). Capernaum was located on a trade route and near the border between the territories of Herod Antipas and his brother Philip, so a toll station there collected customs on goods passing through. Tax collectors were widely despised because they worked for the ruling powers and often enriched themselves by overcharging. Jesus said to Levi, [[v14|Follow me]], and Levi got up and followed Him.

Later, Jesus was eating at Levi's house with many tax collectors and "sinners," people known for immoral lives or for not keeping the law as the Pharisees did. Mark adds that [[v15|there were many, and they followed him]]. When the scribes of the Pharisees saw this, they asked Jesus' disciples why He ate with such people. Sharing a meal signaled acceptance and fellowship, and the Pharisees believed that associating closely with sinners would make a person unclean.

Jesus answered, [[v17|Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance]]. A doctor goes where the sick are. Jesus came for those who know they need Him. (Matthew's account adds Jesus' quotation of Hosea 6:6, studied at Matthew 9:13.)

### The Question About Fasting (2:18–22)

At that time, John's disciples and the Pharisees were fasting. The law required fasting only on the Day of Atonement, but devout groups fasted more often; the Pharisees fasted twice a week (Luke 18:12). Some people asked Jesus why His disciples did not fast.

Jesus answered with a picture from a wedding: [[v19|Can the groomsmen fast while the bridegroom is with them?]] Jewish weddings were joyful celebrations lasting up to a week, and the bridegroom's friends were free from religious duties like fasting during the celebration. Fasting was associated with mourning, and it would be absurd to mourn at a wedding. Jesus' presence was like a wedding feast. In the Old Testament, God is pictured as the bridegroom of His people (Isaiah 62:5; Hosea 2:19–20), so Jesus' use of this image is a quiet claim about His identity. But He added, [[v20|the days will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them, and then they will fast in that day]]. This is the first hint in Mark of Jesus' death.

Then Jesus gave two short parables about new cloth and new wine, studied below. The new reality He was bringing could not simply be added onto the old patterns of religion.

### Picking Grain on the Sabbath (2:23–28)

On a Sabbath, Jesus and His disciples were walking through grain fields, and the disciples began to pick heads of grain. The law allowed travelers to pick grain by hand from a neighbor's field (Deuteronomy 23:25), but the Pharisees considered picking grain on the Sabbath a form of harvesting, which was forbidden work.

Jesus responded by reminding them of David. When David and his men were hungry and fleeing from King Saul, David entered the house of God and ate the consecrated bread, which only priests were allowed to eat (1 Samuel 21:1–6). Mark says this happened [[v26|at the time of Abiathar the high priest]]. In 1 Samuel the priest who gave David the bread was Ahimelech, Abiathar's father. Many scholars explain that Jesus was referring to the section of Scripture known by Abiathar's name or to the general period of Abiathar, who was the better-known high priest of David's time. Jesus' point was that human need took priority over a ceremonial rule, and David was not condemned.

Then Jesus stated a principle found only in Mark: [[v27|The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath]]. He concluded, [[v28|Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath]]. This saying is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 2:5 — Faith That Breaks Through Roofs

verse: Mark 2:5

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes at the moment the paralyzed man is lowered into the room where Jesus is teaching. The scene was dramatic. The house in Capernaum was so crowded that no one else could get in, not even near the door. Four men had carried their paralyzed friend on a mat, but they could not get near Jesus. Instead of giving up, they climbed onto the flat roof, dug through the layers of clay, branches, and beams, and lowered the man down right in front of Jesus. Pieces of roof must have fallen on the people below. It was a bold, disruptive, determined act.

Mark tells us, [[v5|Jesus, seeing their faith]]. Notice the word "their." Jesus saw the faith of the four friends as well as the paralyzed man. Their faith was visible in their actions: they believed Jesus could help, and they refused to let obstacles stop them from bringing their friend to Him.

Then Jesus said something unexpected: [[v5|Son, your sins are forgiven you]]. The friends had brought the man for healing. Jesus began with forgiveness. He called the man "son" (literally "child"), a term of tender affection. Jesus was not saying that this man's paralysis was caused by a particular sin. Rather, He was addressing the man's deepest need first: his relationship with God.

This statement provoked the scribes, who silently accused Jesus of blasphemy, because only God can forgive sins. Jesus then healed the man to prove that He had authority to forgive. The visible healing confirmed the invisible forgiveness.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that faith is often expressed in action. The four friends did not merely feel sympathy or say they believed; they acted, overcoming obstacles to bring their friend to Jesus. James writes that faith without works is dead (James 2:17). Real faith moves people to do something.

It also shows the power of intercessory faith, faith exercised on behalf of someone else. The paralyzed man could not get to Jesus by himself. His friends carried him. Jesus honored their faith. This encourages believers to bring others to Jesus through prayer and practical help, especially those who cannot come on their own.

Finally, the verse reveals that forgiveness is humanity's deepest need. Jesus healed the man's body, but He first healed his relationship with God. Physical healing would last a lifetime; forgiveness would last forever. Jesus' authority to forgive sins shows that He shares the authority of God Himself.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to be like the four friends, bringing others to Jesus. Many people are spiritually or physically unable to come on their own because of illness, despair, doubt, or circumstances. Believers can carry them through prayer, practical care, invitations, encouragement, and persistent love.

It also encourages believers not to give up when obstacles arise. The crowd blocked the door, but the friends found another way. When bringing people to Jesus seems difficult, believers can be creative and persistent, trusting that their efforts matter.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that Jesus cares about their deepest needs. People often come to God for help with circumstances, and He does care about these. But He also offers something greater: forgiveness and restored relationship with God. Believers can receive His forgiveness with gratitude and seek it for others as well.

### Mark 2:22 — New Wine Needs New Wineskins

verse: Mark 2:22

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the second of two short parables Jesus told when He was asked why His disciples did not fast like the disciples of John and the Pharisees. Jesus had answered that the friends of the bridegroom cannot fast while He is with them. His presence was a time of celebration, not mourning. Then He gave two images to explain what His coming meant.

The first image (v. 21) was of a patch. No one sews a piece of new, unshrunk cloth onto an old garment. When washed, the new patch would shrink and tear away from the old fabric, making the hole worse.

The second image is in this verse. In the ancient world, wine was stored in containers made from animal skins, usually goatskins. New wine was still fermenting, producing gas that stretched the container. A new wineskin was flexible and could expand. An old wineskin had already been stretched and had become dry and brittle. If new wine were poured into it, the skin would burst, and both the wine and the skin would be lost. [[v22|but they put new wine into fresh wineskins]].

Jesus' point was that the new reality He brought could not be contained within old religious forms. The joy of the kingdom, the forgiveness of sinners, the welcome of outcasts, and the presence of the Bridegroom required new ways of thinking and living. Trying to squeeze Jesus' message into the rigid patterns of the Pharisees' traditions would damage both.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that Jesus did not come merely to reform or patch up existing religion. He came to bring something new: the arrival of God's kingdom, the new covenant, and the gift of the Spirit. The Old Testament itself pointed toward this newness, as when God said through Isaiah, [[Isaiah 43:19|Behold, I will do a new thing]].

This does not mean that Jesus rejected the Old Testament. He fulfilled it (Matthew 5:17). But the way God's people related to Him would change: through faith in Jesus rather than through the ceremonial system, with the Spirit writing God's law on their hearts, and with Gentiles welcomed alongside Jews. The early church wrestled with these changes, as the book of Acts and Paul's letters show.

The parable also has a broader principle. Whenever God works in fresh ways, rigid patterns and traditions can resist His work. The problem is not tradition itself, but inflexibility that cannot receive what God is doing.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine whether their habits and traditions help or hinder them from receiving what God is doing. Traditions can be valuable when they express faith, but they become "old wineskins" when they are held so rigidly that they block the joy, grace, and mission of Jesus.

It also invites individuals to be open to transformation. When people come to Christ, He does not simply add religion to their old lives; He makes them new. Paul writes, [[2 Corinthians 5:17|if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation]]. Believers can allow Jesus to reshape their attitudes, priorities, and ways of living rather than trying to fit Him into old patterns.

Finally, this verse encourages churches to remain flexible in their methods while faithful to the unchanging gospel. The message of Jesus does not change, but the ways it is expressed and shared may need to adapt to reach new people and situations.

### Mark 2:27 — The Sabbath Was Made for People

verse: Mark 2:27

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words in response to the Pharisees, who criticized His disciples for picking grain as they walked through fields on the Sabbath. The Pharisees viewed this as a form of harvesting, one of the types of work forbidden on the Sabbath according to their interpretation of the law. Jesus had already answered by reminding them that David ate the consecrated bread when he and his men were hungry, showing that human need could take priority over ceremonial rules.

Now He gives a principle that only Mark records: [[v27|The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath]]. The Sabbath was established by God at creation, when He rested on the seventh day (Genesis 2:2–3), and it was commanded in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:8–11). Deuteronomy 5:12–15 adds that the Sabbath reminded Israel that they had been slaves in Egypt and that God had freed them. The Sabbath was a gift: a day of rest from labor, a day to remember God's goodness, and a protection for servants, animals, and even foreigners from endless work.

By Jesus' time, many rules had been developed to define what counted as work on the Sabbath. The intention was to protect the commandment, but the result was often a heavy burden. The Pharisees treated the Sabbath as if people existed to serve it. Jesus reversed this: the Sabbath exists to serve people. It was made for their good.

Then Jesus added, [[v28|Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath]]. As the Son of Man, He has authority over the Sabbath and the right to determine its true meaning. (This claim is discussed further at Matthew 12:8.)

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals God's purpose behind His commands. God's laws are not arbitrary burdens but expressions of His love and care for human beings. The Sabbath was designed for rest, worship, freedom, and delight. When religious rules are applied in ways that harm people or ignore their genuine needs, they have been twisted from God's purpose.

The verse also shows the connection between rest and Jesus Himself. As Lord of the Sabbath, Jesus offers true rest. In Matthew 11:28, He invites the weary to come to Him for rest. Hebrews 4 teaches that a Sabbath rest remains for God's people, entered through faith in Christ. The weekly Sabbath pointed toward this deeper rest of the soul in God.

Christians differ in how they apply the Sabbath today. Some keep Saturday as the Sabbath; most worship on Sunday, the day of Jesus' resurrection; some see the Sabbath principle as calling for a regular day of rest; others see it as fulfilled entirely in Christ. Whatever the practice, Jesus' principle applies: rest and worship are gifts meant for human good, not burdens to be used against others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to receive rest as a gift from God. Many people live without regular rest, driven by work, responsibilities, and constant connection. God designed human beings to need rest, and honoring that need is part of trusting Him. Setting aside regular time for rest and worship acknowledges that life depends on God, not on endless effort.

It also warns against using religious practices to burden or judge others. Believers can examine whether they impose rules on others that go beyond God's intention, or whether they criticize others for not following their particular practices. God's commands are meant to bring life and freedom.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to look at the purpose behind God's commands. When they read Scripture, they can ask not only what God commands but why, and how His commands express His love for people. This understanding leads to obedience that is joyful rather than burdensome.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 2 shows Jesus doing things that surprised and offended religious people: forgiving sins, eating with sinners, celebrating instead of fasting, and defending His disciples on the Sabbath. In each case, He revealed that something new had arrived with Him. This chapter invites believers to examine whether they, like the Pharisees, have become more attached to religious rules and appearances than to the grace and joy Jesus brings.

The story of the paralyzed man and his friends offers a picture of compassionate, determined faith. Believers are called to bring others to Jesus, especially those who cannot come on their own. This may involve prayer, practical help, and persistence when obstacles arise. It also reminds believers that Jesus offers forgiveness, the deepest healing anyone can receive.

The call of Levi and the meal with sinners challenge believers to welcome people the world or the church may reject. Jesus came for the sick, not the healthy. Churches and individual believers can ask whether people with messy lives feel welcome among them, and whether they are willing to share meals and friendship with those who are far from God.

The images of the bridegroom, the new patch, and new wine remind believers that following Jesus is a joyful and transforming reality, not just an addition to old patterns. Believers can allow Him to reshape their lives and remain open to His work. Finally, Jesus' teaching on the Sabbath shows that God's commands are given for human good. Believers can receive rest as a gift, honor God with regular rhythms of worship, and refuse to use religious rules as burdens for others.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You that You have authority to forgive sins and that You came to call sinners, not the righteous. Thank You for welcoming people the world rejects, and for welcoming me. Give me the determined faith of the four friends, who would not let any obstacle keep them from bringing someone to You. Show me who I can carry to You through prayer and practical love.

Keep me from treating my traditions and habits as more important than Your grace. Make me a new wineskin, flexible and open to the new life You bring. Thank You that You are the Bridegroom whose presence fills life with joy. Teach me to receive rest as Your gift, to honor You with regular times of worship, and never to use religious rules to burden others. You are Lord of the Sabbath and Lord of my life. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
