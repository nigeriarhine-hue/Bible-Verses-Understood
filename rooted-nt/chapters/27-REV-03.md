# Revelation 3
theme: Messages to Sardis, Philadelphia, and Laodicea
main: The risen Christ speaks to the last three churches. To Sardis, He says that it has a reputation for being alive, but it is dead; He calls it to wake up, strengthen what remains, remember what it received, obey, and repent, or He will come like a thief. A few in Sardis have not soiled their clothes, and they will walk with Him in white; the overcomer will be clothed in white, never blotted out of the book of life, and acknowledged before the Father. To Philadelphia, a church with little strength that has kept His word and not denied His name, Christ, who holds the key of David, has set before them an open door that no one can shut. He will make their opponents acknowledge that He has loved them, and because they have kept His command to endure, He will keep them from the hour of trial coming on the whole world. He urges them to hold fast so that no one takes their crown, and He promises to make the overcomer a pillar in God's temple, bearing God's name, the name of the new Jerusalem, and His own new name. To Laodicea, Christ, the Amen and faithful witness, says that they are neither cold nor hot but lukewarm, and He is about to spit them out. They think they are rich and need nothing, but they are wretched, poor, blind, and naked. He counsels them to buy from Him refined gold, white clothes, and salve for their eyes. Those He loves, He rebukes and disciplines, so they should be earnest and repent. He stands at the door and knocks; if anyone opens, He will come in and share a meal with them. The overcomer will sit with Him on His throne.

## Chapter Background and Story

Revelation 3 completes the seven messages to the churches. Like the first four, these messages reflect the real circumstances of each city and congregation. Sardis had a proud past but had grown complacent. Philadelphia was small and weak but faithful. Laodicea was wealthy and self-satisfied. Together with the churches in chapter 2, they present a full picture of the strengths and dangers that can be found in any church at any time.

Christ continues to speak with both honesty and love. He exposes spiritual deadness, praises quiet faithfulness, and confronts self-satisfied complacency. In every case, His goal is not to condemn but to restore. Even His sharpest rebuke, to Laodicea, ends with one of the most tender invitations in the Bible.

### Sardis: A Reputation Without Life (3:1–6)

Sardis had once been the capital of the ancient kingdom of Lydia, famous for its wealth under King Croesus. It was built on a high hill with steep cliffs and was considered almost impossible to capture. Yet twice in its history it had fallen because its defenders failed to keep watch, and enemies climbed the cliffs by night. By John's time, Sardis was living on its past glory. The church there seems to have shared the city's condition. The call to wake up is studied below.

[[v4|Nevertheless you have a few names in Sardis that didn’t defile their garments. They will walk with me in white, for they are worthy]]. Even in a spiritually dead church, a faithful few remained. Their "garments" were undefiled; they had not compromised with sin. White garments in Revelation symbolize purity, victory, and celebration.

[[v5|He who overcomes will be arrayed in white garments, and I will in no way blot his name out of the book of life, and I will confess his name before my Father, and before his angels]]. The "book of life" is God's record of those who belong to Him (Exodus 32:32–33; Daniel 12:1; Philippians 4:3). Ancient cities kept registers of citizens and removed the names of those who died or were disgraced. Christ promises that the names of overcomers will never be removed. He echoes His words in the Gospels: [[Matthew 10:32|Everyone therefore who confesses me before men, I will also confess him before my Father who is in heaven]].

### Philadelphia: An Open Door for the Faithful (3:7–13)

[[v7|“To the angel of the assembly in Philadelphia write: “He who is holy, he who is true, he who has the key of David, he who opens and no one can shut, and who shuts and no one opens, says these things:]] Philadelphia was a small city, often shaken by earthquakes. The "key of David" recalls Isaiah 22:22, where God gives a faithful steward authority over the king's house: what he opens, no one can shut. Christ holds full authority over access to God's kingdom.

[[v8|“I know your works (behold, I have set before you an open door, which no one can shut), that you have a little power, and kept my word, and didn’t deny my name]]. This church had "a little power," limited resources and influence. But it had remained faithful. Christ has set before it an open door. This may mean an open door to God's kingdom, which no opponent can close, or an open door for mission and witness (compare 1 Corinthians 16:9; 2 Corinthians 2:12). Both meanings encourage a small, faithful church.

[[v9|Behold, I make some of the synagogue of Satan, of those who say they are Jews, and they are not, but lie—behold, I will make them to come and worship before your feet, and to know that I have loved you]]. As in Smyrna, the Christians in Philadelphia faced hostility from some in the local synagogue, who may have excluded them and claimed they had no place among God's people. Christ promises that their opponents will one day recognize that He has loved these believers. The language echoes Isaiah 60:14, where nations come to bow before restored Israel; here it is reversed to comfort the persecuted believers. As with 2:9, this verse addresses a specific local conflict and must never be used to promote contempt for Jewish people. The goal described is not humiliation but acknowledgment of Christ's love.

[[v10|Because you kept my command to endure, I also will keep you from the hour of testing which is to come on the whole world, to test those who dwell on the earth]]. This verse has been read in several ways. Many futurist interpreters, especially those who hold a pretribulation view, see it as a promise that the church will be removed from the earth before a final period of great tribulation. Others understand "keep you from" to mean protection through the trial rather than removal from it, noting that Jesus prayed for His disciples, [[John 17:15|I pray not that you would take them from the world, but that you would keep them from the evil one]], using similar language. Preterist interpreters connect the "hour of testing" with trials that came upon the Roman world in the first century. In every view, the promise is that Christ will keep His faithful people secure.

[[v11|I am coming quickly! Hold firmly that which you have, so that no one takes your crown]]. The faithful are to hold on to what they have, their faith and their faithful witness, so that they do not lose their reward.

[[v12|He who overcomes, I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God, and he will go out from there no more. I will write on him the name of my God and the name of the city of my God, the new Jerusalem, which comes down out of heaven from my God, and my own new name]]. In a city frequently shaken by earthquakes, where residents sometimes fled to the countryside, the promise of being a permanent pillar in God's temple would be deeply meaningful. The overcomers will have a secure and lasting place in God's presence. They will bear God's name, the name of His city, and Christ's new name, marking them as His forever.

### Laodicea: Lukewarm and Self-Satisfied (3:14–22)

[[v14|“To the angel of the assembly in Laodicea write: “The Amen, the Faithful and True Witness, the Beginning of God’s creation, says these things:]] Christ calls Himself "the Amen," the One whose word is utterly reliable. He is "the Beginning of God’s creation," meaning not that He was created, but that He is its source and ruler (John 1:3; Colossians 1:15–18). Laodicea was a prosperous city known for banking, a textile industry producing black wool, and a medical school associated with an eye ointment. Each of these details appears in the message. Christ's rebuke and invitation are studied below.

[[v21|He who overcomes, I will give to him to sit down with me on my throne, as I also overcame and sat down with my Father on his throne]]. The greatest promise of all the seven messages is given to the most troubled church. Those who overcome will share Christ's throne. This shows the depth of His grace: no church is beyond His offer of restoration.

[[v22|He who has an ear, let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies.”]] The seven messages end with the same call. Every believer is to listen.

## Important Verses Explained

### Revelation 3:1–3 — Wake Up

verse: Revelation 3:1-3

#### Simple Explanation

Christ introduces Himself to Sardis as the One "who has the seven Spirits of God and the seven stars." He holds the fullness of the Spirit, who gives life, and He holds the churches. A dead church needs exactly what He has.

Then He gives a startling assessment: "I know your works, that you have a reputation of being alive, but you are dead." The church in Sardis looked successful. Others thought of it as a lively church. But Christ saw beneath the surface. Spiritually, it was dead.

In verse 2, He calls: "Wake up and strengthen the things that remain, which you were about to throw away." There was still some life left, but it was fading. He adds: "for I have found no works of yours perfected before my God." Their works were incomplete, perhaps begun with good intentions but not carried through in genuine faithfulness.

In verse 3, He gives the remedy: "Remember therefore how you have received and heard. Keep it and repent." They are to remember the gospel they first received, hold on to it, and turn back. Then comes a warning: "If therefore you won’t watch, I will come as a thief, and you won’t know what hour I will come upon you."

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The message to Sardis warns about the danger of appearances. A church, or a believer, can have a good reputation, active programs, and a respected name, yet lack spiritual life. God sees beyond appearances to the heart (1 Samuel 16:7).

The call to "wake up" would have struck the believers in Sardis with special force. Their city had fallen twice because its guards were not watching. Christ warns that spiritual complacency is just as dangerous. The image of His coming "as a thief" appears elsewhere in the New Testament to describe the unexpectedness of His return (Matthew 24:42–44; 1 Thessalonians 5:2). Here it may also refer to a coming in judgment on the church itself if it does not repent.

Yet the message is full of hope. Christ does not say the church is beyond help. There are "things that remain." Life can be strengthened. Remembering the gospel, holding on to it, and repenting can bring renewal. The One who holds the Spirit of life can raise what is dead.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers and churches today can be tempted to rest on reputation, past successes, or outward activity. This message calls them to ask honestly whether their faith is truly alive, whether they are walking with Christ, not just maintaining appearances.

Spiritual renewal often begins with remembering. Believers can return to the gospel that first brought them to faith: the love of God, the death and resurrection of Jesus, the forgiveness of sins, and the gift of the Spirit. Holding on to these truths and turning back to Christ brings fresh life.

The call to watchfulness also applies to everyday faith. Believers can stay awake spiritually through regular prayer, time in Scripture, honest self-examination, and accountability with other believers.

### Revelation 3:15–18 — Neither Cold Nor Hot

verse: Revelation 3:15-18

#### Simple Explanation

Christ's assessment of Laodicea is direct: "I know your works, that you are neither cold nor hot. I wish you were cold or hot." He continues in verse 16: "So, because you are lukewarm, and neither hot nor cold, I will vomit you out of my mouth."

Laodicea had a water problem. Nearby Hierapolis had hot springs, valued for healing. Colossae had cold, refreshing water. But Laodicea's water came through an aqueduct and arrived lukewarm and full of minerals, often making people sick. Christ uses this image: the church was neither healing like hot water nor refreshing like cold water. It was useless and distasteful.

The root problem is revealed in verse 17: "Because you say, ‘I am rich, and have gotten riches, and have need of nothing.’" The church was self-satisfied. But Christ says they did not know that they were "the wretched one, miserable, poor, blind, and naked."

In verse 18, He offers the solution: "I counsel you to buy from me gold refined by fire, that you may become rich." He offers white garments to cover their shame, and "eye salve to anoint your eyes, that you may see."

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The contrast between "hot" and "cold" is often understood as zeal versus open rejection, as though Christ would prefer outright hostility to half-heartedness. But the local background suggests a different meaning. Both hot and cold water were useful; lukewarm water was not. Christ is saying that the church had become spiritually ineffective. It was not bringing healing or refreshment to anyone.

Christ's counsel targets the city's points of pride. Laodicea was a banking center, but Christ offers true riches. It produced fine black wool, but Christ offers white garments to cover spiritual nakedness. It was known for an eye ointment, but Christ offers salve to cure spiritual blindness. Everything the city boasted about, the church lacked spiritually.

The phrase "buy from me" is ironic, since the church has nothing to pay with. This echoes Isaiah 55:1, where God invites the thirsty to come and buy without money. What Christ offers cannot be earned; it must be received from Him as a gift. The great danger of self-sufficiency is that it keeps people from seeing their need and coming to Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

In comfortable times and places, believers can easily slip into lukewarm faith, believing the right things but living with little passion, dependence, or impact. Material comfort can create a false sense that all is well spiritually.

This passage calls believers to honest self-examination. Do they depend on Christ, or have they come to rely on their own resources? Is their faith bringing healing and refreshment to others, or has it become comfortable and ineffective?

The remedy is to come to Christ for what only He can give: the true riches of His grace, the covering of His righteousness, and the clear sight of His truth. Recognizing one's need is the first step to renewal.

### Revelation 3:19–20 — I Stand at the Door and Knock

verse: Revelation 3:19-20

#### Simple Explanation

After His sharpest rebuke, Christ reveals His heart: "As many as I love, I reprove and chasten." His strong words are not signs of rejection but of love. He corrects the Laodiceans because He cares about them. Then He calls: "Be zealous therefore, and repent." They are to turn from their lukewarm complacency with wholehearted earnestness.

Verse 20 contains one of the most beloved invitations in Scripture: "Behold, I stand at the door and knock. If anyone hears my voice and opens the door, then I will come in to him and will dine with him, and he with me."

Christ pictures Himself standing outside the door of the church. He has been shut out by their self-sufficiency. Yet He does not leave. He knocks and calls. If anyone responds, He promises to come in and share a meal, a picture of close fellowship and friendship.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Verse 19 echoes Proverbs 3:12 and Hebrews 12:6: God disciplines those He loves, as a father disciplines his children. Christ's rebuke is an expression of His love, meant to bring His people back to Him. The harshest words in the seven messages lead directly to the tenderest invitation.

Verse 20 is often used in evangelism as an invitation to receive Christ for the first time, and it does express the gracious welcome He offers to all. But in its setting, the invitation is addressed to a church, to people who considered themselves believers. Christ was outside their door. Their self-satisfaction had pushed Him out of the center of their life together. He calls them back to fellowship with Him.

The invitation is personal: "if anyone." Even if the church as a whole remains lukewarm, any individual can respond. And the promise is intimate. In the ancient world, sharing a meal was a sign of friendship and acceptance. Christ wants not merely to be acknowledged but to share life with His people. The image also looks ahead to the marriage supper of the Lamb (19:9), the great feast of fellowship at the end of the age.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can take comfort in knowing that Christ's correction comes from love. When they sense conviction about sin or complacency, they can receive it not as rejection but as an invitation to return.

This passage also invites believers to examine whether Christ has been pushed to the edges of their lives, their families, or their churches. Busy schedules, comfort, and self-reliance can leave Him outside the door. Yet He keeps knocking. He longs for close fellowship with His people.

Opening the door means listening to His voice and welcoming Him back to the center: through repentance, renewed devotion, prayer, worship, and obedience. Those who do will find Him ready to come in and share life with them.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Revelation 3 completes Christ's messages to His churches, and its lessons remain urgent. The message to Sardis warns against living on reputation. A church or a believer can look alive and still be spiritually dead. Christ calls such believers to wake up, remember the gospel, hold fast to it, and repent. Even in dead places, a faithful few can remain, and they will walk with Him in white.

The message to Philadelphia encourages those who feel small and weak. This church had little power, yet it kept Christ's word and did not deny His name. Christ set before it an open door that no one could shut and promised to keep it secure. Faithfulness, not size or strength, is what Christ values.

The message to Laodicea confronts the danger of comfortable, self-satisfied faith. A church can be wealthy and think it needs nothing, yet be poor, blind, and naked in God's sight. Christ calls such believers to come to Him for true riches, clothing, and sight.

Throughout these messages, Christ's love shines through. He rebukes and disciplines those He loves. And He stands at the door and knocks, inviting anyone who hears His voice to open and share fellowship with Him. To those who overcome, He promises white garments, a permanent place in God's presence, and even a seat with Him on His throne. Every believer is invited to listen to what the Spirit says to the churches and to respond.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You know my works and see beyond appearances. Wake me up where I have grown spiritually asleep, and strengthen what remains in me. Help me to remember the gospel I received, to hold it fast, and to turn back to You. Thank You that You value faithfulness more than strength, and that You open doors no one can shut. Keep me secure in You, and help me to hold fast so that no one takes my crown. Save me from lukewarm, self-satisfied faith. Show me my true need, and give me the riches of Your grace, the covering of Your righteousness, and eyes to see Your truth. Thank You that You correct me because You love me. I hear You knocking; I open the door of my heart and my life to You. Come in and share life with me. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
