# Luke 14
theme: The Banquet of the Kingdom and the Cost of Coming
main: At a Sabbath meal in a Pharisee's home, Jesus heals a man and then teaches about humility, telling guests not to grab the best seats and hosts to invite the poor and disabled who cannot repay them. Through the parable of the great banquet, He shows that God's invitation goes out to the outcasts after the invited guests make excuses. Then, turning to the crowds following Him, He warns that discipleship requires putting Him above every other love and counting the cost before beginning.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 14 is set mostly around a dinner table. Meals in the ancient world were important social events that reflected and reinforced people's status. Where you sat, who invited you, and whom you invited all showed your place in society. Guests reclined on couches arranged around tables, with the most honored places closest to the host. Hosts often invited people who could invite them back, creating networks of social obligation. Jesus uses this setting to teach about God's kingdom, where these values are turned upside down.

The chapter continues Jesus' journey to Jerusalem. In Luke 13, He spoke of people coming from east and west to feast in God's kingdom while some who expected to be inside would be shut out (Luke 13:29–30). Luke 14 develops this theme of the kingdom banquet.

### Healing on the Sabbath (14:1–6)

On a Sabbath, Jesus went to eat at the house of a leading Pharisee. Sabbath meals were often special occasions with guests. Luke notes ominously, [[v1|they were watching him]]. In front of Jesus was a man suffering from [[v2|dropsy]], a condition in which the body swells with fluid, often a sign of serious heart, kidney, or liver disease. Some interpreters wonder whether the man had been placed there deliberately to test Jesus.

Jesus asked the lawyers and Pharisees, [[v3|Is it lawful to heal on the Sabbath?]] They said nothing. Jesus took hold of the man, healed him, and sent him away. Then He asked which of them, if a son or an ox fell into a well on the Sabbath, would not immediately pull him out. They had no answer. This is the fourth Sabbath healing in Luke (see 4:31–39; 6:6–11; 13:10–17), and each time Jesus shows that doing good on the Sabbath fulfills its purpose.

### Taking the Lowest Place (14:7–11)

Jesus noticed how the guests chose the places of honor at the table. He told them a parable. At a wedding feast, do not take the place of honor, because someone more distinguished may arrive, and the host may ask you to give up your seat. Then, embarrassed, you would have to move to the lowest place. Instead, take the lowest place, so that the host may say, [[v10|Friend, move up higher]], and you will be honored in front of everyone.

This was practical wisdom, echoing Proverbs 25:6–7. But Jesus was teaching something deeper about God's kingdom: [[v11|For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted]]. In God's kingdom, honor is not grasped but given. Those who humble themselves will be lifted up by God. (A similar saying is studied at Matthew 23:11–12.)

### Inviting Those Who Cannot Repay (14:12–14)

Then Jesus spoke to the host. When you give a lunch or a dinner, do not invite friends, relatives, or rich neighbors, who may invite you back and repay you. Instead, invite [[v13|the poor, the maimed, the lame, or the blind]]. This is studied below. Jesus' list matters: in some Jewish groups of the time, people with physical disabilities were excluded from certain religious gatherings. Jesus told the host to welcome exactly these people.

### The Parable of the Great Banquet (14:15–24)

Hearing this, one of the guests said, [[v15|Blessed is he who will feast in God’s Kingdom!]] Jews looked forward to the great banquet God would host at the end of the age (Isaiah 25:6). The guest seemed to assume that he and others like him would certainly be there.

Jesus responded with a parable. A man prepared a great banquet and invited many guests. As was customary, guests received an initial invitation, and when everything was ready, the host sent a servant to tell them, [[v17|Come, for everything is ready now]]. Accepting the first invitation and then refusing the second was a serious insult.

But they all began to make excuses. One had bought a field and needed to see it. Another had bought five pairs of oxen and needed to test them. A third had just married. These excuses were weak. No one would buy a field without seeing it first, or oxen without testing them. Even the newly married man could have come; the law exempted new husbands from military service (Deuteronomy 24:5), not from dinners. The excuses revealed that the guests simply did not value the invitation.

The host was angry and sent his servant out to the streets and alleys of the town to bring in [[v21|the poor, maimed, blind, and lame]], the same people Jesus told the Pharisee to invite. This is studied below. When there was still room, the master sent the servant to the roads and country lanes outside the town to [[v23|compel them to come in]]. The word "compel" does not mean force; it means urge strongly. People from outside the town, perhaps travelers or outcasts, would be reluctant to believe that the invitation was really for them, so the servant needed to persuade them. The master said that none of the originally invited guests would taste his banquet.

The parable describes how the religious leaders and many in Israel, who had been invited through the prophets, were refusing Jesus' invitation, while outcasts and sinners within Israel, and then Gentiles from outside, were coming in. (A similar parable is studied at Matthew 22, where it is set at a king's wedding feast.)

### The Cost of Discipleship (14:25–35)

Large crowds were traveling with Jesus. He turned to them and spoke words that would have stopped many in their tracks: [[v26|If anyone comes to me, and doesn’t disregard his own father, mother, wife, children, brothers, and sisters, yes, and his own life also, he can’t be my disciple]]. This verse is studied below. He added, [[v27|Whoever doesn’t bear his own cross and come after me, can’t be my disciple]].

Then Jesus told two short parables about counting the cost. Someone who wants to build a tower first sits down and estimates the cost, to see if he has enough to finish it; otherwise people will mock him for starting something he could not complete. And a king going to war against another king first considers whether his ten thousand soldiers can defeat twenty thousand; if not, he sends a delegation to ask for terms of peace. The first of these is studied below.

Jesus concluded, [[v33|So therefore, whoever of you who doesn’t renounce all that he has, he can’t be my disciple]]. Following Jesus means placing everything one has under His lordship.

Finally, Jesus spoke about salt. Salt is good, but if it loses its saltiness, it is useless, not fit even for the soil or the manure pile; it is thrown out. Salt in that region was often mixed with other minerals, and if the actual salt dissolved away, the remaining substance was worthless. A disciple who loses commitment to Jesus loses the very quality that makes them useful. Jesus ended, [[v35|He who has ears to hear, let him hear]].

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 14:13–14 — Invite Those Who Cannot Repay You

verse: Luke 14:13-14

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to the host of a dinner party, a leading Pharisee who had invited Jesus to a Sabbath meal. Jesus had just watched the guests compete for the best seats and had told them to take the lowest places instead. Now He turned to the host and addressed the guest list itself.

In verse 12, Jesus told him not to invite friends, brothers, relatives, or rich neighbors, because they might invite him back and repay him. In that culture, meals were part of a system of social exchange. People invited those of equal or higher status, and they expected to be invited in return. Hospitality built networks of influence and honor.

Then Jesus said, [[v13|But when you make a feast, ask the poor, the maimed, the lame, or the blind]]. These were people at the bottom of society. The poor could not return the invitation. The disabled were often excluded from social and even religious life. Some Jewish writings of the time excluded people with such conditions from certain community gatherings. Inviting them would bring no social advantage.

[[v14|and you will be blessed, because they don’t have the resources to repay you. For you will be repaid in the resurrection of the righteous]]. The blessing comes precisely because they cannot repay. Generosity that expects a return is merely exchange. True generosity gives without expecting anything back. God Himself will reward such generosity at the resurrection of the righteous, when He raises His people and establishes His kingdom.

Jesus was not saying it is wrong to share meals with family and friends. He used a common Jewish style of overstatement ("not this, but that") to make a point: generosity should especially reach those who cannot give back.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This teaching reflects God's own generosity. God invites people into His kingdom who have nothing to offer Him. Every believer is like the poor, the crippled, the lame, and the blind, welcomed to God's table by grace, not by merit. When believers invite those who cannot repay, they imitate the God who invited them.

It also fulfills Jesus' mission. In His first sermon, He announced good news to the poor and sight to the blind (Luke 4:18). In His answer to John the Baptist, He pointed to the blind seeing and the lame walking (Luke 7:22). His followers are called to share this concern for those on the margins.

Finally, the promise of repayment "in the resurrection of the righteous" shows that believers live with an eternal perspective. They do not need to be repaid now, because God will reward faithfulness in the age to come. This frees them to give generously without calculating what they will get back.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers to examine their hospitality and generosity. Do they mostly share their homes, time, and resources with people who can return the favor? Jesus calls His followers to welcome people who cannot repay them: the poor, the lonely, people with disabilities, the elderly, refugees, and others often left out.

Practical expressions might include inviting a lonely neighbor to a meal, including people with disabilities in church and community life, serving at a food ministry, or befriending someone who has few friends. These acts reflect the generous heart of God.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to give without seeking recognition or return. When they help those who cannot repay, they can trust that God sees and will reward them.

### Luke 14:21 — Bring In the Poor, the Maimed, the Blind, and the Lame

verse: Luke 14:21

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the turning point in the parable of the great banquet. Jesus told this parable after a guest at a dinner said, "Blessed is he who will feast in God's Kingdom!" The guest assumed that people like him would be at that feast.

In the parable, a man prepared a great banquet and invited many guests. When the meal was ready, he sent his servant to tell them to come. But one after another, they made excuses: one had bought a field, one had bought oxen, one had just married. These excuses showed that they did not value the invitation.

Then [[v21|That servant came, and told his lord these things]]. The master was angry. His invitation had been rejected and his honor insulted. But instead of canceling the banquet, he said to his servant, [[v21|Go out quickly into the streets and lanes of the city, and bring in the poor, maimed, blind, and lame]].

"Quickly" shows urgency. The banquet was ready, and the master wanted it filled. "Streets and lanes" were where the poor and outcast lived and begged. The list, "the poor, maimed, blind, and lame," exactly matches the list Jesus had just given to the host in verse 13. The master in the parable did what Jesus told the host to do.

In the context of Jesus' ministry, the first invited guests represent the religious leaders and those in Israel who assumed they belonged in God's kingdom but rejected Jesus' invitation. The poor and disabled from the town represent the outcasts and sinners within Israel who were responding to Jesus: tax collectors, prostitutes, the sick, and the poor. Then, in verse 23, the servant goes outside the town to the highways and hedges, which many interpreters understand as representing Gentiles.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that God's grace reaches those whom society overlooks. The kingdom of God is not reserved for the respectable and powerful. It is filled with people who know their need. Paul writes that God chose the foolish, the weak, and the lowly of this world (1 Corinthians 1:26–29).

It also shows the seriousness of rejecting God's invitation. The first guests were not rejected because they were sinful but because they were preoccupied with other things: property, business, and family. These were not evil in themselves, but they were valued above the master's invitation. Many people miss God's kingdom not because of terrible sins but because ordinary concerns crowd out their response to Him.

Finally, the master's determination to fill his house shows God's desire for many to come. God's banquet will not be empty. He keeps sending out invitations until His house is full.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to see themselves among the poor, crippled, blind, and lame, people who have nothing to offer God but are welcomed by grace. This understanding produces humility and gratitude.

It also challenges believers to examine whether ordinary concerns are keeping them from responding to God. Work, possessions, and family are good gifts, but they can become excuses for neglecting God. Believers can ask whether they are making excuses when God calls them to deeper commitment or service.

Finally, this verse calls believers and churches to go to the streets and lanes, reaching people on the margins with God's invitation. The master sent his servant out quickly and urgently. Believers can share the good news with those who feel excluded, assuring them that there is room at God's table.

### Luke 14:26 — Loving Jesus Above All

verse: Luke 14:26

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to large crowds who were traveling with Him toward Jerusalem. Many were enthusiastic about Jesus, perhaps hoping for miracles or expecting Him to establish a political kingdom. Jesus turned and spoke to them about what it really means to be His disciple.

[[v26|If anyone comes to me, and doesn’t disregard his own father, mother, wife, children, brothers, and sisters, yes, and his own life also, he can’t be my disciple]]. The WEB uses "disregard," but the Greek word literally means "hate." This shocking statement must be understood in its context and culture.

In Jewish and Semitic idiom, "love" and "hate" were often used as strong comparisons meaning "love more" and "love less." For example, Genesis 29:30–31 says Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah, and then says Leah was "hated," meaning less loved. Malachi 1:2–3 says God loved Jacob and hated Esau, describing His choice of one over the other. Matthew's parallel saying makes the meaning clear: [[Matthew 10:37|He who loves father or mother more than me is not worthy of me]].

Jesus was not commanding His followers to feel hatred toward their families. Elsewhere He affirmed the command to honor parents (Luke 18:20) and condemned those who used religious excuses to neglect their parents (Mark 7:9–13). He also commanded love for enemies, so He certainly did not command hatred for family. Rather, He was saying that love for Him must be so great that, by comparison, all other loves look like hatred. When loyalty to family conflicts with loyalty to Jesus, Jesus must come first.

[[v26|yes, and his own life also]]. Even one's own life must be held under Jesus' lordship. This connects with the next verse about carrying one's cross.

In that culture, family loyalty was extremely strong. Following Jesus could lead to rejection by family, loss of inheritance, and social isolation. Jesus wanted the crowds to understand the potential cost before deciding to follow Him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that Jesus demands first place in His followers' lives. No other relationship, not even the closest family bonds, can take precedence over Him. This is a claim that only God can rightly make. In the Old Testament, God commanded His people to love Him with all their heart, soul, and strength (Deuteronomy 6:5). Jesus makes the same claim for Himself.

It also shows that following Jesus may be costly. In many parts of the world today, people who become Christians face rejection by their families, loss of community, and even danger. Jesus' words prepare His followers for such costs and assure them that He is worth it.

Finally, putting Jesus first does not reduce love for family; it reorders it. When Jesus is first, believers love their families more truly, with His love, His patience, and His wisdom, rather than less. Family relationships flourish best when they are not made into idols.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine whether anything or anyone has taken first place in their hearts above Jesus. Family, romantic relationships, children, careers, and personal ambitions can all become rivals to Christ. Believers can ask God to help them love Him above all.

It also encourages believers who face family opposition because of their faith. Some are rejected or pressured by family members for following Jesus. Jesus knows this cost, and He promises a new family of believers (Luke 18:29–30). Believers can remain loving and respectful toward their families while staying faithful to Christ.

Finally, this verse must never be used as an excuse to neglect or mistreat family members. Jesus calls His followers to honor parents, love spouses, and care for children. Putting Jesus first should make believers better family members, not worse ones.

### Luke 14:28 — Count the Cost

verse: Luke 14:28

#### Simple Explanation

This verse begins the first of two short parables Jesus told about the cost of discipleship. He had just told the large crowds following Him that anyone who does not love Him above family and even their own life, and does not carry their own cross, cannot be His disciple. Now He uses everyday examples to explain why people should think carefully before committing to follow Him.

[[v28|For which of you, desiring to build a tower, doesn’t first sit down and count the cost, to see if he has enough to complete it?]] A tower might be a watchtower in a vineyard, used to protect crops, or a farm building. Anyone planning such a project would first calculate the cost of materials and labor. If he began without enough resources, he might lay the foundation and then be unable to finish.

The next verses describe the consequences: [[v29|everyone who sees begins to mock him]], saying, [[v30|This man began to build and wasn’t able to finish]]. An unfinished building would be a public embarrassment, a visible sign of poor planning.

Jesus then told a second parable about a king considering whether his army of ten thousand could defeat an opponent with twenty thousand. If not, he would seek peace while the enemy was still far away.

Jesus applied both parables: [[v33|So therefore, whoever of you who doesn’t renounce all that he has, he can’t be my disciple]]. Following Jesus is a serious commitment that requires giving Him everything. Before starting, people should understand what it will require.

The crowds following Jesus were large and enthusiastic. But many were following for the wrong reasons or without understanding where He was going. Jesus was going to the cross. He wanted followers who were committed, not people who would start enthusiastically and then fall away when following became difficult.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that discipleship is a lifelong commitment that should be entered thoughtfully. Jesus does not want shallow enthusiasm that fades when trials come. The parable of the sower describes people who receive the word with joy but fall away in time of testing (Luke 8:13). Counting the cost helps prevent this.

It is important to understand that counting the cost does not mean earning salvation through effort. Salvation is a gift of grace. But receiving that gift means surrendering one's life to Jesus as Lord. Grace is free, but it is not cheap; it costs us our self-rule.

The verse also suggests that Jesus is honest with His followers. He does not hide the cost to attract more people. Instead, He tells them the truth: following Him may cost family relationships, possessions, comfort, and even life. Yet He is worth it, as the parable of the treasure in the field teaches (Matthew 13:44).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages people considering faith in Jesus to think seriously about what it means. Following Jesus is not just adding religion to one's life; it is giving Him lordship over everything. Those who come to Him should do so with understanding and wholehearted commitment.

It also invites believers to renew their commitment regularly. Over time, believers may grow lukewarm or discouraged. Counting the cost again, remembering what Jesus asks and what He offers, can renew their resolve to keep following Him.

Finally, this verse guides how believers share their faith. Rather than presenting Christianity as a way to make life easy, believers can honestly explain both the cost and the incomparable worth of following Jesus. This honesty produces disciples who endure.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 14 challenges the world's values of status, exchange, and self-interest. At the Pharisee's table, Jesus taught humility: take the lower seat and let God lift you up. Believers can resist the desire for recognition and status, trusting that God honors those who humble themselves.

He also taught generosity toward those who cannot repay. Believers can open their homes and lives to the poor, the disabled, the lonely, and the overlooked, imitating the God who welcomes them without merit. Such generosity is rewarded not by social return but by God at the resurrection of the righteous.

The parable of the great banquet warns against making excuses when God invites. Ordinary concerns like work, property, and family can crowd out a response to God. Believers can examine their priorities and respond wholeheartedly to God's invitation. The parable also shows God's determination to fill His house with outcasts and outsiders, calling believers to share the invitation with those on the margins.

Finally, Jesus' teaching on the cost of discipleship calls for wholehearted commitment. He must come first, above even the closest family relationships and one's own life. Followers must carry their cross and surrender all they have to Him. Before starting, they should count the cost, and they should keep their commitment strong so that they do not become like salt that has lost its saltiness. Jesus is honest about the cost because He wants disciples who will finish the journey with Him.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You healed the sick on the Sabbath and taught that the humble will be exalted. Free me from the desire for status and the best seats, and teach me to take the lower place. Give me a generous heart that welcomes the poor, the disabled, and the lonely, without expecting anything in return.

Thank You for inviting me to Your banquet, though I had nothing to offer. Forgive me for the times I have made excuses when You called. Help me to share Your invitation with those on the streets and in the lanes, those who think there is no room for them. Help me to love You above everyone and everything, even my own life. Give me courage to carry my cross and follow You, and help me to count the cost and stay faithful to the end. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
