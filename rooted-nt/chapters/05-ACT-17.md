# Acts 17
theme: Reasoning from the Scriptures and Speaking to the Seekers of Athens
main: In Thessalonica, Paul reasons from the Scriptures that the Messiah had to suffer and rise, and many believe, but opponents accuse the believers of turning the world upside down and proclaiming another king, Jesus. In Berea, the people receive the word eagerly and examine the Scriptures daily. In Athens, distressed by a city full of idols, Paul speaks to philosophers at the Areopagus, proclaiming the unknown God as the Creator of all, who made every nation, is near to every person, and now commands all people everywhere to repent, because He will judge the world through the Man He raised from the dead. Some mock, some want to hear more, and some believe.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 17 continues Paul's second missionary journey. After leaving Philippi, Paul, Silas, and Timothy traveled west along the Via Egnatia, the great Roman road across Macedonia, to Thessalonica, and then to Berea. Paul then went south to Athens, the ancient center of Greek philosophy and culture.

The chapter shows Paul speaking to three very different audiences. In the synagogues of Thessalonica and Berea, he reasoned from the Hebrew Scriptures with Jews and God-fearing Gentiles. In Athens, he spoke to Greek philosophers who did not know the Scriptures, beginning instead with creation and with their own poets. His message was the same, but his approach adapted to his listeners.

### Thessalonica: "Another King, Jesus" (17:1–9)

Thessalonica was the capital of the Roman province of Macedonia, a large and prosperous port city. [[v2|Paul, as was his custom, went in to them; and for three Sabbath days reasoned with them from the Scriptures]]. His message: [[v3|explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, “This Jesus, whom I proclaim to you, is the Christ.”]] Many Jews expected a conquering Messiah, not a suffering one. Paul showed from passages like Isaiah 53 and Psalm 22 that the Messiah had to suffer and die, and from Psalm 16 that He would rise. Then he declared that Jesus was that Messiah.

Some Jews were persuaded, along with [[v4|of the devout Greeks a great multitude, and not a few of the chief women]]. But opponents became jealous, gathered troublemakers from the marketplace, and started a riot. They attacked the house of Jason, where Paul was staying, and when they could not find Paul and Silas, they dragged Jason and other believers before the city officials. Their accusation is studied below. The officials took a security payment from Jason and released them. Paul's letters to the Thessalonians, written soon afterward, show how deeply he loved this young church, which continued to face persecution (1 Thessalonians 2:14).

### Berea: Noble Examiners of Scripture (17:10–15)

The believers sent Paul and Silas away by night to Berea, about fifty miles away. There, too, they went to the synagogue. The Bereans' response is studied below. Many believed, including prominent Greek women and men. But opponents from Thessalonica came to Berea and stirred up the crowds. The believers sent Paul to the coast, while Silas and Timothy stayed behind. Paul's escorts brought him to Athens.

### Athens: A City Full of Idols (17:16–21)

Athens had been the intellectual capital of the ancient world, the city of Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle. By Paul's time, its political power had faded, but it remained a center of philosophy, art, and religion. It was filled with temples, statues, and altars to many gods. An ancient writer joked that it was easier to find a god in Athens than a man.

[[v16|Now while Paul waited for them at Athens, his spirit was provoked within him as he saw the city full of idols]]. Paul was not impressed by the beauty of the art. He was deeply distressed that people were worshiping idols instead of the living God.

Paul reasoned in the synagogue with Jews and God-fearers, and in the marketplace (the agora) every day with anyone who happened to be there, much as Socrates had done centuries earlier. Some Epicurean and Stoic philosophers debated with him. Epicureans believed that the gods were distant and uninvolved in human affairs, and that the goal of life was pleasure, understood as tranquility and freedom from pain. Stoics believed that a divine reason pervaded the universe, and that the goal of life was to live in harmony with reason and accept one's fate with self-control.

Some called Paul a [[v18|babbler]], literally a "seed-picker," a word for a bird that picks up scraps, used of someone who picks up bits of ideas without understanding them. Others said, [[v18|He seems to be advocating foreign deities]], because he preached about Jesus and the resurrection. Some may have thought "Jesus" and "Resurrection" (anastasis in Greek) were a pair of new gods.

They brought him to the Areopagus, which could refer both to a hill near the Acropolis (the "Hill of Ares," or Mars Hill) and to the council that met there, which oversaw matters of religion and education. Luke adds a slightly humorous note: [[v21|Now all the Athenians and the strangers living there spent their time in nothing else, but either to tell or to hear some new thing]].

### Paul's Speech at the Areopagus (17:22–31)

Paul began respectfully: [[v22|You men of Athens, I perceive that you are very religious in all things]]. He had noticed an altar with the inscription [[v23|TO AN UNKNOWN GOD]]. The Athenians, fearing they might have overlooked some deity, had built altars to unknown gods. Paul used this as a starting point: [[v23|What therefore you worship in ignorance, I announce to you]].

Paul then described the true God. He is the Creator of the world and everything in it, [[v24|being Lord of heaven and earth]], who [[v24|doesn’t dwell in temples made with hands]]. Standing within sight of the magnificent Parthenon, this was a bold statement. God [[v25|isn’t served by men’s hands, as though he needed anything, seeing he himself gives to all life and breath and all things]]. The gods of Greece were imagined as needing offerings and temples. The true God needs nothing; He is the giver of everything. Paul then spoke of God's purpose for humanity, studied below.

Paul quoted Greek poets. [[v28|For in him we live, move, and have our being]] is often attributed to the poet Epimenides, and [[v28|For we are also his offspring]] comes from the poet Aratus, who was from Paul's home region of Cilicia. Paul used their words to make his point: if we are God's offspring, [[v29|we ought not to think that the Divine Nature is like gold, or silver, or stone, engraved by art and design of man]]. Living, personal beings cannot have been made by lifeless images. Paul's conclusion is studied below.

### The Response (17:32–34)

[[v32|Now when they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some mocked]]. To Greek thinkers, the idea of bodily resurrection seemed foolish. Many believed the soul was immortal but the body was a prison to escape. Others said, [[v32|We want to hear you again concerning this]]. And some believed, [[v34|including Dionysius the Areopagite, and a woman named Damaris, and others with them]]. Dionysius was a member of the Areopagus council, a man of high standing. Damaris was a woman whom Luke considered worth naming. Paul's ministry in Athens did not produce a large church, but it produced real fruit.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 17:6–7 — Those Who Have Turned the World Upside Down

verse: Acts 17:6-7

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had preached in the synagogue in Thessalonica for three Sabbaths, and many people believed. But some of the Jewish leaders became jealous. They gathered troublemakers from the marketplace, formed a mob, and started a riot. They rushed to the house of Jason, where Paul and Silas were staying, but could not find them.

So [[v6|they dragged Jason and certain brothers before the rulers of the city, crying, “These who have turned the world upside down have come here also,]]. The accusation was meant as an insult. The opponents were saying that these men were troublemakers who caused disorder wherever they went. Yet their words were a remarkable testimony. In less than twenty years since the resurrection, the gospel had spread so widely that the believers were known for turning the world upside down.

[[v7|whom Jason has received. These all act contrary to the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus!]] This was a serious political charge. In the Roman Empire, Caesar was the supreme ruler, and some emperors claimed divine honors. Proclaiming another king could be treated as treason.

The charge was false in the sense intended. Paul was not leading a political rebellion against Rome. But it contained a deep truth. Paul did proclaim Jesus as King, the Lord whose authority is above every earthly ruler. Jesus' kingdom is not of this world (John 18:36), but it does claim the ultimate allegiance of every person.

The city officials were disturbed, but they released Jason and the others after taking a security payment.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the gospel has the power to transform the world. The believers did not have political power, wealth, or armies. But the message of Jesus changed lives, families, and communities wherever it went. What looked like upheaval to opponents was the beginning of God's new creation.

They also show the radical claim of the gospel: Jesus is King. The early Christian confession "Jesus is Lord" (Romans 10:9; 1 Corinthians 12:3) had a direct challenge built into it, since Caesar also claimed to be lord. Christians honored earthly rulers (Romans 13:1–7; 1 Peter 2:17), but they gave their highest loyalty to Christ.

Finally, the accusation shows a pattern seen throughout Acts and history: the gospel is often opposed with false or distorted charges. Jesus Himself was accused of claiming to be a rival king to Caesar (Luke 23:2; John 19:12).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers to consider whether their faith makes any difference in the world. The early believers were known for turning the world upside down. Believers today can ask God to make their faith active and transforming in their families, workplaces, and communities.

They also remind believers that Jesus is King over every area of life. Faith is not merely a private matter. Allegiance to Jesus shapes how believers think about money, work, relationships, justice, and politics. Believers are called to honor earthly authorities while giving their ultimate loyalty to Christ.

Finally, these verses encourage believers who face misunderstanding or false accusations. When believers live faithfully, others may misinterpret their motives. Believers can respond with patience, integrity, and trust in God, knowing that the early church faced the same.

### Acts 17:11 — Examining the Scriptures Daily

verse: Acts 17:11

#### Simple Explanation

After being forced to leave Thessalonica, Paul and Silas went to Berea, a smaller town about fifty miles away. As usual, they went to the synagogue and taught about Jesus.

[[v11|Now these were more noble than those in Thessalonica]]. The word "noble" originally referred to people of high birth, but it came to describe people of noble character, open-minded and generous in spirit. The Bereans showed this character in two ways.

First, [[v11|they received the word with all readiness of mind]]. They listened eagerly. They did not reject Paul's message out of hand or react with jealousy, as some in Thessalonica had done. They were open to hearing something new.

Second, they were [[v11|examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so]]. They did not simply accept everything Paul said because he was a persuasive speaker. They studied the Hebrew Scriptures every day to see whether his claims about Jesus were true. They tested his teaching by God's word.

These two qualities, eagerness and careful examination, belong together. The Bereans were neither closed-minded nor gullible. They were open to truth and committed to testing it.

[[v12|Many of them therefore believed]]. Their careful study led many of them to faith in Jesus.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the importance of Scripture as the standard for truth. The Bereans tested even the teaching of an apostle by the Scriptures. Believers are called to test all teaching by God's word. John writes, [[1 John 4:1|Beloved, don’t believe every spirit, but test the spirits, whether they are of God]].

It also shows that faith and careful thinking go together. The Bereans examined the evidence and came to faith. Christianity does not ask people to believe blindly. It invites them to examine the Scriptures and see whether these things are so.

Finally, the verse shows that eagerness for God's word is a mark of noble character. Psalm 119 is filled with expressions of delight in God's word: [[Psalm 119:97|How I love your law! It is my meditation all day]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to read and study Scripture regularly. The Bereans examined the Scriptures daily. Believers can develop habits of reading the Bible, thinking about it, and applying it.

It also encourages believers to test what they hear. Sermons, books, podcasts, and social media are full of teaching about God. Believers can listen eagerly while also checking what they hear against Scripture. Good teachers welcome this kind of examination.

Finally, the verse invites seekers to examine the evidence for themselves. Those who are curious about Christianity can read the Scriptures and consider whether the claims about Jesus are true. God honors honest seeking.

### Acts 17:26–27 — He Is Not Far from Each One of Us

verse: Acts 17:26-27

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was speaking to the philosophers of Athens at the Areopagus. He had begun by telling them about the unknown God, the Creator of the world who does not live in temples or need anything from human hands. Now he explained God's relationship with all humanity.

[[v26|He made from one blood every nation of men to dwell on all the surface of the earth]]. All people come from one ancestor. The Athenians were proud of their heritage and considered themselves superior to other peoples, whom they called "barbarians." Paul taught that all nations share a common origin. Every human being is part of one human family.

[[v26|having determined appointed seasons and the boundaries of their dwellings]]. God is sovereign over history and geography. He determines the times of nations and the places where they live. Nothing about human history is outside His control.

[[v27|that they should seek the Lord, if perhaps they might reach out for him and find him]]. God's purpose in creating and guiding the nations is that people would seek Him. The phrase "reach out for him" pictures someone groping in the dark, trying to find something by touch. Without God's full revelation, people search for Him, sensing that He exists but not knowing Him clearly.

[[v27|though he is not far from each one of us]]. God is not distant or uninterested, as the Epicureans believed. He is near to every person. He is not hiding. He wants to be found.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses teach the unity and dignity of all humanity. Every person is made by God and descended from a common ancestor. This truth rules out racism, ethnic pride, and contempt for other peoples. Every person, of every nation, bears the image of God (Genesis 1:27) and is precious to Him.

They also reveal God's desire for relationship with all people. God shaped human history so that people would seek Him. Jeremiah records God's promise, [[Jeremiah 29:13|You shall seek me, and find me, when you search for me with all your heart]]. God's nearness is an invitation.

Finally, these verses show God's sovereignty over the nations. The rise and fall of empires, the movements of peoples, and the boundaries of countries are all within His purposes. This gives believers confidence that history is not random.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses call believers to treat every person with dignity. Since all people come from one ancestor and are made by God, there is no place for prejudice or discrimination. Believers can actively love and respect people of every nation, ethnicity, and background.

They also encourage believers to recognize that people around them may be seeking God. Many people sense that there is something more, that God exists, even if they do not know Him. Believers can be attentive to these signs of seeking and gently point people to Jesus.

Finally, these verses offer comfort to those who feel far from God. God is not far from anyone. He is near, ready to be found. Anyone can reach out to Him, and He will respond.

### Acts 17:30–31 — Now He Commands All People Everywhere to Repent

verse: Acts 17:30-31

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been explaining to the philosophers of Athens who the true God is: the Creator who needs nothing, who made all nations, who is near to every person, and who cannot be represented by images of gold, silver, or stone. Now he came to the climax of his speech.

[[v30|The times of ignorance therefore God overlooked]]. In the past, the nations did not have the full revelation of God. They worshiped idols in ignorance. God was patient during these times, not bringing immediate judgment. This does not mean ignorance was innocent, but that God showed forbearance.

[[v30|But now he commands that all people everywhere should repent]]. Something has changed. Now that God has revealed Himself fully through Jesus, the time of patience has given way to a clear command. Everyone, everywhere, must repent: turn from idols and sin to the living God.

[[v31|because he has appointed a day in which he will judge the world in righteousness by the man whom he has ordained]]. There is a coming day of judgment. God will judge the world fairly and justly, through a specific Man whom He has appointed. Paul does not name Him here, but his listeners would soon understand that he meant Jesus.

[[v31|of which he has given assurance to all men, in that he has raised him from the dead]]. The proof that this Man is God's appointed judge is that God raised Him from the dead. The resurrection is God's public confirmation of who Jesus is.

At this point, some of the listeners began to mock. The idea of a dead man being raised seemed foolish to them. Others wanted to hear more.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the gospel calls for a response. It is not merely interesting information to be discussed. It is a command from God to repent. Paul's message in Athens, like Peter's at Pentecost, ended with a call to turn to God.

They also show that judgment is real and coming. God is patient, but He will not overlook sin forever. Jesus, the One who died for sinners, is also the One who will judge the world. This gives urgency to the call to repent. Paul later wrote, [[2 Corinthians 5:10|For we must all be revealed before the judgment seat of Christ]].

Finally, these verses show the central place of the resurrection. Paul pointed to the resurrection as God's proof. The resurrection confirms that Jesus is who He claimed to be and that God's promises are true. Paul later wrote that if Christ has not been raised, faith is useless (1 Corinthians 15:14–17).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses remind believers that the gospel calls everyone to repentance. Believers can share this message with kindness and respect, as Paul did, while also being clear that God calls all people everywhere to turn to Him.

They also encourage believers to live in light of the coming judgment. Knowing that Jesus will judge the world motivates believers to live faithfully and to share the gospel with urgency and love.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to expect different responses. Some mocked, some wanted to hear more, and some believed. When believers share their faith, they will meet the same range of responses. They can continue faithfully, trusting God with the results.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 17 shows how the gospel can turn the world upside down. The early believers had no political power, yet their message about King Jesus transformed lives and communities. Believers today can live out their faith in ways that make a real difference, honoring earthly authorities while giving their ultimate allegiance to Christ.

The chapter also shows the importance of Scripture. Paul reasoned from the Scriptures in Thessalonica, and the Bereans examined the Scriptures daily to test what they heard. Believers can study God's word regularly, receive it eagerly, and use it to test every teaching.

Acts 17 provides a model for sharing faith with people who do not know the Bible. In Athens, Paul began with what his listeners knew, their altar to an unknown God and their own poets, and led them to the truth about the Creator, the unity of humanity, and Jesus' resurrection. Believers can learn to understand the people around them, find points of connection, and clearly present the gospel.

Finally, the chapter calls everyone to respond. God is near to every person and commands all people everywhere to repent, because He has appointed a day of judgment through Jesus, whom He raised from the dead. Believers can share this message with respect, urgency, and love, accepting that some will mock, some will want to hear more, and some will believe.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord God, Creator of heaven and earth, You do not live in temples made with hands, and You need nothing from me, yet You give me life and breath and everything. Thank You that You made every nation from one ancestor, and that You are not far from any of us. Forgive me for the idols in my own heart. Help me to see people of every background as You see them, and to treat them with dignity and love. Give me a noble heart like the Bereans, eager to receive Your word and diligent in searching the Scriptures. Teach me to speak about You wisely, finding points of connection with those who do not yet know You. Thank You that You raised Jesus from the dead and appointed Him as the Judge of the world. I repent and turn to You again. Make my faith turn the world upside down for Your glory. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
