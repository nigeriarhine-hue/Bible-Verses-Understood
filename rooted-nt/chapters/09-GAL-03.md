# Galatians 3
theme: Children of Abraham Through Faith
main: Paul reminds the Galatians that they received the Holy Spirit by believing the message about Christ crucified, not by keeping the Law, and asks why they would now try to finish by human effort what began by the Spirit. He shows from Scripture that Abraham was counted righteous by faith and that God promised to bless all nations through him. Christ redeemed us from the Law's curse by becoming a curse for us, so that Abraham's blessing could come to the Gentiles. The Law, given centuries after the promise, could not cancel it; it served as a guardian until Christ came. Now all who believe are God's children, clothed with Christ, one in Him, and heirs of the promise.

## Chapter Background and Story

In chapters 1 and 2, Paul defended the gospel by telling his own story. In chapter 3, he turns to two kinds of evidence: the Galatians' own experience and the testimony of Scripture.

The teachers troubling the Galatians likely appealed heavily to Abraham. In Genesis 17, God commanded Abraham and his descendants to be circumcised as the sign of the covenant. The teachers probably argued that anyone who wanted to be counted among Abraham's children, and so share in God's blessing, needed to be circumcised and keep the Law given through Moses.

Paul meets this argument head-on. He goes back to Abraham, but to an earlier moment: Genesis 15, where Abraham believed God and was counted righteous, years before he was circumcised. He also points to God's promise in Genesis 12 that all nations would be blessed through Abraham. Paul argues that the true children of Abraham are those who share Abraham's faith, and that this blessing comes to the nations through Christ.

The chapter also addresses an obvious question: If righteousness does not come through the Law, why did God give the Law at all? Paul's answer gives one of the most important explanations in the New Testament of the Law's purpose.

### The Galatians' Experience (3:1–5)

[[v1|Foolish Galatians, who has bewitched you not to obey the truth, before whose eyes Jesus Christ was openly portrayed among you as crucified?]] Paul's language is strong. "Bewitched" suggests they had fallen under a kind of spell. When Paul preached among them, he proclaimed Christ crucified so vividly that it was as if a public notice had been posted before their eyes. How could they turn away from such a clear message? Paul's questions about how they received the Spirit are studied below.

[[v4|Did you suffer so many things in vain, if it is indeed in vain?]] The Galatians had apparently suffered for their faith, perhaps persecution of the kind recorded in Acts 14:2, 19, 22. Would they now throw all that away? Paul still hopes it is not in vain.

[[v5|He therefore who supplies the Spirit to you and does miracles among you, does he do it by the works of the law, or by hearing of faith?]] God was continuing to work powerfully among them, even miraculously. All this came through faith, not through Law-keeping.

### The Example of Abraham (3:6–9)

[[v6|Even so, Abraham “believed God, and it was counted to him for righteousness.”]] Paul quotes Genesis 15:6, the same verse he uses in Romans 4:3. Abraham was counted righteous because he trusted God's promise, not because of his works. [[v7|Know therefore that those who are of faith are children of Abraham]]. Being a child of Abraham is not a matter of physical descent or of circumcision, but of sharing Abraham's faith.

[[v8|The Scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, preached the Good News beforehand to Abraham, saying, “In you all the nations will be blessed.”]] Paul quotes Genesis 12:3. From the very beginning, God's plan included the Gentiles. Scripture [[v8|preached the Good News beforehand to Abraham]]. [[v9|So then, those who are of faith are blessed with the faithful Abraham]].

### The Curse of the Law and Christ's Redemption (3:10–14)

[[v10|For as many as are of the works of the law are under a curse. For it is written, “Cursed is everyone who doesn’t continue in all things that are written in the book of the law, to do them.”]] Paul quotes Deuteronomy 27:26. The Law required complete obedience, and it pronounced a curse on those who failed to keep it. Since no one keeps the Law perfectly, those who rely on the Law for righteousness place themselves under its curse.

[[v11|Now that no man is justified by the law before God is evident, for, “The righteous will live by faith.”]] Paul quotes Habakkuk 2:4, as he does in Romans 1:17. Even in the Old Testament, life before God was by faith. [[v12|The law is not of faith, but, “The man who does them will live by them.”]] Quoting Leviticus 18:5, Paul notes that the Law operates on the principle of doing. It is a different principle from faith. How Christ removes the curse is studied below.

### The Promise and the Law (3:15–22)

Paul uses an illustration from ordinary life. [[v15|Brothers, speaking of human terms, though it is only a man’s covenant, yet when it has been confirmed, no one makes it void or adds to it]]. Once a legal agreement or will has been ratified, no one can add new conditions to it later. In the same way, God made a binding promise to Abraham.

[[v16|Now the promises were spoken to Abraham and to his offspring. He doesn’t say, “To descendants”, as of many, but as of one, “To your offspring”, which is Christ]]. The Hebrew word for "offspring" (or "seed") can refer to one person or to many descendants. Paul sees its ultimate fulfillment in one descendant, Christ, through whom all of Abraham's true offspring are gathered (v29).

[[v17|Now I say this: A covenant confirmed beforehand by God in Christ, the law, which came four hundred thirty years after, does not annul, so as to make the promise of no effect]]. The Law came centuries after the promise to Abraham. It could not change the terms of a promise God had already made. [[v18|For if the inheritance is of the law, it is no more of promise; but God has granted it to Abraham by promise]]. The inheritance is a gift.

[[v19|Then why is there the law? It was added because of transgressions, until the offspring should come to whom the promise has been made]]. This is the natural question. Paul says the Law was "added because of transgressions." It made sin clear as a violation of God's will (Romans 4:15; 5:20), and it restrained sin in Israel's life. But it was temporary, meant to last "until the offspring should come," that is, until Christ. Paul notes that it was given through angels and a mediator, Moses (see Acts 7:53; Hebrews 2:2), while the promise came directly from God.

[[v21|Is the law then against the promises of God? Certainly not! For if there had been a law given which could make alive, most certainly righteousness would have been of the law]]. The Law is not opposed to God's promises. But it could not give life. Only God's Spirit, given through Christ, can do that. [[v22|But the Scripture imprisoned all things under sin, that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those who believe]]. Scripture shows that everyone is under sin, so that the promise comes to all in the same way: through faith in Christ.

### Under a Guardian; Children of God (3:23–29)

The Law's role as a guardian and the believer's new identity as God's child are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Galatians 3:2–3 — Begun in the Spirit

verse: Galatians 3:2-3

#### Simple Explanation

Paul asks the Galatians to think about their own experience. "Did you receive the Spirit by the works of the law, or by hearing of faith?" The Galatians knew how they had come to faith. They had heard the gospel of Christ crucified, believed it, and received the Holy Spirit. They had not first been circumcised or kept the Law. The Spirit came to them as a gift when they believed.

The phrase "hearing of faith" could mean hearing that leads to faith, or hearing the message about faith. Either way, the Spirit came through receiving the gospel by faith, not through religious performance.

Then Paul asks: "Are you so foolish? Having begun in the Spirit, are you now completed in the flesh?" "The flesh" here refers to human effort, especially relying on outward religious acts like circumcision. The Galatians had begun their new life by the power of God's Spirit. Now they were trying to bring it to completion by their own efforts. Paul sees this as completely backward.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The gift of the Holy Spirit was the great sign, promised by the prophets, that God's new age had come (Joel 2:28–29; Ezekiel 36:26–27). For Paul, the presence of the Spirit is the clearest evidence that someone belongs to God (Romans 8:9). The Galatians already had the Spirit, which proved they already belonged fully to God's people. Nothing needed to be added.

This passage also shows that the Christian life is not "begun by grace and finished by effort." It is lived from beginning to end by the Spirit through faith. Believers do grow, work, and obey, but they do so by the Spirit's power, not by self-reliant striving. Paul will develop this in chapter 5, where he describes walking by the Spirit and bearing the Spirit's fruit.

There is a temptation in every generation to think that, having been saved by grace, believers must now prove themselves or maintain their standing through their own performance. Paul's question exposes that error.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many believers start their Christian life with joy and dependence on God, but over time drift into a performance-based faith, measuring their worth by how well they keep spiritual disciplines or rules. This passage invites them to return to the beginning. The same Spirit who gave them new life is the one who will complete the work (Philippians 1:6).

This does not make effort unimportant. Prayer, Scripture reading, worship, and obedience matter. But they are ways of keeping in step with the Spirit, not ways of earning God's acceptance. Believers can approach these practices with freedom and joy, relying on God's power rather than their own.

### Galatians 3:13–14 — Christ Became a Curse for Us

verse: Galatians 3:13-14

#### Simple Explanation

Paul has just explained that everyone who relies on the Law for righteousness is under a curse, because no one keeps it perfectly. Now he announces the good news: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law."

"Redeemed" means bought back or set free by paying a price. In the ancient world, the word was used for freeing slaves or captives. Christ paid the price to set people free from the curse of the Law.

How did He do it? By "having become a curse for us." Christ took the curse on Himself. Paul supports this with Deuteronomy 21:23: "Cursed is everyone who hangs on a tree." In Israel, the body of an executed criminal was sometimes hung on a tree as a sign that he was under God's judgment. Jesus was crucified on a wooden cross, and in this way He took the place of one under the curse.

Verse 14 explains the purpose. First, "that the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Christ Jesus." Through Christ, the promise to bless all nations is fulfilled. Second, "that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith." The blessing of Abraham includes the gift of the Holy Spirit, received by faith.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses express the heart of substitution: Christ took what belonged to others so that they might receive what belonged to Him. This is the same "great exchange" Paul describes in 2 Corinthians 5:21. Christ, who never sinned, bore the curse that sin deserves, so that those who were under the curse could receive God's blessing.

For many Jews in Paul's day, the idea of a crucified Messiah was a contradiction. How could someone under God's curse be God's chosen one? Paul himself once thought this way. But after meeting the risen Christ, he understood: Jesus was under the curse not for His own sin but for others. The cross, which looked like God's rejection, was actually God's way of rescuing His people.

Notice that the blessing reaches both Jews and Gentiles. The "us" who were redeemed from the curse of the Law likely refers first to Jewish people, who were under the Law. But the result is that the blessing reaches the Gentiles too, and "we," all believers, receive the Spirit by faith. In Christ, Jew and Gentile share one blessing.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many people carry a deep sense of being under condemnation, haunted by guilt, shame, or the feeling that God is against them. This passage proclaims that Christ has borne the curse. For those who trust in Him, there is no curse left to bear. As Paul writes in Romans 8:1, there is no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.

This truth also calls for worship and gratitude. Christ willingly took the curse on Himself out of love. Remembering the cost of redemption can deepen love for Him and strengthen the desire to live for Him, not out of fear, but in thankfulness.

### Galatians 3:24–25 — The Law as Our Guardian

verse: Galatians 3:24-25

#### Simple Explanation

Paul uses an image familiar in the Greek and Roman world. The word translated "tutor" (sometimes "guardian" or "schoolmaster") referred to a household servant, often a slave, who was responsible for a child's supervision and discipline. This servant escorted the child to and from school, watched over their behavior, and sometimes punished them. The tutor was not the teacher but a supervisor. And once the child came of age, the tutor's role ended.

Paul says the Law played this role for God's people. In verse 23 he explains that before faith came, "we were kept in custody under the law." The Law guarded, restrained, and disciplined God's people for a time.

"So that the law has become our tutor to bring us to Christ, that we might be justified by faith." The phrase "to bring us to Christ" can mean either "leading us to Christ" or "until Christ came." Both ideas fit. The Law prepared the way for Christ and served until He came.

"But now that faith has come, we are no longer under a tutor." Now that Christ has come and faith in Him has been revealed, the Law's guardianship has ended. God's people have come of age.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This image shows both the value and the limits of the Law. The Law was good and served a necessary purpose. It revealed God's holiness, exposed sin, restrained wrongdoing, and pointed forward to Christ through its sacrifices and promises. Paul never dismisses it as worthless.

But the Law was temporary in its role as guardian. It was never meant to be the final stage of God's dealings with His people. Its job was to bring them safely to the time of maturity, when they would receive the full rights of children (Galatians 4:1–7). Returning to the guardian now would be like an adult insisting on being escorted to school by a childhood supervisor.

This does not mean that God's moral will has been abolished. Paul will say that love fulfills the Law (Galatians 5:14), and that those who walk by the Spirit bear the fruit that no law condemns (Galatians 5:22–23). Christians have differed on how exactly the Old Testament Law applies to believers today, but they broadly agree that the moral character of God revealed in the Law remains, while believers are no longer under the Law as a covenant system for righteousness. They live under Christ, by the Spirit.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

The Old Testament Law remains a precious part of Scripture. Believers can read it to learn about God's holiness, His justice, His care for the poor and vulnerable, and His plan of redemption that points to Christ. It still teaches, warns, and guides.

At the same time, this passage frees believers from living as though they were still under a guardian. Their relationship with God is not that of a child under constant supervision but of mature children who know and love their Father. Obedience flows from the Spirit's life within, not from external rules alone.

### Galatians 3:26–29 — All One in Christ Jesus

verse: Galatians 3:26-29

#### Simple Explanation

Paul now describes the new identity of believers. "For you are all children of God, through faith in Christ Jesus." The Galatians did not need to become Jews to become God's children. They were already His children through faith in Christ. The word "all" is important: every believer, without exception, is a child of God.

"For as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ." In baptism, believers are united with Christ. Paul uses the image of putting on clothes. Just as clothing covers a person, Christ now surrounds and defines those who belong to Him.

Then comes one of the most remarkable statements in the New Testament: "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." These three pairs represented the deepest divisions of the ancient world: ethnic and religious, social and economic, and gender. Paul declares that in Christ, none of these divisions determine a person's standing before God. All are equally God's children, equally clothed with Christ, equally heirs.

"If you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's offspring and heirs according to promise." Because Christ is the true offspring of Abraham (v16), all who belong to Him are Abraham's offspring too. The Gentile believers in Galatia did not need circumcision to be Abraham's children. They already were, through Christ.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage reveals the social power of the gospel. In the ancient world, a Jewish man might thank God daily that he was not a Gentile, a slave, or a woman, a prayer known from later Jewish writings and reflecting attitudes common in the period. Greek writers expressed similar sentiments. Paul's statement directly overturns such thinking. In Christ, all have equal access to God and equal dignity as His children.

Paul does not mean that ethnic, social, or gender differences disappear. Jews remained Jews and Gentiles remained Gentiles; men and women remained men and women. Christians from different traditions have discussed how this verse relates to questions such as roles in marriage and church leadership, and they have reached different conclusions on those questions. But all agree that this verse affirms the full and equal standing of every believer before God. No one is a second-class member of God's family because of their ethnicity, social status, or sex.

Over time, this vision helped transform societies. It challenged racial and ethnic prejudice in the church, and it planted seeds that would later grow into Christian opposition to slavery. The church has not always lived up to it, but the vision stands as a call to repentance and renewal.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage challenges every form of division and prejudice within the church. Believers of every ethnic group, economic level, and both sexes belong to one family. Churches can ask whether their life together reflects this unity: whether all are welcomed, valued, heard, and included.

It also gives each believer a secure identity. In a world that ranks people by race, wealth, education, or appearance, the gospel declares that every believer is a child of God, clothed with Christ, and an heir of God's promises. That identity cannot be taken away by others' opinions.

Finally, this passage calls believers to treat one another accordingly. If all are one in Christ, then contempt, favoritism, and exclusion have no place among God's people. The family of God is meant to display to the world a unity that only Christ can create.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Galatians 3 reminds believers how their life with God began and how it continues. The Galatians received the Holy Spirit by believing the message of Christ crucified, not by keeping the Law. Paul warns them not to try to finish by human effort what God began by His Spirit. Believers today face the same temptation to drift from grace into performance. The Christian life, from start to finish, is lived by faith and by the power of the Spirit.

The chapter also shows that God's plan has always been one of grace and faith. Abraham was counted righteous because he believed God, and God promised that all nations would be blessed through him. That promise is fulfilled in Christ, who took the curse of the Law on Himself so that the blessing of Abraham could reach the whole world. Those burdened by guilt can rest in the truth that Christ has borne the curse.

Paul's explanation of the Law helps believers read the Old Testament rightly. The Law was a good guardian that revealed sin and pointed to Christ, but it was never the way to become righteous before God. Now that Christ has come, believers live as mature children of God, guided by the Spirit.

Finally, the chapter gives a breathtaking vision of unity. In Christ, there is neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, for all are one in Christ Jesus. Every believer is a child of God, clothed with Christ, and an heir of the promise. This truth calls the church to welcome all equally and to reject every form of prejudice and exclusion, displaying the unity that the gospel creates.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, thank You that I received Your Spirit not by my own works but by believing the message of Christ crucified. Keep me from trying to complete by my own effort what You began by Your Spirit. Thank You that, like Abraham, I am counted righteous through faith. Thank You, Lord Jesus, for becoming a curse for me, so that I could be free from condemnation and receive the blessing of Abraham and the promise of the Spirit. Thank You for the Law that showed me my need and led me to You. Thank You that I am Your child, clothed with Christ, and an heir of Your promises. Help me to treat every believer as my brother or sister, whatever their background, status, or circumstances. Make Your church a living picture of our oneness in Christ Jesus. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
