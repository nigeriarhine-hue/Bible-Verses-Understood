# Matthew 9
theme: Authority to Forgive and Compassion for the Lost
main: Jesus shows that His authority reaches beyond physical healing to the forgiveness of sins. He calls a tax collector to follow Him, eats with sinners, explains that His coming brings something new, and restores the sick, the blind, the mute, and even a girl who has died. Moved with compassion for crowds who are like sheep without a shepherd, He asks His disciples to pray for workers for God's harvest.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 9 continues the series of miracles that began in chapter 8. Having shown Jesus' power over disease, storms, and demons, Matthew now adds His authority to forgive sin and to raise the dead. Alongside these miracles runs a growing conflict with religious leaders, especially scribes and Pharisees, who object to Jesus' claims and His friendships. The chapter ends with Jesus' deep compassion for the crowds and His call to prayer for laborers, which leads directly into chapter 10, where He sends out the Twelve.

### The Paralyzed Man Forgiven and Healed (9:1–8)

Jesus crossed the lake back to [[v1|his own city]], meaning Capernaum, where He had made His home (Matthew 4:13). Some people brought a paralyzed man lying on a mat. Mark and Luke add the memorable detail that his friends opened up the roof to lower him down because of the crowd (Mark 2:1–12). Matthew keeps the account short and focuses on what Jesus said.

Seeing their faith (the faith of the friends as well as the man), Jesus said, [[v2|Son, cheer up! Your sins are forgiven you]]. This must have surprised everyone. The man came for healing, and Jesus spoke first about forgiveness. Many in that culture assumed a connection between sin and sickness. Jesus did not say this man's paralysis was caused by a specific sin, but He addressed the man's deepest need first.

Some scribes thought to themselves, [[v3|This man blasphemes]]. In their view, only God could forgive sins, because all sin is ultimately against God. If Jesus claimed to forgive sins, He was claiming God's prerogative. Their reasoning was correct, but their conclusion was wrong. Jesus knew their thoughts and asked which was easier: to say "Your sins are forgiven" or "Get up and walk." Anyone could say the first words, since no one could see whether forgiveness had happened. Saying the second could be tested immediately. So Jesus did the visible miracle to prove the invisible one. He told the man to get up, take his mat, and go home, and the man did. The crowds were filled with awe and praised God for giving such authority.

### The Call of Matthew (9:9–13)

As Jesus walked on, He saw a man named Matthew sitting at the tax booth. (Mark and Luke call him Levi; he likely had both names.) Tax collectors in Galilee worked for Herod Antipas, collecting tolls and customs on goods passing through Capernaum, which lay on a trade route. They were notorious for overcharging and were seen as collaborators with oppressive rulers. Devout Jews considered them unclean and excluded them from religious life. Jesus said to him, [[v9|Follow me]], and Matthew got up and followed. Unlike the fishermen, who could return to fishing, Matthew walked away from a lucrative position he could not easily get back.

Matthew then hosted a meal at his house, and many tax collectors and "sinners" came to eat with Jesus and His disciples. "Sinners" referred to people known for immoral lives or who did not observe the law as the Pharisees did. In that culture, sharing a meal expressed acceptance and fellowship. The Pharisees objected, asking the disciples why their teacher ate with such people.

Jesus answered with a proverb: healthy people do not need a doctor; sick people do. Then He quoted Hosea 6:6: [[v13|I desire mercy, and not sacrifice]], telling the Pharisees to go and learn what it means. In Hosea, God criticized Israel for offering sacrifices while lacking faithful love. Jesus applied it to the Pharisees, who were careful about religious purity but lacked mercy toward sinners. He concluded that He came to call not the righteous but sinners to repentance.

### The Question About Fasting (9:14–17)

Next, disciples of John the Baptist asked Jesus why they and the Pharisees fasted often but His disciples did not. Jesus answered with an image from a wedding. Wedding celebrations in that culture lasted a week, and the groom's friends were free from religious duties like fasting during the celebration. It would be absurd for them to mourn while the bridegroom was present. Jesus identified Himself as the bridegroom, an image the Old Testament used for God in relation to His people (Isaiah 62:5; Hosea 2:19–20). But He added a somber note: [[v15|the days will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them, and then they will fast]], His first hint in Matthew of His coming death.

He then gave two short parables. No one sews a patch of unshrunk cloth onto an old garment, because when it gets wet the new patch shrinks and tears the old fabric. And no one pours new, still-fermenting wine into old, brittle wineskins, because the expanding wine would burst them. New wine needs new wineskins. Jesus was saying that the new reality He brought could not simply be added onto old patterns of religion. The kingdom He announced required new ways of living.

### A Ruler's Daughter and a Suffering Woman (9:18–26)

While Jesus was speaking, a ruler came, knelt before Him, and said, [[v18|My daughter has just died, but come and lay your hand on her, and she will live]]. Mark and Luke tell us his name was Jairus and that he was a leader of the synagogue. Matthew's shorter account says that the girl had already died, emphasizing the man's remarkable faith. Jesus got up and went with him.

On the way, a woman who had suffered from bleeding for twelve years came up behind Jesus and touched the fringe of His garment. Under Leviticus 15:25–27, a woman with ongoing bleeding was considered ritually unclean, and anyone she touched became unclean. For twelve years she had lived in isolation, unable to participate fully in worship or community. Jewish men wore tassels on the corners of their garments as reminders of God's commands (Numbers 15:38–40). She thought, [[v21|If I just touch his garment, I will be made well]]. Jesus turned, saw her, and said, [[v22|Daughter, cheer up! Your faith has made you well]]. He called her "daughter," welcoming her into the family of God, and publicly affirmed her healing.

When Jesus reached the ruler's house, professional mourners were already there. Families, even poor ones, hired flute players and wailing women for funerals. Jesus told them to leave because [[v24|the girl isn’t dead, but sleeping]]. They laughed at Him because they knew she was dead. Jesus was not denying that she had died; He was saying that death, in His presence, is like sleep from which a person can be awakened. He sent the crowd out, took her by the hand, and she got up. News of this spread throughout the region.

### Two Blind Men and a Mute Man (9:27–34)

As Jesus went on, two blind men followed Him, calling, [[v27|Have mercy on us, son of David!]] This is the first time someone in Matthew calls Jesus "Son of David" in a request, recognizing Him as the promised Messiah. The prophets had said that when God came to save His people, the eyes of the blind would be opened (Isaiah 35:5). Jesus asked them, [[v28|Do you believe that I am able to do this?]] They said, "Yes, Lord." He touched their eyes and said, [[v29|According to your faith be it done to you]]. Their eyes were opened. Jesus sternly told them not to tell anyone, perhaps because He did not want to be seen as a political messiah, but they spread the news everywhere.

Then a man who was mute because of a demon was brought to Him. When Jesus cast out the demon, the man spoke. The crowds were amazed, saying nothing like this had ever happened in Israel. But the Pharisees said, [[v34|By the prince of the demons, he casts out demons]]. They could not deny the miracles, so they attributed them to Satan. This accusation will return in Matthew 12, where Jesus responds to it directly.

### Compassion for the Crowds (9:35–38)

Matthew closes with a summary that echoes Matthew 4:23: Jesus went through all the towns and villages, teaching, preaching the kingdom, and healing every disease. When He saw the crowds, [[v36|he was moved with compassion for them because they were harassed and scattered, like sheep without a shepherd]]. Then He told His disciples that the harvest was plentiful but the workers few, and He asked them to pray for the Lord of the harvest to send out workers. In the next chapter, Jesus will send these same disciples out as the answer to that prayer.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 9:6 — Authority to Forgive Sins

verse: Matthew 9:6

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the turning point of the healing of the paralyzed man in Capernaum. His friends had brought him to Jesus on a mat, hoping for healing. Instead, Jesus first said, [[v2|Son, cheer up! Your sins are forgiven you]]. Some scribes, experts in the law, silently accused Jesus of blasphemy, because they knew that only God can forgive sins. Jesus knew their thoughts and challenged them with a question: which is easier to say, "Your sins are forgiven" or "Get up and walk"?

The point of the question is that forgiveness cannot be seen, so anyone could claim to forgive sins without being proven wrong. Healing paralysis, however, can be seen immediately. Jesus now says that He will do the visible miracle so that they may know He has the invisible authority: [[v6|But that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins]]. Then He turns to the paralyzed man and commands him to get up, take his mat, and go home.

The title "Son of Man" comes from Daniel 7:13–14, where one like a son of man comes before God and receives authority, glory, and an everlasting kingdom. By using this title, Jesus claims that heavenly authority. The phrase "on earth" is important: forgiveness, which was understood as something God granted from heaven, was now being given on earth through Jesus.

The man stood up and went home, proving that Jesus' words had real power. The crowds were filled with awe and glorified God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse makes one of the clearest claims in the Gospels about Jesus' identity. The scribes were right that only God can forgive sins. Jesus did not deny that; He demonstrated that He has the authority to do what only God can do. Matthew's readers are meant to draw the conclusion that Jesus shares God's authority because He is [[Matthew 1:23|God with us]].

The verse also shows that forgiveness is our deepest need. The paralyzed man and his friends wanted physical healing, and Jesus gave it. But He addressed the man's relationship with God first. Physical healing was temporary; the man would eventually die. Forgiveness of sins restored him to God forever. Jesus' healing ministry serves as a sign pointing to His greater work of saving people from sin, which He will accomplish through His death (Matthew 26:28).

Finally, the verse connects forgiveness and new life. The man who was forgiven got up and walked. In a similar way, God's forgiveness is not only the removal of guilt but the beginning of a new life of obedience and freedom.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse assures believers that Jesus has full authority to forgive sins. Many people carry guilt for things they have done, wondering whether they can ever be truly free. Jesus' words to the paralyzed man, "Your sins are forgiven," are words He still speaks to those who come to Him in faith. Believers can confess their sins honestly and trust that His forgiveness is real and complete.

It also invites believers to keep their priorities in line with Jesus'. It is natural to pray mostly for physical needs and circumstances, and God cares about these. But Jesus shows that our relationship with God is the most important matter. Praying for forgiveness, spiritual renewal, and reconciliation with God deserves a central place.

Finally, those who have been forgiven are called to "get up and walk," living new lives. Forgiveness is not permission to keep sinning but the freedom to begin again, leaving behind what once held us down.

### Matthew 9:13 — Mercy, Not Sacrifice

verse: Matthew 9:13

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to the Pharisees after they criticize Him for eating with tax collectors and sinners at Matthew's house. Matthew, a tax collector, had just left his tax booth to follow Jesus, and he invited his friends, other tax collectors and people with bad reputations, to a meal with Jesus. The Pharisees, who carefully avoided contact with people they considered unclean, asked Jesus' disciples why their teacher ate with such people.

Jesus first answered with a proverb: [[v12|Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick do]]. A doctor who avoided sick people would be useless. Jesus came for those who know they need help.

Then He told the Pharisees, [[v13|But you go and learn what this means]]. This phrase was a common way rabbis sent students back to study Scripture more carefully. It carried a gentle irony: these experts in the law needed to learn its meaning. Jesus quoted Hosea 6:6: [[v13|I desire mercy, and not sacrifice]]. In Hosea, God was rebuking Israel for continuing to offer sacrifices while their lives lacked loyalty and love. God was not rejecting sacrifices entirely, since He had commanded them. He was saying that rituals without mercy are empty, and mercy is what He values most.

Jesus applied this to the Pharisees. They were concerned with ritual purity and separation from sinners, but they lacked compassion. Jesus concluded, [[v13|for I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance]]. He came to call those who know they are sinners. The "righteous" may refer to those who consider themselves righteous and therefore see no need for Him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of God toward sinners. God is not repelled by sinful people in the way the Pharisees were. He seeks them out, invites them, and offers them restoration. Jesus' table fellowship with tax collectors and sinners was a living picture of God's welcome. Luke 15 contains three parables, the lost sheep, the lost coin, and the lost son, that make the same point: heaven rejoices when sinners repent.

The verse also warns against religious practice without mercy. Jesus quotes Hosea 6:6 again in Matthew 12:7, showing how central this principle is to His teaching. Religious activities, such as worship, giving, and moral discipline, matter, but they become distorted when they make people proud, harsh, or unwilling to help those who are struggling.

Notice that Jesus calls sinners "to repentance." His welcome is not approval of sin; it is an invitation to change. Matthew's own life shows this: he left his tax booth to follow Jesus. Mercy and the call to repentance go together. Jesus accepts people as they are, but He does not leave them as they are.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to show mercy to people the world, or the church, may look down on. Followers of Jesus can build genuine friendships with people far from God, sharing meals, listening, and caring for them, not to look down on them but because they too have been shown mercy. This does not mean joining in sin, but it does mean refusing to keep people at a distance because of their reputation.

It also invites believers to examine whether their religion has become more about rules and appearances than about love. If someone is more concerned with being seen as respectable than with helping those in need, Jesus' words apply.

Finally, the verse is good news for anyone who feels too sinful for God. Jesus came for sinners. The first step is simply to recognize one's need, like a sick person who goes to a physician, and come to Him. His welcome is real, and His call to repentance is the path to new life.

### Matthew 9:36 — Sheep Without a Shepherd

verse: Matthew 9:36

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes at the end of a long section describing Jesus' ministry. Matthew has just summarized that Jesus went through all the towns and villages, teaching in synagogues, preaching the kingdom, and healing every kind of disease. Everywhere He went, crowds gathered: the sick, the poor, the oppressed, and the curious. Now Matthew tells us how Jesus saw them.

[[v36|He was moved with compassion for them]]. The Greek word used for compassion is very strong; it refers literally to the inner organs and describes a deep, gut-level feeling of pity and love. Jesus did not look at the crowds as an inconvenience or merely as an audience. He felt their need deeply.

Matthew describes the crowds as [[v36|harassed and scattered]]. "Harassed" means troubled, weary, or mistreated. "Scattered" can mean thrown down or helpless. They were burdened by sickness, poverty, oppression, and religious leaders who loaded them with heavy requirements without helping them (Matthew 23:4).

They were [[v36|like sheep without a shepherd]]. This phrase comes from the Old Testament. Moses asked God to provide a leader so that Israel would not be [[Numbers 27:17|as sheep which have no shepherd]]. In Ezekiel 34, God condemned Israel's leaders as bad shepherds who fed themselves instead of the flock, and He promised that He Himself would shepherd His people and set over them one shepherd, His servant David. Jesus is the fulfillment of that promise: the Good Shepherd who cares for the lost and scattered sheep.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of Jesus, and through Him the heart of God, toward suffering people. God is not distant or indifferent. He sees human need and is moved by it. The compassion of Jesus is not only an emotion; it leads to action. In this case, it leads Him to call His disciples to pray for workers and then to send them out to minister (Matthew 10).

The image of a shepherd was widely used in the ancient world for kings and leaders. A good shepherd protected, fed, guided, and cared for the flock, even at personal risk. Israel's leaders had often failed in this role. Jesus came as the true shepherd, and in John 10:11 He says that the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep.

This verse also connects compassion with mission. Seeing people as harassed and helpless led Jesus to speak of a harvest. People's needs are not only a reason for sadness but an opportunity for God's kingdom to reach them through His workers.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to see people the way Jesus does. Crowds in cities, neighbors, coworkers, and even difficult people can be seen as harassed and helpless, burdened by needs that are often hidden. Asking God for a heart of compassion can transform how believers relate to those around them, replacing irritation or indifference with care.

It also calls believers to act on compassion. Jesus' compassion led to prayer and to sending workers. Believers can pray for people in need, for their communities, and for those who serve them. They can also look for ways to help, whether by meeting practical needs, offering friendship, or sharing the good news of Jesus.

Finally, this verse offers comfort to those who feel harassed and helpless themselves. Jesus sees such people with compassion, not disappointment. He is the Shepherd who seeks the scattered sheep and invites them to come to Him for care and rest.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 9 presents Jesus as the one who brings forgiveness, mercy, and new life to people who need it most. He forgives a paralyzed man, calls a despised tax collector, eats with sinners, heals a woman isolated for twelve years, raises a dead girl, and gives sight to the blind. Each story shows that no one is beyond His reach. Believers can bring their deepest needs to Him, beginning with the need for forgiveness, and trust that He has authority to meet them.

The chapter also challenges religious attitudes that keep people at a distance. The Pharisees objected when Jesus forgave sins and when He ate with sinners. Jesus answered that God desires mercy more than ritual, and that He came for the sick, not the healthy. Churches and individual believers can ask whether they welcome people with messy lives or quietly keep them away. Showing mercy does not mean ignoring sin, but it does mean treating others as people Jesus came to save.

The teaching about new wine and new wineskins reminds believers that following Jesus brings real change. Faith in Him is not simply adding a religious layer to an old life; it creates a new way of living. Habits, attitudes, and priorities may need to be reshaped to fit the life of the kingdom.

Finally, the chapter ends with Jesus' compassion for the crowds and His call to pray for workers. Believers today are called to share His compassion and to join His mission. This can begin with prayer for the people around us, and continue with willingness to be part of the answer: serving, helping, and speaking about the One who forgives, heals, and shepherds His people.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for Your authority to forgive sins and for the mercy You show to people the world looks down on. Thank You that You came for the sick and not the healthy, and that You call sinners like me to repentance and new life. Forgive my sins, and help me to rise and walk in the freedom You give.

Keep me from religion that values appearances more than mercy. Give me Your heart for people who are harassed and helpless, like sheep without a shepherd. Teach me to welcome those others avoid, to show compassion in practical ways, and to share the good news of Your forgiveness. Lord of the harvest, send out workers into Your harvest, and make me willing to be one of them. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
