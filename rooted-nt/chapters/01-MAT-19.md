# Matthew 19
theme: Marriage, Children, Riches, and the Impossible Made Possible
main: As Jesus travels toward Jerusalem, He answers a test question about divorce by pointing back to God's design for marriage at creation. He welcomes children whom His disciples try to turn away, and He tells a wealthy young man to sell his possessions and follow Him. When the disciples wonder who can be saved, Jesus answers that salvation is impossible for people but possible for God, and He promises that no sacrifice made for His sake will be lost.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 19 marks a geographical turning point. Jesus has finished His ministry in Galilee and now heads south toward Jerusalem, where He will be crucified. He crosses into [[v1|the borders of Judea beyond the Jordan]], the region east of the Jordan River known as Perea, which was also ruled by Herod Antipas. This was the same territory where John the Baptist had been arrested for criticizing Herod's marriage. Large crowds follow Jesus, and He heals them.

Chapters 19 and 20 address questions about family, wealth, status, and reward in God's kingdom. Several encounters reveal how the values of the kingdom differ from the values of the world, and they continue the theme of humility and dependence from chapter 18.

### The Question About Divorce (19:1–12)

Some Pharisees came to test Jesus: [[v3|Is it lawful for a man to divorce his wife for any reason?]] This was a debated issue. The law of Moses in Deuteronomy 24:1–4 allowed a man to divorce his wife if he found "some unseemly thing" in her and required him to give her a certificate of divorce. Two famous rabbinic schools disagreed about what "some unseemly thing" meant. The school of Shammai taught that it referred to serious sexual immorality. The school of Hillel interpreted it broadly, allowing divorce for almost any reason, even burning a meal or finding a more attractive woman. In practice, only men could initiate divorce in most Jewish settings, which left women very vulnerable. The question may also have been a trap, since Herod Antipas, ruler of the region, had divorced his wife to marry Herodias.

Jesus did not begin with the debate about Deuteronomy. He went back to creation. God made humanity male and female (Genesis 1:27) and said that a man would leave his parents, be joined to his wife, and the two would become one flesh (Genesis 2:24). Jesus concluded: [[v6|What therefore God has joined together, don’t let man tear apart]]. Marriage is God's work, a lifelong covenant union.

The Pharisees asked why Moses commanded a certificate of divorce. Jesus corrected them: Moses did not command divorce; he permitted it [[v8|because of the hardness of your hearts]]. The certificate was a regulation in a sinful world, protecting a divorced woman by allowing her to remarry. But [[v8|from the beginning it has not been so]]. God's original design was permanence.

Jesus then said that whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another commits adultery. The Greek word for sexual immorality, "porneia," is a broad term covering various kinds of sexual sin. Jesus' teaching was stricter than both rabbinic schools in practice, since it ruled out divorce for trivial reasons entirely.

The disciples were stunned: if marriage was this binding, maybe it was better not to marry. Jesus replied that not everyone can accept this teaching, only those to whom it is given. He spoke of eunuchs: some were born unable to marry or have children, some were made so by others (as servants in royal courts sometimes were), and some had chosen to remain unmarried [[v12|for the Kingdom of Heaven’s sake]]. In a culture where marriage and children were expected, Jesus honored those who remained single in service to God. Jesus Himself was unmarried, and Paul later spoke of singleness as a gift (1 Corinthians 7:7).

### Jesus Blesses the Children (19:13–15)

People brought little children to Jesus so that He would lay His hands on them and pray. It was a common practice to ask respected rabbis to bless children. The disciples rebuked those who brought them, perhaps thinking Jesus was too important or too busy for children. Jesus said, [[v14|Allow the little children, and don’t forbid them to come to me; for the Kingdom of Heaven belongs to ones like these]]. He laid His hands on them and blessed them. This scene echoes His teaching in Matthew 18:3–5: the kingdom belongs to those who receive it with childlike dependence, and those who welcome children welcome Him.

### The Rich Young Man (19:16–22)

A man came to Jesus and asked, [[v16|Good teacher, what good thing shall I do, that I may have eternal life?]] Matthew tells us he was young (v. 20) and wealthy (v. 22); Luke adds that he was a ruler (Luke 18:18). His question assumed that eternal life could be gained by doing some good deed.

Jesus responded, [[v17|Why do you call me good? No one is good but one, that is, God]]. Jesus was not denying His own goodness but challenging the man to think about what "good" really means. True goodness belongs to God alone. Then Jesus said that if he wanted to enter life, he should keep the commandments. When the man asked which ones, Jesus listed several of the Ten Commandments dealing with relationships with others, adding, [[v19|You shall love your neighbor as yourself]] (Leviticus 19:18).

The man replied that he had kept all these and asked, [[v20|What do I still lack?]] His question suggests that despite his outward obedience, he sensed something was missing. Jesus answered that if he wanted to be complete, he should sell his possessions, give to the poor, and he would have treasure in heaven; then he should come and follow Jesus. The young man went away sad, because he had great wealth.

### The Danger of Riches (19:23–26)

Jesus told His disciples that it is hard for a rich person to enter the kingdom of heaven. He added a startling image: [[v24|it is easier for a camel to go through a needle’s eye than for a rich man to enter into God’s Kingdom]]. The camel was the largest animal in Palestine, and the eye of a needle was the smallest opening. Some have suggested that "the eye of a needle" referred to a small gate in Jerusalem, but there is no evidence that such a gate existed in Jesus' time. Jesus was using deliberate exaggeration, as He often did, to describe something humanly impossible.

The disciples were [[v25|exceedingly astonished]]. Many people in that culture believed wealth was a sign of God's blessing. If the rich, who seemed favored by God, could not be saved, who could? Jesus answered, [[v26|With men this is impossible, but with God all things are possible]]. This is studied below.

### The Reward for Following Jesus (19:27–30)

Peter, perhaps thinking of the contrast with the rich young man, said, [[v27|Behold, we have left everything and followed you. What then will we have?]] Jesus responded with a promise. In [[v28|the regeneration]], the renewal of all things when the Son of Man sits on His glorious throne, the twelve apostles will sit on twelve thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel. Their sacrifice would be honored in the kingdom.

Jesus then extended the promise to everyone who has left houses, family members, or lands for His sake: they will receive a hundred times as much and inherit eternal life. Mark adds that they will receive these things "now in this time," with persecutions, referring to the new family of believers (Mark 10:30). Finally Jesus said, [[v30|But many will be last who are first, and first who are last]]. This saying, which also ends the parable in chapter 20, warns that God's evaluation often reverses human expectations.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 19:6 — What God Has Joined Together

verse: Matthew 19:6

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words in response to a question from the Pharisees designed to test Him. They asked whether it was lawful for a man to divorce his wife "for any reason." In Jesus' time, many teachers allowed men to divorce their wives for trivial reasons, and women had little ability to object or protect themselves. The Pharisees may also have hoped Jesus would say something that would get Him in trouble with Herod Antipas, who had divorced his wife to marry his brother's wife.

Jesus took the discussion back to the beginning. He reminded them that God made human beings male and female and that God declared a man would leave his parents and be joined to his wife, and the two would become one flesh (Genesis 1:27; 2:24). Then He drew the conclusion in this verse: [[v6|So that they are no more two, but one flesh]]. Marriage creates a profound union of two lives, physically, emotionally, and socially.

[[v6|What therefore God has joined together, don’t let man tear apart]]. Jesus teaches that marriage is not merely a human contract that can be dissolved at will. God Himself joins a husband and wife together. Human beings, whether the spouses themselves or others, should not break apart what God has united.

When the Pharisees asked why Moses allowed divorce, Jesus explained that Moses permitted it because of the hardness of human hearts, but that this was not God's original intention. He then stated that divorce and remarriage, except in cases of sexual immorality, amounts to adultery.

His listeners, including His own disciples, found this teaching shocking. They had grown up in a culture where divorce was relatively easy for men. Jesus was restoring God's design for marriage and protecting women from being discarded.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that marriage is part of God's good design for creation. It is a covenant relationship, a solemn commitment made before God, that reflects His own faithfulness. The prophets used marriage as an image of God's covenant love for Israel, and the New Testament presents marriage as a picture of the relationship between Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:31–32).

Jesus' teaching also shows how God's law accommodates human sinfulness without approving it. Divorce was permitted in the law because of hard hearts, to limit the damage sin causes. But the ideal remains lifelong faithfulness. This pattern helps believers understand that some biblical laws regulate a broken world rather than express God's highest desire.

Christians have understood the application of Jesus' teaching in different ways. Some traditions, such as the Roman Catholic Church, teach that a valid sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved, though an annulment may declare that a valid marriage never existed. Many Protestants believe divorce is permitted for sexual unfaithfulness and also for abandonment by an unbelieving spouse (1 Corinthians 7:15), and many also believe that abuse, which violates the covenant at its core, can be grounds for separation and divorce. Traditions also differ on remarriage after divorce. These questions require careful study and pastoral wisdom, and they should be handled with compassion for those whose marriages have been broken.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls married believers to treat their marriage as a sacred covenant joined by God. That means investing in the relationship, keeping promises, practicing forgiveness, seeking help when problems arise, and refusing to treat divorce as an easy escape from ordinary difficulties. It also means guarding marriage against unfaithfulness in thought and action.

For those considering marriage, this verse encourages careful thought and prayer. Marriage is a lifelong commitment, and entering it wisely is part of honoring God's design.

At the same time, this teaching must never be used to trap someone in danger. God's design for marriage includes love, faithfulness, and protection, and abuse betrays that design. A person facing abuse should seek safety and help, and the church should support them. Jesus' teaching protected vulnerable spouses from being cast aside; it should not be used to harm them. Those who have experienced divorce, whatever the circumstances, can find in Christ forgiveness, healing, and a welcome in God's family.

### Matthew 19:21 — What the Rich Young Man Lacked

verse: Matthew 19:21

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to a wealthy young man who had come asking what good thing he must do to receive eternal life. Jesus had told him to keep the commandments and listed several of them, including honoring parents and loving one's neighbor as oneself. The young man replied that he had kept all these from his youth, and then asked, [[v20|What do I still lack?]]

The man was sincere. He seems to have lived an outwardly moral life. Yet he sensed that something was missing. Jesus answered, [[v21|If you want to be perfect, go, sell what you have, and give to the poor]]. "Perfect" means complete or whole, the same word used in Matthew 5:48. Jesus was showing him what would make his obedience complete.

The command to sell everything was specific to this man. Jesus had listed commandments about loving others, and the man claimed to have kept them. But his wealth had a hold on his heart that kept him from loving his neighbor fully and from loving God above all. Jesus put His finger on the one thing the man could not give up.

Jesus promised that if he did this, [[v21|you will have treasure in heaven]], echoing His teaching in Matthew 6:19–21 about storing up lasting treasure. Then came the most important part: [[v21|and come, follow me]]. Giving away possessions was not an end in itself; it was a way of freeing the man to follow Jesus.

The young man's response was sad: he went away grieving because he had great possessions. He wanted eternal life, but not at the cost of his wealth. His possessions, in reality, possessed him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This encounter reveals that outward obedience is not the same as wholehearted devotion to God. The young man had kept many commandments, but his wealth had become an idol. Jesus' command exposed what truly ruled his heart. The first commandment, to have no other gods before the LORD, was the one he was breaking.

The story also shows that eternal life cannot be earned by doing "good things." The man asked what good thing he should do. Jesus' answer showed him that he could not reach completeness on his own. What he needed was not one more good deed but a new allegiance: following Jesus.

It is important to note that Jesus does not command every believer to sell everything. Some of His followers, such as Zacchaeus, gave away much but not all (Luke 19:8), and some wealthy followers supported Jesus' ministry (Luke 8:3). But Jesus does call every believer to put Him above all possessions and to be willing to give up whatever stands between them and Him. Generosity toward the poor is a consistent mark of faithful discipleship throughout Scripture.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to ask what might be holding them back from following Jesus wholeheartedly. For the rich young man it was wealth. For others it might be comfort, a relationship, a career, a reputation, or an ambition. Jesus lovingly identifies these idols and calls His followers to let them go.

It also challenges believers' relationship with money. In many societies, financial security and material comfort are highly valued. Believers can examine whether their possessions serve God's purposes or have become sources of identity and security. Practical steps include giving generously, living simply, caring for the poor, and holding possessions with open hands.

Finally, the story warns against assuming that moral respectability is enough. Many people live decent lives but have never surrendered themselves to Jesus. Following Him means more than avoiding wrongdoing; it means making Him Lord over everything.

### Matthew 19:26 — With God All Things Are Possible

verse: Matthew 19:26

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words after the rich young man has gone away sad, unwilling to give up his possessions to follow Jesus. Jesus then told His disciples that it is hard for a rich person to enter the kingdom of heaven, easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle. The image describes something completely impossible.

The disciples were astonished and asked, [[v25|Who then can be saved?]] In their culture, wealth was often seen as a sign of God's favor. If even the rich, who seemed blessed by God, could not enter the kingdom, what hope was there for anyone?

Jesus looked at them and said, [[v26|With men this is impossible, but with God all things are possible]]. The phrase "looking at them" suggests a moment of deliberate emphasis. Jesus did not soften His teaching about the danger of wealth. He agreed that salvation is impossible from a human standpoint. No one, rich or poor, can save themselves by their own efforts, goodness, or resources.

But God can do what people cannot. Salvation is God's work. He can change a heart that is attached to wealth, as He later did with Zacchaeus, a rich tax collector who gave half his possessions to the poor (Luke 19:1–10). He can bring anyone into His kingdom.

The phrase echoes Genesis 18:14, where God asked Abraham, [[Genesis 18:14|Is anything too hard for the LORD?]] when promising a son to elderly Sarah. Jesus applies the same truth to salvation.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse expresses the heart of the gospel: salvation is by God's grace, not by human achievement. The rich young man asked what good thing he could do to earn eternal life. Jesus' answer to the disciples makes clear that no human effort is enough. Paul later wrote, [[Ephesians 2:8|for by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God]].

This truth humbles everyone. The rich cannot buy their way in; the moral cannot earn their way in; the religious cannot qualify themselves. Every person depends entirely on God's mercy. Yet the same truth gives hope to everyone. No one is beyond God's reach. Those who seem least likely to be saved can be transformed by His power.

The verse is sometimes quoted as a general promise that God will do anything a person wants. In context, it is specifically about salvation: God can do what is impossible for humans by bringing people into His kingdom. It reveals God's power, but it should be applied according to His will and purposes, not as a guarantee of every human desire.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to stop relying on themselves for salvation. Whether through moral effort, religious activity, or good deeds, people often try to earn God's acceptance. Jesus says this is impossible. The right response is to trust God's grace and receive salvation as His gift.

It also gives hope for those who seem far from God, including family members and friends. Believers sometimes give up praying for people they think will never change. Jesus' words remind them that God can do what seems impossible. They can keep praying and keep loving, trusting God to work.

Finally, this verse encourages believers in their own struggles with sin and attachment to the things of this world. Letting go of idols, such as wealth, comfort, or control, may feel impossible. But God can change hearts. Believers can ask Him to free them from whatever holds them back, trusting His power to do what they cannot.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 19 addresses some of the most personal areas of life: marriage, children, money, and reward. In each area, Jesus calls His followers to see things from God's perspective rather than from the perspective of their culture.

On marriage, Jesus points back to God's design at creation. Marriage is a lifelong covenant joined by God, not a contract to be dissolved for convenience. Believers who are married can honor their covenant through faithfulness, forgiveness, and seeking help when needed. Those who are single can find dignity and purpose in serving God, as Jesus honored those who remain unmarried for the kingdom. And those whose marriages have been broken, including through abuse or abandonment, can find compassion and healing in Christ and in His church. Jesus' teaching protects the vulnerable; it must never be used to keep someone in danger.

Jesus' welcome of children reminds believers that no one is too small or insignificant for Him. Churches and families can make room for children, value them, and help them come to Jesus. It also reminds every believer to approach God with childlike trust.

The rich young man's story challenges believers' relationship with wealth. Possessions can become idols that keep people from following Jesus wholeheartedly. Believers can examine what they are unwilling to give up and ask God to free them. Generosity, simplicity, and care for the poor are practical expressions of a heart that treasures Christ above all.

Finally, Jesus' words about salvation and reward offer hope. Salvation is impossible for people but possible for God, and no sacrifice made for Jesus' sake will go unrewarded. Believers can trust God's grace for their salvation and follow Jesus generously, knowing that God's kingdom often reverses the world's rankings and that what is given up for Him will be repaid many times over.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for showing me Your good design for marriage and for honoring both those who marry and those who remain single for Your kingdom. Help me to keep my promises, to honor the covenants I make, and to treat others with faithfulness and love. Comfort those whose marriages have been broken, and protect those who are in danger.

Thank You for welcoming little children. Give me a childlike faith that depends on You. Show me anything I am holding onto more tightly than I hold onto You, and give me grace to let it go. Free me from trusting in wealth or my own goodness. I confess that salvation is impossible for me by my own efforts, but I thank You that with God all things are possible. Help me to follow You wholeheartedly, trusting that nothing I give up for Your sake will be lost. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
