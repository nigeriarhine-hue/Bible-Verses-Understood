# Hebrews 12
theme: Run the Race, Looking to Jesus
main: Surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, believers are to throw off every weight and the sin that entangles them, and run with endurance the race set before them, fixing their eyes on Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith, who for the joy before Him endured the cross, despising its shame, and sat down at God's right hand. Considering how He endured hostility, they will not grow weary. The hardships they face are the loving discipline of a Father who treats them as His children, training them to share His holiness; though painful at the time, discipline later produces the peaceful fruit of righteousness. So they should strengthen weak hands and knees, pursue peace with everyone and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord, watch that no one falls short of God's grace or lets a bitter root grow, and not be like Esau, who traded his birthright for a single meal. Believers have not come to the terrifying, untouchable mountain of Sinai, but to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, to joyful angels, to the church of the firstborn, to God the Judge of all, to the spirits of the righteous made perfect, and to Jesus, mediator of a new covenant, whose blood speaks a better word than Abel's. Therefore they must not refuse the One who speaks from heaven. God will shake earth and heaven once more, removing what can be shaken, but believers are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, so they should be thankful and worship God with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire.

## Chapter Background and Story

Chapter 11 ended with a long list of faithful people who endured hardship, many without receiving what was promised in their lifetimes. Chapter 12 turns that history into a call to action. The readers are now running the same race. The heroes of faith surround them like a great crowd in a stadium. And at the finish line is Jesus Himself.

The readers were weary. They had endured persecution in the past (Hebrews 10:32–34), but now they were growing tired and discouraged, perhaps facing new pressures. The author gives them three encouragements: look to Jesus, who endured far more; understand hardship as the loving discipline of a Father; and remember the glorious reality they have come to in Christ.

The chapter ends with the fifth and final warning in Hebrews: do not refuse the God who speaks. But it also contains one of the most glorious descriptions of the believer's privileges in all of Scripture (vv22–24).

### Running the Race (12:1–3)

The call to run with endurance, looking to Jesus, is studied below. [[v3|For consider him who has endured such contradiction of sinners against himself, that you don’t grow weary, fainting in your souls]]. When believers are tempted to give up, they can think about Jesus, who endured hostility from sinners far greater than anything they face. Considering Him renews strength.

[[v4|You have not yet resisted to blood, striving against sin]]. The readers had suffered, but not yet to the point of death, as Jesus and many of the faithful in chapter 11 had. This is not a rebuke but a perspective: their struggle, though real, has not yet demanded everything.

### The Father's Discipline (12:4–13)

[[v5|You have forgotten the exhortation which reasons with you as with children, “My son, don’t take lightly the chastening of the Lord, nor faint when you are reproved by him]]; [[v6|for whom the Lord loves, he disciplines, and chastises every son whom he receives.”]] The author quotes Proverbs 3:11–12. God's discipline is not a sign of rejection but of love. He treats believers as His children.

[[v7|It is for discipline that you endure. God deals with you as with children, for what son is there whom his father doesn’t discipline?]] [[v8|But if you are without discipline, of which all have been made partakers, then you are illegitimate, and not children]]. In the ancient world, a father invested in training his legitimate children and heirs. A child who received no training might be one the father did not acknowledge as his own. God's discipline shows that believers truly belong to Him.

[[v9|Furthermore, we had the fathers of our flesh to chasten us, and we paid them respect. Shall we not much rather be in subjection to the Father of spirits and live?]] If people respect human fathers who trained them, how much more should they submit to God, the Father of their spirits, whose discipline leads to life. The purpose and fruit of discipline are studied below.

[[v12|Therefore lift up the hands that hang down and the feeble knees]], [[v13|and make straight paths for your feet, so what is lame may not be dislocated, but rather be healed]]. Drawing on Isaiah 35:3 and Proverbs 4:26, the author pictures weary runners whose arms droop and knees buckle. They are to take courage, strengthen themselves, and make the path straight, not only for themselves but for weaker members of the community, so that those who are "lame" are healed rather than injured further.

### Pursue Peace and Holiness (12:14–17)

The call to peace, holiness, and watchfulness is studied below. [[v16|lest there be any sexually immoral person or profane person, like Esau, who sold his birthright for one meal]]. Esau traded his birthright, his inheritance as firstborn, for a bowl of stew (Genesis 25:29–34). He valued immediate satisfaction above God's blessing. "Profane" means treating sacred things as common.

[[v17|For you know that even when he afterward desired to inherit the blessing, he was rejected, for he found no place for a change of mind though he sought it diligently with tears]]. Later, Esau wanted the blessing, but it had been given to Jacob (Genesis 27:30–40). His tears could not undo his choice. "No place for a change of mind" likely means he could not reverse the consequences or change his father's decision. The warning is that some choices have lasting consequences. This is not meant to frighten those who sincerely seek God's forgiveness; God always welcomes the repentant. It warns against treating God's gifts with contempt.

### Sinai and Zion (12:18–24)

[[v18|For you have not come to a mountain that might be touched and that burned with fire, and to blackness, darkness, storm]], [[v19|the sound of a trumpet, and the voice of words; which those who heard it begged that not one more word should be spoken to them]], [[v20|for they could not stand that which was commanded, “If even an animal touches the mountain, it shall be stoned”.]] [[v21|So fearful was the appearance that Moses said, “I am terrified and trembling.”]] When God gave the Law at Mount Sinai, the scene was terrifying: fire, darkness, storm, trumpet blasts, and God's voice (Exodus 19:16–19; 20:18–21; Deuteronomy 9:19). The people were kept at a distance. Even Moses trembled.

[[v22|But you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and to innumerable multitudes of angels]], [[v23|to the festal gathering and assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven, to God the Judge of all, to the spirits of just men made perfect]], [[v24|to Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling that speaks better than that of Abel]]. In contrast, believers have come to Mount Zion, the heavenly city. Notice the tense: "you have come." By faith, believers already belong to this heavenly reality. They are part of a joyful celebration with countless angels and the whole church, "the firstborn," whose names are written in heaven. They come to God, the Judge of all, and to the righteous of past ages, now made perfect. And they come to Jesus, whose blood speaks a better word than Abel's. Abel's blood cried out for justice (Genesis 4:10); Jesus' blood speaks of forgiveness and reconciliation.

### An Unshakable Kingdom (12:25–29)

[[v25|See that you don’t refuse him who speaks. For if they didn’t escape when they refused him who warned on the earth, how much more will we not escape who turn away from him who warns from heaven]], [[v26|whose voice shook the earth then, but now he has promised, saying, “Yet once more I will shake not only the earth, but also the heavens.”]] This is the final warning in Hebrews. At Sinai, God's voice shook the earth. He promised through the prophet Haggai to shake heaven and earth once more (Haggai 2:6).

[[v27|This phrase, “Yet once more” signifies the removing of those things that are shaken, as of things that have been made, that those things which are not shaken may remain]]. God's final shaking will remove everything temporary, leaving only what is eternal. The believer's response is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Hebrews 12:1–2 — Looking to Jesus

verse: Hebrews 12:1-2

#### Simple Explanation

"Therefore let’s also, seeing we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses." The "witnesses" are the people of faith described in chapter 11. They surround believers like a crowd in a stadium. They are witnesses not mainly in the sense of spectators watching, but of those who bear witness, by their lives, that faith in God is worth it.

"Lay aside every weight and the sin which so easily entangles us." Runners in the ancient games stripped off anything that would slow them down. Believers must lay aside "every weight," things that may not be sinful in themselves but hinder their progress, and "the sin which so easily entangles," sin that wraps around them like a long robe tangling a runner's legs.

"And let's run with perseverance the race that is set before us." The Christian life is a race, not a sprint but a long-distance run requiring endurance. Each believer has a race "set before" them by God.

Verse 2: "looking to Jesus." Runners fix their eyes on the finish line. Believers fix their eyes on Jesus.

"The author and perfecter of faith." Jesus is the "author," the pioneer or champion who blazed the trail of faith, and the "perfecter," the one who completed it perfectly and who brings His people's faith to completion.

"Who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising its shame." Jesus endured the agony and humiliation of crucifixion because He looked beyond it to the joy that would follow: the joy of accomplishing salvation, of returning to the Father, and of bringing many children to glory. He "despised the shame," refusing to let the disgrace of the cross stop Him.

"And has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God." Jesus finished His race and now reigns in glory.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses describe the heart of Christian endurance. The secret is not willpower but focus: looking to Jesus. He is both the model and the source of faith. He ran the race before His people, and He runs with them now.

The word "joy" is striking in connection with the cross. Jesus did not enjoy suffering, but He endured it for the joy beyond it. This gives believers a pattern for their own hardships: looking beyond present pain to the joy God has promised.

The phrase "the race that is set before us" suggests that each believer's race is unique. God sets the course. Some face more obvious hardships than others. But all are called to run with endurance and to keep their eyes on Jesus.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can ask what weights are slowing them down. These might include distractions, unhealthy relationships, misplaced priorities, or habits that are not sinful in themselves but hinder their spiritual growth. They can also identify specific sins that tend to entangle them and ask for God's help to put them off.

When believers are weary, discouraged, or tempted to quit, they can look to Jesus. Reading the Gospels, meditating on His endurance, and remembering His victory can renew their strength.

Finally, believers can remember that they are not alone. The faithful of past generations show that the race can be run. And Jesus Himself waits at the finish line.

### Hebrews 12:10–11 — The Peaceful Fruit of Righteousness

verse: Hebrews 12:10-11

#### Simple Explanation

The author compares human fathers with God. "For they indeed for a few days disciplined us as seemed good to them." Human fathers disciplined their children for a limited time, during childhood, and according to their own judgment, which was not always perfect.

"But he for our profit, that we may be partakers of his holiness." God's discipline is always for believers' good. Its goal is that they would share in His holiness, becoming more like Him.

Verse 11: "All chastening seems for the present to be not joyous but grievous." Discipline is painful. The author does not pretend otherwise.

"Yet afterward it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it." Over time, discipline produces good results: righteousness and peace. "Trained" is the athletic word from which "gymnasium" comes. Discipline is like an athlete's training, difficult but strengthening.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

"Discipline" in Hebrews means training, not merely punishment. It includes all the ways God shapes His children, especially through hardship. The readers' sufferings, including persecution, were being used by God to train them. This does not mean God causes every evil; persecution came from human sin. But God uses even hardship for His children's good (Romans 8:28; James 1:2–4).

It is very important to read this passage rightly. It describes God's perfect, loving discipline, not human abuse. The author acknowledges that human fathers disciplined "as seemed good to them," that is, imperfectly. This passage must never be used to justify harsh treatment, abuse, or violence toward children or anyone else. God's discipline is always loving, always purposeful, and always for good. Human discipline that harms, humiliates, or terrorizes is not a reflection of God's character. Those who have been abused, whether by parents, spouses, or leaders, should know that God does not approve of what was done to them, and that seeking safety and help is right.

The promise of "the peaceful fruit of righteousness" gives hope. Believers may not understand their hardships now, but God is producing something good in them. Romans 5:3–5 describes a similar process: suffering produces endurance, endurance produces character, and character produces hope.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

When believers face hardships, they can ask not only "Why is this happening?" but also "What is God teaching me through this?" Hardship can become a means of growth, deepening trust, patience, and holiness.

Believers can also be honest about pain. The author admits that discipline is "not joyous but grievous." Faith does not require pretending that hard things are pleasant.

Finally, believers can hold onto hope. The fruit of discipline often comes "afterward." What seems painful and pointless now may later produce peace and righteousness.

### Hebrews 12:14–15 — Pursue Peace and Holiness

verse: Hebrews 12:14-15

#### Simple Explanation

"Follow after peace with all men." "Follow after" means to pursue eagerly, to chase. Believers are to actively seek peace with everyone, not just fellow believers, as far as it depends on them (Romans 12:18).

"And the sanctification without which no man will see the Lord." Believers must also pursue holiness. "Sanctification" here means holiness of life. Those who do not pursue holiness will not see the Lord. This does not mean that holiness earns salvation, which Hebrews has clearly shown comes through Christ's sacrifice. It means that genuine faith always produces a growing holiness, and those who reject holiness show that they do not truly know God. Jesus said, [[Matthew 5:8|Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God]].

Verse 15: "looking carefully lest there be any man who falls short of the grace of God." Believers are to watch over one another, making sure no one drifts away from God's grace.

"Lest any root of bitterness springing up trouble you and many be defiled by it." Bitterness is like a poisonous root. It may start small, but it grows and spreads, affecting many people. This phrase echoes Deuteronomy 29:18, which warns against a root bearing bitter poison among God's people.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Peace and holiness go together. Peace without holiness can become compromise, tolerating sin for the sake of avoiding conflict. Holiness without peace can become harshness, judging others without love. Believers are called to pursue both.

The call to watch over one another is a community responsibility. As in Hebrews 3:12–13 and 10:24–25, believers are responsible for one another's spiritual well-being. They are to notice when someone is drifting and help them return.

Bitterness is especially dangerous because it spreads. A bitter person can poison a whole community through gossip, resentment, and division. Paul urges believers to put away all bitterness and to forgive one another as God in Christ forgave them (Ephesians 4:31–32). Addressing bitterness, including bitterness arising from real hurts, is important for the health of both individuals and communities. Healing may require honest acknowledgment of wrongs, forgiveness, and sometimes the help of a counselor or pastor.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can ask whether they are actively pursuing peace in their relationships. Are there conflicts they need to address, apologies to make, or bridges to rebuild?

They can also examine their pursuit of holiness. Are there areas of life they have not surrendered to God? Holiness is not perfection, but it is a direction of life.

Finally, believers can watch for bitterness in their own hearts. Unresolved hurt can turn into bitterness that poisons relationships. Bringing those hurts to God, seeking healing, and choosing to forgive can uproot bitterness before it spreads.

### Hebrews 12:28–29 — A Kingdom That Cannot Be Shaken

verse: Hebrews 12:28-29

#### Simple Explanation

"Therefore, receiving a Kingdom that can’t be shaken." God will one day shake heaven and earth, removing everything temporary (vv26–27). But believers are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken. God's kingdom is eternal and secure. Notice the present tense: believers are already receiving it.

"Let's have grace, through which we serve God acceptably." The phrase can also be translated "let us be thankful." The right response to receiving an unshakable kingdom is gratitude, which leads to serving God in a way that pleases Him.

"With reverence and awe." Worship should be marked by reverence and holy fear. Though believers have bold access to God through Christ (Hebrews 4:16; 10:19), they should never treat Him lightly.

Verse 29: "for our God is a consuming fire." This phrase comes from Deuteronomy 4:24. God is holy, and His holiness consumes everything impure. The God of the new covenant is the same holy God who appeared at Sinai.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses bring together the security and the seriousness of the Christian life. Believers are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken. Everything else in the world, governments, economies, institutions, and even the physical universe, can and will be shaken. But God's kingdom will stand forever. Daniel 2:44 prophesied that God would set up a kingdom that would never be destroyed.

At the same time, the God who gives this kingdom is a consuming fire. The new covenant brings believers near to God, but it does not make Him less holy. Grace does not cancel reverence; it deepens it. Those who understand how much God has given and how holy He is will respond with both gratitude and awe.

The call to serve God "acceptably" means worshiping Him in ways that please Him. Hebrews 13:15–16 will explain that acceptable worship includes praise, confession of His name, doing good, and sharing with others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

In a world where so much feels unstable, believers can rest in the security of God's kingdom. Whatever is shaken in their lives, whether health, finances, relationships, or nations, God's kingdom remains.

This truth calls for gratitude. Believers can cultivate thankfulness in prayer and worship, remembering the unshakable kingdom they are receiving.

Finally, believers can approach God with both confidence and reverence. They are welcome in His presence through Christ, but He is holy. Worship that combines joy and awe honors the God who is both loving and a consuming fire.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Hebrews 12 calls weary believers to keep running. Surrounded by a great cloud of witnesses who proved that faith is worth it, they are to throw off every weight and entangling sin and run with endurance the race God has set before them. The key is to look to Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith, who endured the cross for the joy set before Him and is now seated at God's right hand. When believers grow tired, they can consider Him.

The chapter also helps believers understand hardship. God uses the trials of life as loving discipline, training His children to share in His holiness. Discipline is painful at the time, but later it produces the peaceful fruit of righteousness. This describes God's perfect love, never human abuse, which God opposes. Believers can face hardship with honesty and hope, trusting that God is at work.

Believers are called to strengthen one another, to pursue peace with everyone and the holiness without which no one will see the Lord, to watch that no one falls short of grace, and to guard against bitterness and against despising God's gifts like Esau.

Finally, the chapter reminds believers of their glorious privilege. They have not come to the terrifying mountain of Sinai, but to Mount Zion, the heavenly Jerusalem, to joyful angels, to the whole church, to God the Judge of all, and to Jesus, whose blood speaks a better word than Abel's. They must not refuse the God who speaks from heaven. They are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, so they can respond with gratitude and worship God with reverence and awe, for He is a consuming fire.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the author and perfecter of my faith. Help me to throw off every weight and the sin that so easily entangles me, and to run with endurance the race You have set before me, keeping my eyes on You. Thank You for enduring the cross for the joy set before You. When I grow weary, help me to consider You. Father, when I face hardship, help me to trust that You are training me in love to share in Your holiness, and let it produce the peaceful fruit of righteousness. Heal those who have been wounded by harsh and unloving treatment. Help me to pursue peace with everyone and holiness of life, and guard my heart from bitterness. Thank You that I have come to Mount Zion and to Jesus, whose blood speaks a better word. Thank You for a kingdom that cannot be shaken. Fill me with gratitude, and help me to worship You with reverence and awe. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
