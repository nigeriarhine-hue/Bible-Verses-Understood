# Hebrews 5
theme: A High Priest Who Learned Obedience Through Suffering
main: Every high priest is chosen from among people to represent them before God, offering gifts and sacrifices for sins, and because he shares their weakness, he can deal gently with the ignorant and wayward. No one takes this honor for himself; he must be called by God, as Aaron was. Likewise, Christ did not exalt Himself to become high priest; God appointed Him, saying, "You are my Son," and "You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek." During His earthly life, Jesus offered prayers and petitions with loud cries and tears to the One who could save Him from death, and He was heard because of His reverent submission. Although He was a Son, He learned obedience through what He suffered, and having been made perfect, He became the source of eternal salvation for all who obey Him. The author then pauses, rebuking his readers for being slow to learn: they ought to be teachers by now, but they still need milk rather than solid food, which belongs to the mature, whose senses are trained by practice to distinguish good from evil.

## Chapter Background and Story

In Hebrews 4:14–16, the author introduced Jesus as [[Hebrews 4:14|a great high priest]] who sympathizes with human weakness. Now he begins to explain in detail what makes Jesus a true high priest. This theme will dominate chapters 5–10.

In ancient Israel, the high priest held a special role. He was descended from Aaron, Moses' brother, and he represented the people before God. Once a year, on the Day of Atonement, he entered the Most Holy Place of the tabernacle or temple to offer blood for the sins of the people (Leviticus 16). He was the mediator between a holy God and a sinful people.

For Jewish readers, a question naturally arose: How could Jesus be a high priest? He was from the tribe of Judah, not Levi, and not descended from Aaron. The author answers by pointing to Psalm 110:4, where God declares the Messiah to be "a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek." Melchizedek was a mysterious king-priest who met Abraham in Genesis 14. The author will explain his significance more fully in chapter 7.

At the end of chapter 5, the author pauses. He wants to teach about Melchizedek, but he is concerned that his readers have become spiritually sluggish. This leads into the third warning passage of the letter, which continues into chapter 6.

### The Role of a High Priest (5:1–4)

The qualities of a high priest, especially gentleness toward the weak, are studied below. [[v3|Because of this, he must offer sacrifices for sins for the people, as well as for himself]]. Human high priests were sinners too. On the Day of Atonement, the high priest first offered a sacrifice for his own sins before offering one for the people (Leviticus 16:6, 11). Jesus, being sinless, did not need to offer for Himself (Hebrews 7:27).

[[v4|Nobody takes this honor on himself, but he is called by God, just like Aaron was]]. The high priesthood was not a position anyone could grab for themselves. When Korah and others rebelled against Aaron's priesthood, God judged them (Numbers 16). The high priest had to be appointed by God.

### Christ Appointed by God (5:5–6)

[[v5|So also Christ didn’t glorify himself to be made a high priest, but it was he who said to him, “You are my Son. Today I have become your father.”]] Jesus did not seize the role of high priest. He was appointed by the Father. The author quotes Psalm 2:7, already cited in Hebrews 1:5, linking Jesus' sonship with His priesthood.

[[v6|As he says also in another place, “You are a priest forever, after the order of Melchizedek.”]] Psalm 110:4 is central to Hebrews. The same psalm that invites the Messiah to sit at God's right hand (Psalm 110:1; Hebrews 1:13) also declares Him a priest forever. Jesus is both King and Priest. His priesthood is "forever," unlike the Levitical priests who died and were replaced. And it is "after the order of Melchizedek," a different kind of priesthood than Aaron's.

## Important Verses Explained

### Hebrews 5:1–2 — Dealing Gently with the Wayward

verse: Hebrews 5:1-2

#### Simple Explanation

The author describes what a high priest is. "For every high priest, being taken from among men, is appointed for men in things pertaining to God." A high priest is a human being, chosen from among the people, to act on their behalf before God. He is their representative.

"That he may offer both gifts and sacrifices for sins." His main task is to offer offerings to God, especially sacrifices that deal with sin.

Verse 2: "The high priest can deal gently with those who are ignorant and going astray." The phrase "deal gently" describes a balanced, moderate response, neither harsh nor indulgent. The high priest does not overreact in anger nor ignore sin. He responds with patience and understanding.

"Those who are ignorant and going astray" are people who sin through ignorance or weakness, wandering like lost sheep. In the Old Testament, sacrifices were provided especially for unintentional sins (Leviticus 4; Numbers 15:27–29).

"Because he himself is also surrounded with weakness." The high priest can be gentle because he understands weakness from personal experience. He too struggles.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This description of a high priest shows what kind of mediator humanity needs: someone who represents us, who deals with our sin, and who understands our weakness. Human high priests met this need partially. Jesus meets it perfectly.

Jesus shares human nature fully and understands weakness (Hebrews 2:17; 4:15). But unlike human priests, His gentleness does not come from personal sin; it comes from His perfect compassion and His experience of temptation and suffering. He is both completely holy and completely understanding.

The quality of gentleness toward the wayward is a model for all who care for others spiritually. Jesus did not crush bruised reeds or snuff out smoldering wicks (Matthew 12:20). He treated sinners with compassion, as with the woman caught in adultery (John 8:1–11) and with Peter after his denial (John 21:15–19).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers who feel they have wandered or failed can take comfort in knowing that their high priest deals gently with the wayward. They can come back to Him without fear of harsh rejection.

Those in positions of spiritual leadership, such as pastors, parents, mentors, and friends, can follow this example. Responding to others' failures with gentleness, patience, and understanding, while still taking sin seriously, reflects the heart of Christ.

Remembering one's own weakness also helps believers treat others with compassion. Those who know they too are "surrounded with weakness" are less likely to judge harshly.

### Hebrews 5:7–8 — Prayers with Loud Cries and Tears

verse: Hebrews 5:7-8

#### Simple Explanation

These verses give a vivid picture of Jesus' humanity. "He, in the days of his flesh." This refers to Jesus' earthly life.

"Having offered up prayers and petitions with strong crying and tears to him who was able to save him from death." Jesus prayed with intense emotion, with loud cries and tears. This most clearly recalls Gethsemane, where Jesus was deeply distressed and prayed that the cup might pass from Him (Mark 14:32–36), as discussed in the study of Mark 14:36. It may also recall His cry from the cross (Mark 15:34).

"And having been heard for his godly fear." God heard Jesus' prayer because of His reverent submission. But God did not spare Him from dying. How was He "heard"? Many understand it to mean that God answered by raising Him from the dead, saving Him out of death rather than from death. Others understand it to mean that God gave Jesus the strength to submit to His will (Luke 22:43). Either way, Jesus' prayer was not unanswered; it was answered in God's way.

Verse 8: "though he was a Son, yet learned obedience by the things which he suffered." Jesus was always obedient; He never sinned. But He "learned" obedience in the sense of experiencing what obedience costs in a world of suffering. As a human being, He grew in obedience through each trial, culminating in His obedience to death on the cross (Philippians 2:8).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal the depth of Jesus' identification with human suffering. He did not glide through life untouched by pain. He wept. He cried out. He felt fear and anguish. His sinlessness did not make Him less human; it made Him fully what humanity was meant to be, even in suffering.

This shows that strong emotion in prayer is not a lack of faith. Jesus Himself prayed with loud cries and tears. The Psalms are full of such prayers. Believers can bring their deepest anguish to God honestly.

"Though he was a Son" is significant. One might expect that the Son of God would be exempt from suffering. But He was not. His sonship did not shield Him from suffering; rather, His suffering was part of His perfect obedience as the Son. This gives dignity to believers' suffering. They too are God's children, and their suffering does not mean God has rejected them (Hebrews 12:5–11).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

When believers face anguish, fear, or grief, they can pray honestly, with tears if need be. Jesus did, and He understands.

Believers can also trust that God hears their prayers, even when the answer is not what they asked. Jesus was heard, and God's answer was resurrection. God's answers to believers' prayers may also come in ways they cannot yet see.

Finally, this passage encourages believers to see their own trials as opportunities to grow in obedience and trust. Like Jesus, they can learn through what they suffer, relying on the Father throughout.

### Hebrews 5:9 — The Source of Eternal Salvation

verse: Hebrews 5:9

#### Simple Explanation

"Having been made perfect." As explained at Hebrews 2:10, [[Hebrews 2:10|perfect]] does not mean that Jesus was ever morally flawed. It means He was made complete, fully qualified for His role as Savior and high priest. Through His suffering and obedience, culminating in His death and resurrection, He completed everything needed to save His people.

"He became to all of those who obey him the author of eternal salvation." "Author" here means "source" or "cause." Jesus is the one from whom eternal salvation flows. This salvation is "eternal," lasting forever, in contrast to the temporary effects of the old sacrifices.

"To all of those who obey him." Salvation is for those who respond to Jesus with obedience. In Hebrews, obedience is closely connected with faith (Hebrews 3:18–19). Those who truly trust Jesus will follow Him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the connection between Jesus' obedience and our salvation. Jesus learned obedience through suffering (v8), and He became the source of salvation for those who obey Him. His perfect obedience is the basis of our salvation; our obedience is the response to His saving work.

This does not mean salvation is earned by obedience. Hebrews teaches clearly that salvation comes through Christ's sacrifice (Hebrews 9:12, 26; 10:10, 14). But genuine faith always leads to obedience. Jesus said, [[John 14:15|If you love me, keep my commandments]]. Obedience is the evidence of faith, not its replacement.

The word "eternal" is important in Hebrews. Christ brings [[Hebrews 5:9|eternal salvation]], [[Hebrews 9:12|eternal redemption]], and [[Hebrews 9:15|the eternal inheritance]], through [[Hebrews 9:14|the eternal Spirit]]. What He provides is permanent and complete.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can rest in the security of eternal salvation. What Christ has accomplished cannot be undone. It is not a temporary fix but a permanent gift.

This verse also calls believers to respond to Christ with obedience. Faith is not merely agreement with ideas but a commitment to follow Jesus. Believers can ask how they are obeying Him in their daily lives.

Finally, this verse invites gratitude. Jesus suffered and obeyed perfectly so that He could become the source of salvation. Everything believers have flows from Him.

### Hebrews 5:12–14 — From Milk to Solid Food

verse: Hebrews 5:12-14

#### Simple Explanation

The author pauses to address his readers' spiritual condition. In verse 11 he says that he has much to say about Melchizedek, but it is hard to explain, because they have "become dull of hearing." The problem is not that the subject is too difficult but that the readers have become sluggish, slow to listen and learn.

Verse 12: "For although by this time you should be teachers, you again need to have someone teach you the rudiments of the first principles of the revelations of God." These believers had been Christians long enough that they should have been mature enough to teach others. Instead, they needed to relearn the basics.

"You have come to need milk, and not solid food." Milk is for babies; solid food is for those who are grown. Spiritually, they were still infants.

Verse 13: "For everyone who lives on milk is not experienced in the word of righteousness, for he is a baby." Those who remain on milk are unskilled in understanding and applying God's word.

Verse 14: "But solid food is for those who are full grown, who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern good and evil." Mature believers have trained their spiritual senses through constant practice. Like athletes, they have exercised their ability to distinguish what is good from what is evil.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage shows that spiritual growth is expected. God does not intend believers to remain spiritual infants. Paul similarly rebuked the Corinthians for being "babies in Christ" who still needed milk (1 Corinthians 3:1–2). Peter urged believers to crave pure spiritual milk so that they could grow (1 Peter 2:2). The goal is maturity.

Spiritual maturity is not merely about knowing more facts. It is about learning [[v14|to discern good and evil]], the practical wisdom to apply God's word to life. This discernment develops "by reason of use," through practice. Just as physical muscles grow stronger through exercise, spiritual discernment grows through regularly applying God's word in real decisions.

The readers' spiritual sluggishness was connected to their temptation to drift away. Believers who stop growing are more vulnerable to falling away. Growth is a safeguard.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can ask whether they are growing spiritually or have become stagnant. Have they moved beyond the basics of the faith into deeper understanding and practice?

Growth requires intentional effort: studying Scripture more deeply, learning from mature believers, applying God's word to everyday decisions, and serving others. Teaching others is also a sign and a means of maturity.

This passage is not meant to shame new believers. Everyone begins with milk, and that is good and right. The concern is for those who have been believers for a long time and have stopped growing. For them, the invitation is to move forward, to hunger for solid food, and to train their spiritual senses through practice.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Hebrews 5 explains what makes Jesus a true high priest. A high priest represents people before God, offers sacrifices for sins, and deals gently with the wayward because he understands weakness. And he must be appointed by God. Jesus fulfills every requirement perfectly. He did not exalt Himself but was appointed by the Father as Son and as a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek.

The chapter also offers a moving picture of Jesus' humanity. During His earthly life, He prayed with loud cries and tears, especially in Gethsemane. Although He was the Son, He learned obedience through suffering. Through that obedience, He was made perfect, fully qualified as the source of eternal salvation for all who obey Him. Believers can bring their own anguish to God honestly, trusting that He hears, as He heard Jesus.

Because Jesus deals gently with the wayward, believers who have wandered can return to Him without fear. And those who care for others can follow His example of gentle, patient understanding.

Finally, the chapter calls believers to spiritual maturity. Those who have been believers for some time should not remain spiritual infants, needing milk, but should grow into those who can handle solid food, with senses trained by practice to discern good and evil. Growth guards against drifting and prepares believers to help others grow as well.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for being my great high priest, appointed by the Father and faithful forever. Thank You that You deal gently with the weak and wayward. When I wander, draw me back with Your compassion. Thank You for praying with loud cries and tears in the days of Your flesh, and for learning obedience through what You suffered. When I am in anguish, help me to pray honestly, trusting that the Father hears. Thank You that You are the source of eternal salvation. Help me to respond to You with faithful obedience. Keep me from becoming dull of hearing or spiritually stagnant. Give me a hunger for solid food, and train my senses through practice to discern good from evil, so that I may grow to maturity and help others grow as well. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
