# Acts 18
theme: Faithful Work in Corinth and the Ministry of Priscilla and Aquila
main: In Corinth, Paul works as a tentmaker alongside Aquila and Priscilla, Jewish believers recently expelled from Rome, while preaching every Sabbath. When the synagogue rejects him, he turns to the Gentiles, and many Corinthians believe. The Lord tells Paul in a vision not to be afraid, because He has many people in that city, and Paul stays a year and a half. The Roman proconsul Gallio refuses to hear the case against him. Paul travels to Ephesus, Jerusalem, and Antioch, beginning his third journey, and Priscilla and Aquila gently teach the gifted preacher Apollos the way of God more accurately.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 18 describes Paul's ministry in Corinth, one of the most important cities of the Roman world. Corinth was the capital of the province of Achaia (southern Greece). It sat on a narrow strip of land between two seas, with ports on each side, making it a wealthy center of trade and travel. It was famous for its commerce, its athletic games, and its temples, including a temple to Aphrodite, the goddess of love. The city had such a reputation for immorality that "to act like a Corinthian" became a Greek expression for living immorally.

Paul founded a church in Corinth that would cause him both great joy and great concern. His letters to the Corinthians (1 and 2 Corinthians) address many problems in the church, including divisions, immorality, lawsuits, and confusion about worship and the resurrection.

The chapter also includes a valuable historical marker. Gallio, the brother of the philosopher Seneca, was proconsul of Achaia around A.D. 51–52, according to an inscription found at Delphi. This helps date Paul's time in Corinth.

### Paul Meets Aquila and Priscilla (18:1–4)

Paul left Athens and came to Corinth, about fifty miles away. There he met [[v2|a certain Jew named Aquila, a man of Pontus by race, who had recently come from Italy with his wife Priscilla, because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome]]. The Roman historian Suetonius records that Emperor Claudius expelled Jews from Rome because of disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus," which many scholars believe refers to conflicts over Christ in the Jewish community there. This expulsion took place around A.D. 49. Aquila and Priscilla may already have been believers when they arrived in Corinth.

Paul's work with them is studied below. Every Sabbath, Paul reasoned in the synagogue, persuading both Jews and Greeks.

### Paul Turns to the Gentiles (18:5–8)

When Silas and Timothy arrived from Macedonia, probably bringing a financial gift from the churches there (2 Corinthians 11:9; Philippians 4:15), Paul devoted himself fully to preaching, [[v5|testifying to the Jews that Jesus was the Christ]]. When the synagogue opposed him and spoke abusively, Paul [[v6|shook out his clothing]], a gesture of separation, and said, [[v6|Your blood be on your own heads! I am clean. From now on, I will go to the Gentiles!]] These words echo Ezekiel 33:1–9, where God told the prophet that a watchman who warned the people faithfully was not responsible for those who ignored the warning.

Paul moved next door, to the house of [[v7|a certain man named Justus, one who worshiped God]]. Remarkably, [[v8|Crispus, the ruler of the synagogue, believed in the Lord with all his house]]. Paul mentions baptizing Crispus personally (1 Corinthians 1:14). [[v8|Many of the Corinthians, when they heard, believed and were baptized]].

### The Lord's Encouragement (18:9–11)

Paul received a vision from the Lord, studied below. [[v11|He lived there a year and six months, teaching the word of God among them]]. This was one of Paul's longest stays in any city.

### Before Gallio (18:12–17)

When Gallio became proconsul, some Jewish leaders brought Paul before his judgment seat, accusing him: [[v13|This man persuades men to worship God contrary to the law]]. Before Paul could even defend himself, Gallio dismissed the case. He saw it as a dispute about [[v15|questions about words and names and your own law]], not a crime under Roman law. [[v16|So he drove them from the judgment seat]].

Gallio's decision was significant. By treating the Christian movement as a matter within Judaism, which was a legally recognized religion in the Roman Empire, he gave the church a measure of legal protection for years to come.

Then the crowd seized Sosthenes, the new synagogue ruler, and beat him in front of the court. [[v17|Gallio didn’t care about any of these things]]. Interestingly, a Sosthenes is mentioned as a "brother" with Paul when he wrote 1 Corinthians (1 Corinthians 1:1). If this is the same man, then another synagogue ruler came to faith.

### Paul's Return and New Journey (18:18–23)

After staying many more days, Paul sailed for Syria with Priscilla and Aquila. At Cenchreae, the eastern port of Corinth, [[v18|He shaved his head in Cenchreae, for he had a vow]]. This was probably a Nazirite-type vow, a Jewish practice of special dedication to God (Numbers 6:1–21). Paul, though free from the Law as a requirement for salvation, still sometimes practiced Jewish customs as expressions of devotion.

In Ephesus, Paul reasoned in the synagogue, and the people asked him to stay longer. He declined, with words studied below. He left Priscilla and Aquila in Ephesus and sailed to Caesarea, greeted the church (probably in Jerusalem), and went down to Antioch. This ended his second journey. After some time, he set out again, traveling through Galatia and Phrygia, [[v23|establishing all the disciples]]. This marks the beginning of his third journey.

### Apollos (18:24–28)

Meanwhile, a Jew named Apollos came to Ephesus. He was from Alexandria in Egypt, a great center of learning with a large Jewish community. He was [[v24|an eloquent man]] and [[v24|mighty in the Scriptures]]. He had been instructed in the way of the Lord and [[v25|being fervent in spirit, he spoke and taught accurately the things concerning Jesus, although he knew only the baptism of John]]. Apollos knew about Jesus, perhaps from disciples of John the Baptist, but his understanding was incomplete. He may not have known about the full significance of Jesus' death and resurrection, Christian baptism, or the gift of the Holy Spirit. How Priscilla and Aquila helped him is studied below.

Apollos then went to Achaia, with a letter of recommendation from the believers in Ephesus. There [[v27|he greatly helped those who had believed through grace]], [[v28|for he powerfully refuted the Jews, publicly showing by the Scriptures that Jesus was the Christ]]. Apollos became an important teacher in Corinth. Paul later wrote, [[1 Corinthians 3:6|I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase]].

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 18:3 — By Trade They Were Tentmakers

verse: Acts 18:3

#### Simple Explanation

When Paul arrived in Corinth, he was alone. Silas and Timothy were still in Macedonia. He needed a place to stay and a way to support himself. He met Aquila and Priscilla, a Jewish couple who had recently arrived from Rome.

[[v3|and because he practiced the same trade, he lived with them and worked, for by trade they were tent makers]]. Paul shared their trade. The word translated "tent makers" may refer to making tents from goat-hair cloth or to working with leather more generally. Jewish rabbis were expected to learn a trade so that they would not need to profit from teaching the Law. Paul had learned this trade, perhaps in his hometown of Tarsus, which was known for a goat-hair cloth called cilicium.

Paul worked with his hands to support himself while he preached. In his letters, he often mentions this. He told the Thessalonians, [[1 Thessalonians 2:9|For you remember, brothers, our labor and travail; for working night and day, that we might not burden any of you, we preached to you the Good News of God]]. In Corinth, he refused to accept financial support from the church, so that no one could accuse him of preaching for money (1 Corinthians 9:12–18; 2 Corinthians 11:7–9).

Paul did not consider manual labor beneath him or a distraction from ministry. His workshop became a place of relationships. Working side by side with Aquila and Priscilla, he formed a lifelong partnership. They became some of his most trusted co-workers. Paul later wrote that they [[Romans 16:4|risked their own necks]] for his life.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the dignity of ordinary work. In the Greek world, many educated people looked down on manual labor. Paul, a highly educated former Pharisee, worked with his hands without shame. Scripture teaches that work is part of God's good design for humanity (Genesis 2:15). All honest work can be done for God's glory.

It also shows the integration of work and ministry. Paul did not see his tentmaking as separate from his calling. His work supported his ministry, gave him credibility, and created relationships. Believers who work in ordinary jobs are not second-class servants of God. Their workplaces can be places of witness and service.

Finally, the verse shows the value of partnership. Aquila and Priscilla were not apostles, but they became key partners in Paul's ministry. They hosted churches in their home (1 Corinthians 16:19; Romans 16:5) and taught others. God uses partnerships of believers with different gifts and roles.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to see their work as meaningful. Whether believers work in offices, factories, fields, homes, or shops, their work can honor God. Paul wrote, [[Colossians 3:23|And whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord, and not for men]].

It also encourages believers to see their workplaces as places of ministry. Paul formed deep relationships through his work. Believers can build friendships, show integrity, and share their faith with coworkers.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to support ministry in different ways. Some are called to full-time ministry supported by others. Others, like Paul in Corinth, may support themselves while serving. Both are valuable. Believers can also follow Aquila and Priscilla's example of offering their homes and skills to support God's work.

### Acts 18:9–10 — [[Acts 18:9|Don't Be Afraid, but Speak]]

verse: Acts 18:9-10

#### Simple Explanation

Paul's ministry in Corinth had faced opposition. The synagogue leaders had opposed him and spoken abusively, and he had moved his teaching next door. Paul had faced violent opposition in nearly every city on his journey: he had been beaten and imprisoned in Philippi, driven out of Thessalonica and Berea, and mocked in Athens. He later wrote that he came to Corinth [[1 Corinthians 2:3|in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling]]. Even great apostles feel fear.

Then [[v9|The Lord said to Paul in the night by a vision, “Don’t be afraid, but speak and don’t be silent;]]. The Lord knew Paul's fear and spoke directly to it. He gave Paul three commands: do not be afraid, keep speaking, and do not be silent. The repetition emphasizes that Paul should continue his ministry boldly.

The Lord gave three reasons. First, [[v10|for I am with you]]. The presence of Jesus was Paul's greatest security. This echoes Jesus' promise at the Great Commission: [[Matthew 28:20|I am with you always]].

Second, [[v10|and no one will attack you to harm you]]. God promised protection during this time in Corinth. Paul would face opposition, but he would not be harmed.

Third, [[v10|for I have many people in this city]]. God had people in Corinth who would believe. They had not yet heard or responded, but God already knew them and considered them His. Paul's preaching would be the means by which they would come to faith.

Encouraged by this vision, Paul stayed in Corinth for a year and a half.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that God cares for His servants' fears. Paul was a bold apostle, yet he needed encouragement. God did not rebuke him for being afraid; He reassured him. Throughout Scripture, God often says "Do not be afraid" to His people, from Abraham to Joshua to Mary.

They also show the connection between God's sovereignty and human responsibility. God said, "I have many people in this city." He already knew who would believe. Yet Paul still needed to speak. God's sovereign plan does not make evangelism unnecessary; it makes evangelism hopeful. Because God has people everywhere, believers can share the gospel with confidence that it will bear fruit.

Finally, these verses show the importance of Christ's presence. The greatest encouragement was not a promise of success but the promise "I am with you." Christ's presence gives courage in every situation.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers who feel afraid to share their faith. Fear is a common experience, even for mature believers. God's word to Paul applies to them: do not be afraid, keep speaking, for I am with you.

They also give believers hope for their communities. God has people in every city, neighborhood, and workplace, people He is drawing to Himself. Believers can share the gospel with hope, knowing that God is at work.

Finally, these verses remind believers to seek encouragement from the Lord. When believers feel discouraged or afraid, they can turn to God in prayer and Scripture, listening for His reassurance. He knows their fears and meets them with His presence.

### Acts 18:21 — [[Acts 18:21|If God Wills]]

verse: Acts 18:21

#### Simple Explanation

After his long stay in Corinth, Paul sailed to Ephesus with Priscilla and Aquila. Ephesus was a major city in the province of Asia, the same region where the Holy Spirit had earlier prevented Paul from preaching (16:6). Now Paul went to the synagogue in Ephesus and reasoned with the Jews.

The response was positive: [[v20|When they asked him to stay with them a longer time, he declined]]. This was unusual. In many cities, the synagogue had rejected Paul. Here they wanted him to stay. Yet Paul felt he needed to move on.

[[v21|but taking his leave of them, he said, “I must by all means keep this coming feast in Jerusalem, but I will return again to you if God wills.”]] Some manuscripts mention the feast in Jerusalem; others do not. Either way, Paul felt he needed to go. But he promised to return [[v21|if God wills]].

Paul made plans, but he held them humbly, recognizing that God's will was greater than his own. He did return to Ephesus on his next journey and stayed for nearly three years (19:1–10; 20:31). God did will it.

Paul often used this kind of language. He wrote to the Corinthians, [[1 Corinthians 4:19|But I will come to you shortly, if the Lord is willing]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the right attitude toward planning. Believers can and should make plans, but they hold them in submission to God's will. James writes, [[James 4:15|For you ought to say, “If the Lord wills, we will both live, and do this or that.”]] This is not a magic formula but a heartfelt recognition that God is sovereign over the future.

It also shows that God's timing is often different from human timing. Earlier, the Spirit prevented Paul from going to Asia. Now an open door was there, but Paul could not stay. Later, God brought him back for an extended ministry. God's timing was perfect.

Finally, the verse shows the humility of a great leader. Paul did not presume that his plans would succeed. He trusted God to direct his steps.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to make plans with humility. Believers can set goals and make decisions while acknowledging that God's will may be different. This attitude brings peace, since believers do not have to control the future.

It also helps believers handle disappointment. When plans fall through, believers can trust that God's will is good, even when they do not understand it.

Finally, the verse invites believers to pray about their plans. Before making major decisions, believers can seek God's guidance and commit their plans to Him. Proverbs says, [[Proverbs 16:3|Commit your deeds to the LORD, and your plans shall succeed]].

### Acts 18:26 — Explaining the Way of God More Accurately

verse: Acts 18:26

#### Simple Explanation

Apollos was a gifted Jewish teacher from Alexandria who came to Ephesus. He was eloquent, deeply knowledgeable in the Scriptures, and passionate. He taught accurately about Jesus, but his understanding was incomplete; he knew only the baptism of John. He may not have fully understood Jesus' death and resurrection, Christian baptism, or the coming of the Holy Spirit.

[[v26|He began to speak boldly in the synagogue]]. Apollos was courageous and enthusiastic. He preached what he knew with conviction.

[[v26|But when Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him aside, and explained to him the way of God more accurately]]. Priscilla and Aquila, the tentmakers who had worked with Paul in Corinth, heard Apollos preach. They recognized both his gifts and the gaps in his understanding.

Notice how they responded. They did not criticize him publicly or interrupt his preaching. They "took him aside," speaking with him privately, possibly inviting him into their home. They "explained to him the way of God more accurately." They filled in what he lacked, with gentleness and respect.

Notice also that Priscilla is named first here, as she often is in the New Testament. This may suggest that she was especially prominent in this teaching ministry. Both husband and wife taught Apollos together.

Apollos received their instruction humbly. A gifted, eloquent preacher was willing to learn from two tentmakers. Afterward, he became an even more effective minister, helping the believers in Achaia greatly.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the importance of sound teaching. Apollos was gifted and sincere, but his understanding needed to be completed. Even passionate believers need to grow in their knowledge of God's truth. Paul writes that God gives teachers to the church [[Ephesians 4:13|until we all attain to the unity of the faith, and of the knowledge of the Son of God]].

It also shows a gracious way of correcting others. Priscilla and Aquila did not shame Apollos. They spoke privately, explained carefully, and built him up. Paul later wrote that believers should restore those who need correction [[Galatians 6:1|in a spirit of gentleness]].

Finally, the verse shows that God uses ordinary believers, both men and women, to teach and strengthen others. Priscilla and Aquila were not apostles or official leaders in the usual sense, but they played a key role in the growth of the early church. Their home became a place where believers were taught and encouraged.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to help others grow in their understanding of God's word. When believers see someone who sincerely loves God but has gaps in their understanding, they can come alongside them privately and gently, sharing what they know.

It also encourages believers to remain teachable. Apollos was gifted and knowledgeable, yet he was willing to learn. No believer ever outgrows the need to learn. Believers can welcome correction and instruction from others, even those who seem less impressive.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to use their homes for ministry. Priscilla and Aquila's home became a place of teaching and hospitality. Believers can open their homes for Bible study, mentoring, and encouragement.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 18 shows that ordinary work matters to God. Paul worked as a tentmaker alongside Aquila and Priscilla while preaching the gospel. Believers today can see their work as meaningful, doing it for God's glory and using their workplaces as places to build relationships and share their faith.

The chapter also offers encouragement for those who are afraid. Paul came to Corinth in weakness and fear, and the Lord told him not to be afraid but to keep speaking, because He was with him and had many people in that city. Believers can take courage from Christ's presence and share their faith with hope, knowing that God is at work in their communities.

Acts 18 teaches humility in planning. Paul made plans but held them loosely, saying, "If God wills." Believers can make plans while trusting God's sovereignty and accepting His timing.

Finally, the chapter shows the value of gentle, private instruction. Priscilla and Aquila took Apollos aside and explained the way of God more accurately, and he became an even more effective minister. Believers can help one another grow, offering correction with gentleness and receiving it with humility, and they can use their homes as places of teaching and fellowship.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for the dignity of work. Help me to do my work wholeheartedly for You and to use my workplace as a place to love and serve others. When I am afraid, speak to me as You spoke to Paul: [[v9|Don’t be afraid, but speak and don’t be silent|-]]; [[v10|for I am with you|-]]. Thank You that You have people in my city whom You are drawing to Yourself; make me faithful to share Your word with them. Teach me to make plans humbly, always saying, "If God wills." Give me the wisdom and gentleness of Priscilla and Aquila to help others grow in their understanding of You, and give me a teachable heart like Apollos, always ready to learn. Use my home and my life for Your kingdom. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
