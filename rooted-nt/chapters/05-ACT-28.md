# Acts 28
theme: Rome at Last: Boldly and Without Hindrance
main: Shipwrecked on Malta, Paul and his companions are welcomed with unusual kindness. Paul survives a viper's bite, heals the father of the island's chief official and many others, and the islanders send them on with supplies. Arriving in Italy, Paul is met by believers from Rome, thanks God, and takes courage. In Rome, under house arrest, he meets with Jewish leaders and spends a whole day explaining Jesus from the Law and the Prophets. Some believe and some do not. The book closes with Paul living two years in his rented house, welcoming all who come, and proclaiming the kingdom of God and the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness, without hindrance.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 28 completes Luke's two-volume work. The Gospel of Luke began in Jerusalem with the announcement of Jesus' birth; Acts ends in Rome, the capital of the empire, with the gospel being freely proclaimed. Jesus had said that His witnesses would go "to the uttermost parts of the earth" (1:8). Rome was not the end of the earth, but it was the center of the known world, and from there the gospel would spread in every direction.

The ending of Acts surprises many readers. We are not told the outcome of Paul's trial before Caesar or how Paul died. Instead, Luke ends with Paul preaching freely. This open ending suggests that the story of Acts is not finished. The work of the Spirit through the church continues, and every generation of believers takes up the task.

### Kindness on Malta (28:1–10)

After the shipwreck, the survivors learned that they were on Malta, an island south of Sicily. Malta lies about 600 miles from Crete, the distance the ship had been driven by the storm. [[v2|The natives showed us uncommon kindness; for they kindled a fire and received us all, because of the present rain and because of the cold]]. The word Luke uses for the islanders is "barbarians," which in Greek simply meant people who did not speak Greek. They welcomed 276 cold, wet, exhausted strangers with remarkable hospitality.

Paul, the great apostle, did not stand idle. He gathered sticks for the fire. When he laid them on the fire, [[v3|a viper came out because of the heat and fastened on his hand]]. The islanders assumed Paul must be a murderer. He had escaped the sea, but [[v4|Justice has not allowed to live]]. "Justice" (Dike) was a Greek goddess of justice and vengeance. The islanders believed that the gods would not let a guilty man escape. But Paul [[v5|shook off the creature into the fire, and wasn’t harmed]]. Jesus had promised His disciples that they would have authority over serpents (Luke 10:19). The islanders waited for him to swell up or drop dead. When nothing happened, [[v6|they changed their minds and said that he was a god]]. Their opinions swung from one extreme to the other, much as the people of Lystra had once tried to worship Paul (Acts 14:11–18).

Nearby was the estate of [[v7|the chief man of the island, named Publius]], who welcomed them and entertained them for three days. An inscription found on Malta uses the same title, "chief man," for the island's Roman official, confirming Luke's accuracy. Publius's father was sick with fever and dysentery. [[v8|Paul entered in to him, prayed, and laying his hands on him, healed him]]. Notice that Paul prayed first; he made clear that healing came from God. Then the rest of the sick people on the island came and were healed. The islanders honored Paul and his companions and supplied them with what they needed for the journey.

Luke does not record Paul preaching on Malta, but it is likely that the gospel was shared there during the three months they stayed. Christian tradition holds that the church on Malta began with Paul's visit.

### The Journey to Rome (28:11–16)

After three months, at the beginning of spring sailing season, they set sail on another Alexandrian ship that had wintered on the island. Its figurehead was [[v11|The Twin Brothers]], Castor and Pollux, sons of Zeus who were believed to protect sailors. Luke may include this detail to contrast these supposed protectors with the true God who had protected them all.

They sailed to Syracuse in Sicily, then to Rhegium at the toe of Italy, and then to Puteoli, a major port near Naples. There they found believers, who urged them to stay a week. The gospel had already reached Italy before Paul.

[[v14|So we came to Rome]]. Luke states it simply. After years of longing, imprisonment, trials, a storm, and a shipwreck, Paul had arrived. The welcome he received is studied below.

In Rome, Paul [[v16|was allowed to stay by himself with the soldier who guarded him]]. He lived under house arrest, probably chained to a soldier at all times. The guards would have rotated, so over time many soldiers heard the gospel. Paul later wrote that his imprisonment had become known throughout the whole palace guard (Philippians 1:13).

### Paul and the Jewish Leaders in Rome (28:17–29)

Three days after arriving, Paul called together the leaders of the Jewish community in Rome. As always, he wanted to share the gospel with his own people first. He explained that he had done nothing against the Jewish people or their customs, but had been handed over to the Romans, who wanted to release him. When the Jewish leaders objected, he had been forced to appeal to Caesar, [[v19|not that I had anything about which to accuse my nation]]. Paul had no desire to bring charges against his people. [[v20|For because of the hope of Israel I am bound with this chain]]. The hope of Israel, the Messiah and the resurrection, was the reason for his chains.

The Jewish leaders said they had received no letters from Judea about Paul and no one had spoken against him. But they wanted to hear his views, [[v22|For, as concerning this sect, it is known to us that everywhere it is spoken against]]. Paul's day of teaching is studied below.

When they disagreed among themselves and began to leave, Paul quoted Isaiah 6:9–10, the same passage Jesus had quoted about those who hear but do not understand (Matthew 13:14–15). [[v27|For this people’s heart has grown callous. Their ears are dull of hearing. Their eyes they have closed]]. Isaiah's words, spoken centuries earlier, described the hardening of heart that keeps people from receiving God's message. Paul concluded, [[v28|Be it known therefore to you that the salvation of God is sent to the nations, and they will listen]].

This statement does not mean that God had rejected the Jewish people. Some of the Jewish leaders in Rome believed (28:24). Paul himself wrote that God has not rejected His people (Romans 11:1–2), and that he longed for their salvation (Romans 10:1). Throughout Acts, many thousands of Jews believed. But Paul recognized that as many rejected the message, the gospel would go forth to the Gentiles, who would receive it. (Verse 29 appears in some later manuscripts but not the earliest.)

### Two Years in Rome (28:30–31)

The final verses of Acts are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 28:2 — Uncommon Kindness

verse: Acts 28:2

#### Simple Explanation

The ship carrying Paul had broken apart on the coast of an unknown island. All 276 people on board, sailors, soldiers, prisoners, and passengers, had swum or floated to shore on pieces of wreckage. They were soaked, cold, exhausted, and probably injured. It was raining. They had lost everything except their lives.

Then they learned that the island was called Malta. The people living there were not Greeks or Romans. Luke calls them "natives," people who spoke their own language.

[[v2|The natives showed us uncommon kindness]]. The islanders could have been suspicious or hostile toward a large group of strangers, including prisoners and soldiers. Instead, they welcomed them warmly. The word translated "uncommon" means unusual or extraordinary. Their kindness went beyond what anyone would expect.

[[v2|for they kindled a fire and received us all, because of the present rain and because of the cold]]. Their kindness was practical. They built a fire to warm everyone. They "received us all," welcoming everyone without discrimination. They cared for physical needs in a time of crisis.

Luke, a careful historian, made a point of recording this kindness. These islanders were not believers, yet they showed compassion to strangers in need. Their kindness opened the door for Paul's ministry of healing on the island.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that kindness can be found among people of all backgrounds. The islanders were not part of God's covenant people, yet they reflected something of God's character. All people are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27), and even those who do not know Him can show kindness and mercy. Jesus' parable of the Good Samaritan shows that compassion can come from unexpected people (Luke 10:30–37).

It also shows God's care for His people through others. God had promised that everyone on the ship would survive. Part of how He fulfilled that promise was through the kindness of the islanders. God often provides for His people through the generosity of others.

Finally, the verse shows the value of hospitality. Scripture repeatedly calls God's people to welcome strangers. Hebrews says, [[Hebrews 13:2|Don’t forget to show hospitality to strangers, for in doing so, some have entertained angels without knowing it]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to show kindness to strangers, especially those in need. Refugees, immigrants, travelers, and people affected by disasters often arrive with nothing. Believers can follow the islanders' example of practical, generous welcome.

It also encourages believers to recognize and honor kindness wherever it appears, including among people who do not share their faith. Believers can be grateful for the goodness of others and see it as evidence of God's common grace.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that small acts of kindness matter. Building a fire on a rainy day may seem simple, but it meant everything to people who had just escaped a shipwreck. Believers can look for practical ways to meet the needs of those around them.

### Acts 28:15 — He Thanked God and Took Courage

verse: Acts 28:15

#### Simple Explanation

After three months on Malta, Paul and his companions sailed to Italy. They landed at Puteoli and stayed a week with believers there. Then they traveled by land toward Rome along the Appian Way, the great Roman road.

[[v15|From there the brothers, when they heard of us, came to meet us as far as The Market of Appius and The Three Taverns]]. Word had reached the believers in Rome that Paul was coming. Some walked out to meet him. The Market of Appius was about forty-three miles from Rome; the Three Taverns was about thirty-three miles away. These believers traveled a long distance on foot to welcome Paul and escort him into the city, the way a city might go out to welcome an honored guest.

Paul had written his letter to the Romans about three years earlier, expressing his longing to visit them (Romans 1:10–12; 15:23–24). He had never met most of them. Now they came out to meet him.

[[v15|When Paul saw them, he thanked God and took courage]]. Paul was arriving in Rome as a prisoner, facing trial before Caesar. He had been through storms and shipwreck. He may have wondered how he would be received. When he saw these believers who had come so far to welcome him, he thanked God and was encouraged.

Even the great apostle needed encouragement. The love of fellow believers renewed his strength.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the power of Christian fellowship. Paul was strengthened by the presence and love of other believers. Paul wrote to the Romans, [[Romans 1:12|that is, that I with you may be encouraged in you, each of us by the other’s faith, both yours and mine]]. Believers need one another.

It also shows the importance of gratitude. Paul's first response was to thank God. He recognized that these believers were God's gift to him. Gratitude often leads to renewed courage.

Finally, the verse shows that even strong believers need encouragement. Paul had faced countless trials with faith and courage. Yet he still needed the support of others. No believer is meant to walk alone.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to support those who are facing difficulties. A visit, a phone call, a meal, or simply showing up can bring great encouragement. The Roman believers walked thirty or forty miles to meet Paul. Believers can make the effort to be present for others.

It also encourages believers to receive encouragement with gratitude. When others show love and support, believers can thank God for them and let that love renew their courage.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that everyone needs encouragement, including leaders. Pastors, missionaries, and other leaders may seem strong, but they also need the support and love of the church. Believers can look for ways to encourage those who serve them.

### Acts 28:23–24 — Persuading Them Concerning Jesus from Morning until Evening

verse: Acts 28:23-24

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had met with the leaders of the Jewish community in Rome and explained why he had been arrested. They wanted to hear more about his message, so they set a day to meet with him.

[[v23|When they had appointed him a day, many people came to him at his lodging]]. Many Jews came to the house where Paul was staying under guard. Even as a prisoner, Paul had a ministry. His home became a place of teaching.

[[v23|He explained to them, testifying about God’s Kingdom, and persuading them concerning Jesus, both from the law of Moses and from the prophets, from morning until evening]]. Paul spent the whole day teaching. He explained God's kingdom, the reign of God that Jesus had announced and brought. He tried to persuade them about Jesus, using the Scriptures they knew: the Law of Moses and the Prophets. Paul showed how the Old Testament pointed to Jesus as the Messiah.

This was the same approach Jesus used with the disciples on the road to Emmaus: [[Luke 24:27|Beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, he explained to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself]].

[[v24|Some believed the things which were spoken, and some disbelieved]]. The response was divided. Some were persuaded and believed in Jesus. Others did not. This mixed response had been typical throughout Paul's ministry.

Paul's patience is striking. He spent a whole day explaining and persuading. He did not give up quickly or present a short message. He took time to answer questions and work through the Scriptures with his listeners.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the gospel is rooted in the Old Testament. Paul did not present Jesus as a new idea but as the fulfillment of everything the Law and the Prophets had promised. Jesus Himself said that the Scriptures testify about Him (John 5:39).

They also show the importance of patient, thoughtful evangelism. Paul reasoned and persuaded from morning until evening. He engaged people's minds as well as their hearts. Peter urges believers to always be ready [[1 Peter 3:15|to give an answer to everyone who asks you a reason concerning the hope that is in you, with humility and fear]].

Finally, these verses show that the same message receives different responses. Paul preached faithfully, but not everyone believed. The outcome depends on God's work in hearts and on each person's response. Faithful witnesses are responsible for faithfulness, not for results.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to know the Scriptures well enough to explain how they point to Jesus. Believers can study the Old Testament as well as the New, seeing how the whole Bible tells one story centered on Christ.

They also encourage believers to be patient when sharing their faith. Some people need time to consider the gospel. Believers can take time to listen, answer questions, and explain the Scriptures, rather than expecting quick decisions.

Finally, these verses comfort believers when some reject the message. Not everyone will believe. Believers can share faithfully, trust God with the results, and rejoice over those who do believe.

### Acts 28:30–31 — With All Boldness, Without Hindrance

verse: Acts 28:30-31

#### Simple Explanation

These are the final verses of the book of Acts. Paul had arrived in Rome as a prisoner. He was waiting for his trial before Caesar. Luke tells us what Paul did during this time.

[[v30|Paul stayed two whole years in his own rented house]]. Paul lived in a rented house, guarded by a soldier, for two years. He was not free to travel, but he was able to live in his own lodgings.

[[v30|and received all who were coming to him]]. Paul welcomed everyone who came to visit. Jews and Gentiles, believers and seekers, friends and strangers came to his house. His home became a center of ministry.

[[v31|preaching God’s Kingdom and teaching the things concerning the Lord Jesus Christ]]. Paul's message remained the same as it had been from the beginning: the kingdom of God and the Lord Jesus Christ. Jesus had begun His ministry preaching the kingdom of God (Luke 4:43), and Acts began with Jesus teaching about the kingdom (Acts 1:3). Now Acts ends with Paul doing the same.

[[v31|with all boldness, without hindrance]]. The final words of Acts are significant. Paul preached "with all boldness," with courage and freedom of speech. And he preached "without hindrance." Even though Paul was in chains, the gospel was not chained. The Roman authorities did not stop him from teaching.

During this time, Paul probably wrote several letters, including Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, and Philemon, often called the Prison Letters. Through these letters, his ministry reached far beyond his rented house.

Luke does not tell us what happened next. Early Christian tradition says that Paul was released, traveled further, was arrested again, and was executed in Rome under Nero around A.D. 64–67. But Luke ends with the gospel going forward, unhindered.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the gospel cannot be stopped. Paul was imprisoned, yet he preached freely. Paul wrote from prison, [[2 Timothy 2:9|in which I suffer hardship to the point of chains as a criminal. But God’s word isn’t chained]]. No human power can bind the word of God.

They also show the faithfulness of God's promises. Jesus had told Paul that he would testify in Rome (23:11). Despite plots, trials, storms, shipwreck, and snakebite, Paul arrived in Rome and testified there. God's promises are sure.

Finally, the open ending of Acts invites every reader into the story. The book does not end with a final conclusion but with the gospel going forward. The story of the church continues through every generation of believers who carry the message of the kingdom and of Jesus to the world.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers who face limitations. Paul could not travel, yet he used his situation to welcome visitors and write letters. Believers who are limited by illness, age, circumstances, or responsibilities can still serve God where they are, welcoming others and sharing their faith.

They also encourage believers to keep the gospel at the center. Paul's message never changed: the kingdom of God and the Lord Jesus Christ. Believers can keep their focus on Jesus and His kingdom in all their witness.

Finally, these verses invite believers to see themselves as part of the continuing story of Acts. The Holy Spirit who empowered Peter and Paul still empowers the church today. Believers are called to carry the gospel boldly, trusting that God's word cannot be hindered.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 28 shows that kindness and hospitality reflect God's heart. The people of Malta welcomed shipwrecked strangers with uncommon kindness. Believers can follow their example, offering practical welcome and care to strangers, refugees, and anyone in need, and can recognize and honor kindness wherever it appears.

The chapter also shows the importance of Christian fellowship. When believers from Rome walked out to meet Paul, he thanked God and took courage. Believers can encourage one another, especially those facing hardship, and can receive the encouragement of others with gratitude.

Acts 28 models patient, Scripture-centered witness. Paul spent an entire day explaining Jesus from the Law and the Prophets. Some believed and some did not. Believers can grow in their knowledge of how the whole Bible points to Jesus, share their faith patiently, and trust God with the results.

Finally, the chapter ends with Paul preaching the kingdom of God and the Lord Jesus Christ boldly and without hindrance, even in chains. The book of Acts is open-ended because the story is still being written. Every believer is invited to take up the mission, empowered by the same Holy Spirit, carrying the good news of Jesus to the ends of the earth with confidence that God's word cannot be chained.

Make me a person of uncommon kindness, welcoming strangers and caring for those in need. Thank You for the believers who encourage me; help me to encourage others, especially those who are weary or facing hardship. Give me a deep knowledge of Your word, so that I can explain how the whole Bible points to Jesus, and give me patience when I share my faith. When I face limitations, help me to serve You where I am.
