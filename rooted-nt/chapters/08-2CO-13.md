# 2 Corinthians 13
theme: Examine Yourselves and Live in Peace
main: As Paul prepares for his third visit, he warns that he will deal firmly with unrepentant sin, because Christ, who was crucified in weakness but lives by the power of God, is powerful among them. He calls the Corinthians to examine themselves to see whether they are in the faith, and he prays that they will do what is right, even if he seems to fail. Paul closes with a call to rejoice, mature, be comforted, agree, and live in peace, and with a blessing that names the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, the love of God, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit.

## Chapter Background and Story

The final chapter of 2 Corinthians brings the letter to a close with a mixture of firm warning and warm blessing. Paul is about to visit Corinth for the third time. His first visit founded the church (Acts 18). His second was the "painful visit" mentioned in 2 Corinthians 2:1. Now he is coming again, and he wants the church to be ready.

Throughout chapters 10–12, Paul has defended his apostleship against critics who claimed he was weak. In this chapter he answers one final challenge. The Corinthians wanted "proof" that Christ was speaking through him (v3). Paul says they will have it, but he hopes he will not need to show it through discipline. He would much rather see them repent and grow strong.

The chapter ends with one of the most beloved blessings in Christian worship, a closing benediction that names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together. After all the conflict and hard words of this letter, Paul's last word is grace, love, and fellowship.

### Firm Warning (13:1–4)

[[v1|This is the third time I am coming to you. “At the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established.”]] Paul quotes Deuteronomy 19:15, the principle that any accusation must be confirmed by two or three witnesses. He may mean that when he arrives, any charges against members of the church will be handled fairly, with proper evidence, not on the basis of rumor. He may also be suggesting that his three visits serve as three witnesses or warnings. Either way, he signals that matters will be dealt with justly.

[[v2|I have warned previously, and I warn again, as when I was present the second time, so now, being absent, I write to those who have sinned before now and to all the rest that if I come again, I will not spare]]. Paul has been patient, but patience has a purpose. If those who persist in sin do not repent, he will act.

[[v3|seeing that you seek a proof of Christ who speaks in me who is not weak, but is powerful in you]]. The Corinthians wanted evidence of Christ's power in Paul. Paul reminds them that Christ is not weak among them. The relationship between weakness and power in Christ's life and Paul's is studied below.

### Examine Yourselves (13:5–10)

Paul turns the Corinthians' demand back on them. They have been testing him; now they need to test themselves. This call is studied below.

[[v6|But I hope that you will know that we aren’t disqualified]]. Paul is confident that if they examine themselves honestly, they will recognize that his ministry is genuine, since their very faith came through it.

[[v7|Now I pray to God that you do no evil; not that we may appear approved, but that you may do that which is honorable, though we may seem to have failed]]. This is a remarkable prayer. Paul cares more about the Corinthians' doing right than about his own reputation. If they repent and do what is good, he will not need to show his authority, and he may seem "weak" to his critics. He is willing to look like a failure if it means they are faithful.

[[v8|For we can do nothing against the truth, but for the truth]]. Paul's authority is bound to the truth. He cannot use it for any purpose other than the gospel and the good of the church.

[[v9|For we rejoice when we are weak and you are strong. We also pray for this: your becoming perfect]]. The word translated "perfect" means being restored, mended, or made complete. It was used of setting broken bones or mending nets. Paul prays for the church's restoration.

[[v10|For this cause I write these things while absent, that I may not deal sharply when present, according to the authority which the Lord gave me for building up and not for tearing down]]. Paul wrote this letter so that, when he arrives, he can come in gentleness rather than severity. He repeats the principle from 2 Corinthians 10:8: his authority exists for building up.

### Final Greetings and Blessing (13:11–14)

Paul's closing words, studied below, gather up the whole letter's hopes for the church. [[v12|Greet one another with a holy kiss]]. In that culture, a kiss on the cheek was a common greeting among family and friends. Paul calls it "holy" because it expressed the family love of believers, who had become brothers and sisters in Christ. [[v13|All the saints greet you]]. The believers in Macedonia, where Paul was writing, send their greetings too. The Corinthian church was part of a much larger family. The closing blessing is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### 2 Corinthians 13:4 — Crucified in Weakness, Living by God's Power

verse: 2 Corinthians 13:4

#### Simple Explanation

The Corinthians wanted proof of Christ's power in Paul. Paul answers by pointing to Christ Himself. "He was crucified through weakness." At the cross, Jesus appeared utterly weak. He was arrested, mocked, beaten, and executed as a criminal. He did not call down angels or defend Himself. To onlookers, it looked like total defeat.

"Yet he lives through the power of God." The weakness of the cross was not the end. God raised Jesus from the dead by His mighty power. The crucified one is now the risen Lord.

Paul says the same pattern is true in his own life. "For we also are weak in him." Paul shares in Christ's weakness through his sufferings and limitations. "But we will live with him through the power of God toward you." Just as Christ lives by God's power, Paul too will act in God's power when he comes to Corinth. His weakness is real, but it is not the whole story.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse summarizes the theology of the whole letter. The life of a believer follows the shape of Christ's life: weakness and death, followed by resurrection power. Paul said it earlier: [[2 Corinthians 4:10|always carrying in the body the putting to death of the Lord Jesus, that the life of Jesus may also be revealed in our body]].

The cross shows that God's power does not work the way human power works. Human power dominates and displays itself. God's power was revealed through the self-giving weakness of the crucified Christ, and then through His resurrection. As Paul wrote in 1 Corinthians 1:25, the weakness of God is stronger than men.

This means that weakness, when joined to Christ, is never final. Every grave, failure, and sorrow can be followed by God's resurrection power. Believers live in hope because the pattern of Christ's life is the pattern of their own.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

When believers feel weak, defeated, or overwhelmed, this verse reminds them that weakness is not the end of the story. Christ's weakness led to resurrection, and those who are in Him share in His life through the power of God.

This verse also offers a pattern for leadership and service. Paul did not try to prove his authority through displays of power. He pointed to the cross and the resurrection. Believers can serve with humility, trusting that God's power works through them most clearly when they rely on Him rather than on their own strength or status.

### 2 Corinthians 13:5 — Examine Yourselves

verse: 2 Corinthians 13:5

#### Simple Explanation

The Corinthians had spent a great deal of energy examining Paul, questioning his credentials, his speaking, his motives, and his authority. Now Paul turns the question around. "Examine your own selves, whether you are in the faith. Test your own selves."

The word "test" was used for testing metals to see whether they were genuine. Paul asks the Corinthians to look honestly at their own lives to see whether their faith is real.

"Or don't you know about your own selves, that Jesus Christ is in you?" This question expects a positive answer. Paul believes that most of them will recognize that Christ is indeed living in them. Their faith came through Paul's preaching, and the Spirit's presence among them was evidence that the gospel was real.

"Unless indeed you are disqualified." This is a sober warning. If someone examines their life and finds no evidence of Christ, no faith, no love, no repentance, no fruit of the Spirit, that is a serious matter that should lead them to turn to Christ.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Self-examination is a regular theme in Scripture. Paul told the Corinthians to examine themselves before taking the Lord's Supper (1 Corinthians 11:28). The psalmist prayed, [[Psalms 139:23|Search me, God, and know my heart]]. Healthy self-examination looks at one's life in the light of God's truth.

It is important to understand what this examination is meant to do. It is not meant to produce endless anxiety or doubt. Paul fully expected that the Corinthians would find Christ in them. The goal is honest assurance, not fear. Assurance of salvation rests ultimately on Christ's work and God's promises (Romans 8:38–39; 1 John 5:13), but genuine faith also shows itself in a changed life, and examining that evidence can confirm faith or reveal a need to turn to God.

Different Christian traditions place different emphases here. Some stress that believers should look mainly to Christ's promises rather than to their own performance, since looking too much at oneself can lead to discouragement. Others stress the importance of examining the fruit of one's life. Both emphases have value, and Paul's words hold them together: look honestly at yourself, and look for Christ in you.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

It is often easier to evaluate others than to evaluate oneself. Like the Corinthians, believers can spend much energy critiquing leaders, churches, or fellow Christians while neglecting their own hearts. This verse calls for a turn inward, an honest look at one's own faith, love, and obedience.

This examination can be done prayerfully, perhaps by reading a passage like Galatians 5:22–23 or 1 Corinthians 13 and asking God to show where growth is needed. For those who struggle with constant doubt about their salvation, it is helpful to remember that the goal is to find Christ in them, not to find perfection. Even weak faith, placed in Christ, is genuine faith. Those who find no evidence of faith at all can take the examination as an invitation to come to Christ today.

### 2 Corinthians 13:11 — Rejoice, Be Perfected, Live in Peace

verse: 2 Corinthians 13:11

#### Simple Explanation

As Paul closes this intense letter, he gives a series of brief, warm instructions.

"Rejoice!" The word can also mean "farewell," as it was a common way to say goodbye, but Paul likely intends both meanings. After all the tension, he calls them to joy.

"Be perfected." This is related to the word in verse 9 meaning restored or mended. Paul calls them to put things right, to let God repair what has been broken in their community.

"Be comforted." This may also mean "be encouraged" or "listen to my appeal." Paul wants them to receive the comfort and exhortation of this letter.

"Be of the same mind." The church had been divided. Paul calls them to unity, to share the same purpose and commitment to Christ.

"Live in peace." Conflict had marked the church for a long time. Paul urges them to pursue peace with one another.

Then comes the promise: "and the God of love and peace will be with you." When believers pursue love and peace, they experience the presence of the God who is Himself love and peace.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These five instructions form a summary of what Paul has hoped for the Corinthians throughout both letters. He wanted them to be a joyful, restored, encouraged, united, and peaceful community. These are not merely human goals; they reflect the character of God.

The title "the God of love and peace" appears only here in the New Testament, though Paul often speaks of "the God of peace" (Romans 15:33; Philippians 4:9). It reminds the Corinthians that their divisions and conflicts were contrary to the nature of the God they worshiped. A community that reflects God's character will be marked by love and peace.

The order is also worth noting. The promise of God's presence comes with the call to pursue love and peace. This does not mean God's love is earned by good behavior, but that a community living in harmony experiences His presence more fully, while a community torn by conflict grieves the Spirit (Ephesians 4:30–32).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These five short commands offer a practical guide for any church, family, or friendship that has been through conflict. Rejoice in what God has done. Allow God to restore what is broken. Receive encouragement. Seek unity around what matters most. Pursue peace actively.

Being "of the same mind" does not mean everyone must agree on every opinion. It means sharing a common commitment to Christ and to one another, so that differences do not become divisions. Peace may require humility, forgiveness, and patience. Where peace is not possible because of ongoing harm or abuse, safety must come first, but wherever reconciliation is possible, believers can pursue it, trusting that the God of love and peace will be with them.

### 2 Corinthians 13:14 — Grace, Love, and Fellowship

verse: 2 Corinthians 13:14

#### Simple Explanation

Paul ends the letter with a blessing that has been used in Christian worship for centuries. It names three gifts and three persons.

"The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ." Grace is God's undeserved favor, shown most fully in Jesus, who became poor so that others might become rich (2 Corinthians 8:9). Paul begins with grace because it is through Christ that people come to know God's love.

"God’s love." This is the Father's love, the source of salvation. God's love sent His Son and reconciled the world to Himself (2 Corinthians 5:18–19).

"The fellowship of the Holy Spirit." "Fellowship" means sharing, participation, or communion. The Holy Spirit brings believers into fellowship with God and with one another. It can mean both the fellowship the Spirit creates among believers and believers' participation in the Spirit Himself.

"Be with you all." Paul's blessing is for everyone in the church, including those who had opposed him. His final word is not condemnation but grace.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is one of the clearest expressions in the New Testament of what the church later came to describe as the Trinity: one God in three persons, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Paul does not explain the doctrine here, but he names the three persons side by side as the source of every blessing. Matthew 28:19 does something similar when Jesus commands baptism in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

The order is interesting. Paul mentions Christ first, then God the Father, then the Spirit. This reflects how believers come to know God. Through the grace of Christ, they discover the love of the Father, and they enter into the fellowship of the Spirit.

For a church divided by conflict, this blessing was especially fitting. The very life of God is a life of love and fellowship. The Corinthians were being invited into that divine fellowship and called to reflect it in their relationships with one another.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many churches use this blessing at the end of worship services. It can also be a daily prayer for oneself and others. Praying it reminds believers that every blessing comes from the Father, the Son, and the Spirit, and that their life together is meant to reflect God's own life of love.

This verse also shows how to end a difficult conversation or season. After sharp words and painful conflict, Paul ends with grace, love, and fellowship. Even when correction is necessary, the final word for God's people is His blessing. Believers can follow this example in their own relationships, letting grace have the last word.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Second Corinthians 13 brings together the firmness and the tenderness of Paul's ministry. He warns the Corinthians that unrepented sin will be dealt with, and he insists that matters be handled fairly, on the testimony of witnesses. Yet his deepest desire is not to exercise discipline but to see the church restored. He would rather appear weak than have to act harshly, because his authority exists for building up, not tearing down. This offers a model for any leader, parent, or friend who must address wrongdoing: be honest and firm, act justly, and always aim for restoration.

The chapter also calls each believer to self-examination. It is easy to evaluate others and neglect one's own heart. Paul invites the Corinthians, and readers today, to look honestly at their lives and find Christ living in them. This is not meant to produce fear but to bring assurance and, where needed, repentance. Even weak faith, resting in Christ, is real faith.

Paul's reflection on Christ's weakness and power reminds believers that weakness is never the final word. Christ was crucified in weakness, but He lives by the power of God. Those who are united with Him share in both, so they can face their own weaknesses with hope.

Finally, the letter ends with a call to rejoice, be restored, be encouraged, be united, and live in peace, followed by a blessing that names Father, Son, and Spirit. After a letter full of conflict and hard truths, the last word is grace. Believers can carry this blessing into their own relationships, letting the grace of Christ, the love of God, and the fellowship of the Spirit shape how they live together.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, thank You for the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, for Your boundless love, and for the fellowship of Your Spirit. Search my heart and show me whether I am living in the faith; let me find Christ living in me, and lead me to repentance wherever I have wandered. Thank You that Christ was crucified in weakness but lives by Your power, and that my weakness is not the end of my story. Help me to care more about others doing what is right than about how I appear. Give me joy, restore what is broken in me and in my relationships, comfort me, unite me with Your people, and make me a peacemaker. May the God of love and peace be with me and with all Your people. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
