# Philemon 1
theme: Receive Him as a Beloved Brother
main: Paul, writing from prison, greets Philemon, a dear coworker whose home hosts a church, and thanks God for his love and faith, which have refreshed the hearts of the believers. Although Paul could command him, he appeals to him in love on behalf of Onesimus, Philemon's slave, who became Paul's spiritual son while Paul was in chains. Once "useless" to Philemon, Onesimus is now truly useful. Paul sends him back, though he would have liked to keep him, so that Philemon's goodness would be freely chosen. Perhaps Onesimus was separated for a while so that Philemon could have him back forever, no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother. Paul asks Philemon to welcome Onesimus as he would welcome Paul himself, and to charge anything Onesimus owes to Paul's account. Confident that Philemon will do even more than he asks, Paul requests a guest room and sends greetings from his companions.

## Chapter Background and Story

Philemon was a believer in Colossae, probably a man of some wealth, since he owned at least one slave and had a house large enough for a church to meet in. He had likely come to faith through Paul's ministry, perhaps during Paul's years in Ephesus (v19; Acts 19:10). Apphia may have been his wife, and Archippus perhaps his son or a church leader (Colossians 4:17).

Onesimus was a slave in Philemon's household. At some point, he left. He may have run away, perhaps taking money or property with him (v18). Some scholars suggest instead that he sought out Paul deliberately, as a friend of his master, to plead for help after a dispute, which was a recognized practice in Roman law. Either way, Onesimus ended up with Paul in prison, heard the gospel, and became a believer. He then served Paul faithfully.

Now Paul faced a delicate situation. Under Roman law, a runaway slave could be severely punished, branded, or even executed. Paul could not simply keep Onesimus without Philemon's consent. So he sent Onesimus back, together with Tychicus, carrying this letter (Colossians 4:7–9). The letter is a masterpiece of tact, love, and gospel-shaped persuasion.

This letter also raises the question of how the gospel relates to slavery. Paul does not explicitly command Philemon to free Onesimus, though many readers believe that is what he hints at when he says Philemon will do "even beyond what I say" (v21). What Paul does is even more revolutionary in its implications: he insists that Onesimus be received as "a beloved brother," as Paul himself would be received. Once a master sees his slave as a brother in Christ and an equal before God, the foundations of slavery begin to crumble. Early Christian tradition suggests that Onesimus may later have become a leader in the church; a bishop of Ephesus named Onesimus is mentioned in a letter written by Ignatius of Antioch in the early second century, though whether he is the same person is uncertain.

## Important Verses Explained

### Philemon 1:4–7 — Love That Refreshes Hearts

verse: Philemon 1:4-7

#### Simple Explanation

Paul begins with thanksgiving. "I thank my God always, making mention of you in my prayers." Paul regularly prayed for Philemon.

Verse 5: "hearing of your love and of the faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints." Philemon was known for his love and faith, directed both toward Jesus and toward fellow believers.

Verse 6 states Paul's prayer: "that the fellowship of your faith may become effective in the knowledge of every good thing which is in us in Christ Jesus." This verse is difficult to translate, but its meaning seems to be that Paul prays Philemon's shared faith, his partnership with other believers, would become active and effective, leading to a fuller understanding of every good thing believers have in Christ. In other words, Paul prays that Philemon's faith would show itself in action, and that as it does, he would understand more deeply the riches of life in Christ.

Verse 7: "For we have much joy and comfort in your love, because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed through you, brother." Philemon's love had brought joy and encouragement to many believers. "Hearts" here is a strong word meaning the deepest emotions. "Refreshed" means given rest, like a weary traveler finding relief.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This thanksgiving sets the stage for Paul's request. Philemon was known for refreshing others with his love. Now Paul will ask him to extend that same love to Onesimus. Paul is not asking Philemon to become someone different, but to act consistently with who he already is.

Verse 6 connects faith with action. Faith is not merely private belief; it becomes "effective" when it is expressed in love and generosity. James 2:14–17 makes a similar point. As believers put their faith into action, they come to know more fully the good things they have in Christ.

The description of Philemon as one who refreshes hearts is a beautiful picture of Christian love. Some people leave others drained; others leave them refreshed. Philemon was the second kind. His hospitality, generosity, and kindness renewed the spirits of those around him.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can ask whether their love refreshes others. Do people leave their company encouraged, strengthened, and renewed? Hospitality, kindness, listening, and practical help are ways of refreshing others' hearts.

Believers can also pray for one another as Paul prayed for Philemon: that their faith would become active and effective, expressed in love and good works.

Finally, this passage shows the value of encouragement. Paul tells Philemon how much his love has meant. Believers can take time to tell others how they have been encouraged by them.

### Philemon 1:8–11 — Useless Became Useful

verse: Philemon 1:8-11

#### Simple Explanation

Paul now begins his request. "Therefore though I have all boldness in Christ to command you that which is appropriate." As an apostle, Paul could have commanded Philemon to do the right thing.

Verse 9: "yet for love’s sake I rather appeal to you." Instead of commanding, Paul appeals on the basis of love. He describes himself as "Paul, the aged, but also a prisoner of Jesus Christ." Some interpreters read "the aged" as "an ambassador," since the Greek words are very similar. Either way, Paul presents himself not as a powerful authority but as an old man in chains, appealing to a friend.

Verse 10: "I appeal to you for my child Onesimus, whom I have become the father of in my chains." Paul finally names Onesimus, after carefully preparing Philemon's heart. He calls Onesimus "my child," because Onesimus had come to faith through Paul while Paul was in prison.

Verse 11: "who once was useless to you, but now is useful to you and to me." This is a wordplay. "Onesimus" means "useful" or "profitable." He had not lived up to his name. He had been useless, perhaps even harmful, to Philemon. But now, transformed by Christ, he is truly useful.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul's approach shows the power of love over authority. He could have demanded obedience, but he chose to appeal. Love invites rather than compels. This reflects the way God Himself often works, drawing people with kindness rather than forcing them (Romans 2:4; Hosea 11:4).

The transformation of Onesimus is a picture of what the gospel does. Those who were once "useless," lost in sin, unable to fulfill their purpose, are made new in Christ. Paul himself had been transformed from persecutor to apostle. Onesimus was transformed from runaway slave to beloved brother and faithful helper. Christ makes people what God intended them to be.

The fact that Onesimus came to faith while Paul was in chains shows that God works in unexpected places. Paul's imprisonment, which seemed like a setback, became the setting for a life changed by grace.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can follow Paul's example by appealing to others with love rather than demanding with authority, especially in delicate situations. Gentle persuasion often achieves more than force.

This passage also offers hope to anyone who feels "useless," whether because of past failures, broken relationships, or wasted years. In Christ, people are made new and given purpose. No one is beyond His transforming power.

Finally, believers can look for God at work in difficult circumstances. Like Paul in prison, they may find that hardship becomes an opportunity for God to change lives.

### Philemon 1:15–16 — No Longer as a Slave, but a Beloved Brother

verse: Philemon 1:15-16

#### Simple Explanation

Paul offers a new way of looking at what happened. "For perhaps he was therefore separated from you for a while that you would have him forever." Notice the gentle word "perhaps." Paul does not claim to know God's purposes for certain, but he suggests that God may have used Onesimus's departure for good. Notice also the passive phrase "was separated," as if God was quietly at work in the events. Onesimus was gone "for a while," but now Philemon could have him "forever," as a brother for eternity.

Verse 16: "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave, a beloved brother." This is the heart of the letter. Philemon is to receive Onesimus not merely as a returning slave but as something far greater: a brother in Christ, beloved.

"Especially to me, but how much rather to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord." Onesimus was dear to Paul, and he should be even dearer to Philemon, both "in the flesh," in their everyday human relationship, and "in the Lord," as fellow believers.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show how God can work through human failures and wrongs for good, much as Joseph said to his brothers, [[Genesis 50:20|you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good]]. This does not excuse wrongdoing, but it shows that God's purposes can prevail even through it.

The phrase "a beloved brother" is radical. In the Roman world, slaves were legally considered property. Paul declares that in Christ, Onesimus is Philemon's brother, equal before God (Galatians 3:28; Colossians 3:11). If Philemon truly received Onesimus as a beloved brother, the relationship of master and slave would be transformed from within, and its foundations would be undermined.

"Both in the flesh and in the Lord" means this brotherhood was not only spiritual but practical. It would change how Philemon treated Onesimus in daily life, not only on Sundays.

This passage, along with the rest of the New Testament's teaching on human dignity, helped plant the seeds that eventually led many Christians to work for the abolition of slavery. Tragically, others in later centuries ignored this teaching and used Scripture to defend slavery, a grave distortion that the church must reject.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers to see one another as family, regardless of social status, background, or past wrongs. In the church, there are no second-class members. Employers and employees, rich and poor, people of every ethnic background, are brothers and sisters in Christ.

They also encourage believers to look for God's purposes in painful situations. A broken relationship, a departure, or a loss may, in God's hands, become an opportunity for something new and lasting.

Finally, these verses invite believers to examine whether their faith changes how they treat people "in the flesh," in daily life. True brotherhood in Christ shows up in practical respect, kindness, and justice.

### Philemon 1:17–19 — Put That to My Account

verse: Philemon 1:17-19

#### Simple Explanation

Paul makes his request explicit. "If then you count me a partner, receive him as you would receive me." Paul identifies himself completely with Onesimus. Whatever welcome Philemon would give Paul, he should give Onesimus.

Verse 18: "But if he has wronged you at all or owes you anything, put that to my account." Onesimus may have stolen from Philemon or caused him financial loss. Paul offers to take responsibility for the debt. Whatever Onesimus owes, Philemon can charge to Paul.

Verse 19: "I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay it." Paul writes this promise himself, like signing a legal IOU. He is serious.

"Not to mention to you that you owe to me even your own self besides." With gentle humor, Paul reminds Philemon that he owes Paul far more, his very life in Christ, since he likely came to faith through Paul's ministry. Paul does not insist on it, but the reminder adds weight to his appeal.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses offer one of the most beautiful pictures of the gospel in the New Testament. Onesimus had wronged his master and owed a debt he could not pay. Paul stepped in as his advocate, identified himself with Onesimus, and offered to pay the debt himself. Philemon was asked to receive Onesimus as he would receive Paul.

This is exactly what Christ has done for sinners. Humanity has wronged God and owes a debt it cannot pay. Christ stands between sinners and God as their advocate (1 John 2:1). He identifies with them, takes their debt upon Himself, and pays it in full at the cross (Colossians 2:14). And God receives sinners as He receives His beloved Son, because they are in Him (Ephesians 1:6). Martin Luther once said that all of us are God's Onesimus.

This passage also shows what reconciliation can cost. Paul was willing to pay personally so that two people could be reconciled. True reconciliation sometimes requires someone to absorb a cost, whether financial, emotional, or relational.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can marvel at the grace of Christ, who has said to the Father, "Put that to my account," taking their debt of sin upon Himself. This truth can bring deep assurance and gratitude.

Believers can also follow Paul's example by helping others be reconciled. This might involve mediating conflicts, standing with someone who has done wrong and is seeking to make things right, or even bearing some cost to help restore a relationship.

At the same time, reconciliation should never pressure someone to return to a dangerous or abusive situation. Paul's appeal assumed that Philemon was a believer known for his love, and that Onesimus would be safe and welcomed as a brother. Reconciliation must always protect the dignity and safety of all involved.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Philemon is a short letter with a powerful message about the transforming power of the gospel in real relationships. Paul appeals to Philemon, a believer known for refreshing the hearts of others, to welcome back Onesimus, a slave who had wronged him but who had since become a believer and a beloved helper to Paul.

The letter shows how the gospel changes people. Onesimus, whose name means "useful," had once been useless to his master. But in Christ, he became truly useful. No one is beyond the reach of God's grace, and those who have failed can be made new.

The letter also shows how the gospel changes relationships. Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus not merely as a slave but as a beloved brother, both in everyday life and in the Lord. This vision of brotherhood across the deepest social divisions planted seeds that would one day help bring down the institution of slavery. Believers today are called to see every fellow believer as family, regardless of status or past wrongs, and to let that family bond shape how they treat one another.

Paul's method is also instructive. Rather than commanding, he appeals in love, wanting Philemon's kindness to be freely given. He is willing to take Onesimus's debt upon himself so that reconciliation can happen. In this, Paul reflects Christ, who stands between sinners and God and says, "Put that to my account." Believers who have received such grace are called to extend it, helping others be reconciled and treating one another with the welcoming love they have received from God.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for standing in my place, taking my debt upon Yourself, and bringing me to the Father as one He welcomes and loves. Make my love like Philemon's, refreshing the hearts of those around me. Help my faith to become active and effective in love. Thank You that You make the useless useful and give new life to those who have failed. Help me to see every believer as a beloved brother or sister, whatever their status, background, or past. Give me humility to appeal with love rather than demand with power, and courage to help others be reconciled, even when it costs me something. Keep me always mindful of the safety and dignity of every person. Let the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with my spirit, and with all Your people. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
