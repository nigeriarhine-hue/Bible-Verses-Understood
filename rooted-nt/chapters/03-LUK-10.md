# Luke 10
theme: Sent Out, Showing Mercy, and Sitting at Jesus' Feet
main: Jesus sends out seventy messengers to prepare His way and, when they return rejoicing over their power, tells them to rejoice instead that their names are written in heaven. Asked by a lawyer who counts as a neighbor, He tells the parable of the Good Samaritan, showing that being a neighbor means showing mercy to anyone in need. At the home of Martha and Mary, He gently teaches that listening to Him is the one thing most needed.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 10 continues the journey to Jerusalem that began in Luke 9:51, when Jesus [[Luke 9:51|intently set his face to go to Jerusalem]]. Much of the material in this chapter is found only in Luke, including the mission of the seventy, the parable of the Good Samaritan, and the visit to Martha and Mary. The chapter moves from mission (going out to others), to love for neighbor (showing mercy), to devotion (sitting at Jesus' feet). Together these three scenes show what a disciple's life looks like: serving, loving, and listening.

### The Mission of the Seventy (10:1–16)

After choosing and sending the Twelve (Luke 9:1–6), Jesus [[v1|appointed seventy others]] and sent them two by two ahead of Him into every town He planned to visit. (Some manuscripts read seventy-two.) The number may recall the seventy elders who helped Moses (Numbers 11:16–25) or the seventy nations listed in Genesis 10, suggesting that the mission would eventually reach all peoples.

Jesus told them, [[v2|The harvest is indeed plentiful, but the laborers are few. Pray therefore to the Lord of the harvest, that he may send out laborers into his harvest]]. He warned them that He was sending them [[v3|as lambs among wolves]], vulnerable in a hostile world. They were to travel light, carrying no purse, bag, or sandals, and not stop to exchange long greetings on the road, a sign of urgency.

When they entered a house, they were to say, [[v5|Peace be to this house]]. If someone of peace lived there, a person open to God's message, their peace would rest on that home. They were to stay in the same house, eating whatever was offered, because [[v7|the laborer is worthy of his wages]]. Paul later quotes this saying (1 Timothy 5:18). They were to heal the sick and announce, [[v9|God’s Kingdom has come near to you]].

If a town rejected them, they were to wipe its dust off their feet as a warning and still declare that the kingdom had come near. Jesus pronounced woes on Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum, towns that had seen His miracles but had not repented (see Matthew 11:20–24). He concluded, [[v16|Whoever listens to you listens to me, and whoever rejects you rejects me. Whoever rejects me rejects him who sent me]]. The messengers represented Jesus Himself.

### The Return of the Seventy (10:17–24)

The seventy returned with joy, saying, [[v17|Lord, even the demons are subject to us in your name!]] Jesus replied, [[v18|I saw Satan having fallen like lightning from heaven]]. Their success over demons was a sign that Satan's power was being broken by the coming of God's kingdom. Jesus had given them authority to [[v19|tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy]]. Serpents and scorpions were symbols of evil and danger (compare Psalm 91:13). Then He redirected their joy, a teaching studied below.

[[v21|In that same hour, Jesus rejoiced in the Holy Spirit]]. Luke alone records that Jesus was full of joy in the Spirit. He thanked the Father for hiding these things from the wise and learned and revealing them to little children, people with humble, childlike faith. He declared that all things had been entrusted to Him by the Father, and that only the Son truly knows the Father and those to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him. Then He told His disciples privately that they were blessed to see what they were seeing, because many prophets and kings had longed to see and hear these things but had not.

### The Parable of the Good Samaritan (10:25–37)

An expert in the law stood up to test Jesus: [[v25|Teacher, what shall I do to inherit eternal life?]] Jesus asked what the law said. The man answered by combining Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18: love God with all your heart, soul, strength, and mind, and love your neighbor as yourself. Jesus said, [[v28|You have answered correctly. Do this, and you will live]].

But the man, [[v29|desiring to justify himself]], asked, [[v29|Who is my neighbor?]] He wanted to define "neighbor" narrowly so that he could be sure he had fulfilled the command. Many Jews of that time understood "neighbor" to mean fellow Jews, especially those who kept the law.

Jesus answered with a story. A man was going down from Jerusalem to Jericho. The road descended about 3,300 feet over roughly seventeen miles through rocky, desolate terrain, and it was known for robbers. The man was attacked, stripped, beaten, and left half dead.

A priest happened to be going down the same road. He saw the man and passed by on the other side. A Levite, a temple assistant, did the same. Both were religious leaders who served in the temple. They may have feared that the man was dead, since touching a corpse would make them ritually unclean (Numbers 19:11–16), or they may have feared that robbers were nearby. Whatever their reasons, they did not help.

Then a Samaritan came along. Jews and Samaritans despised each other. Just one chapter earlier, a Samaritan village had refused to welcome Jesus, and James and John wanted to call down fire on it (Luke 9:52–54). Jesus' listeners would have expected the Samaritan to be the villain. Instead, the Samaritan was the hero. This parable is studied below.

Jesus asked which of the three proved to be a neighbor to the man. The lawyer answered, [[v37|He who showed mercy on him]]; he could not bring himself to say "the Samaritan." Jesus told him, [[v37|Go and do likewise]].

### Martha and Mary (10:38–42)

As Jesus and His disciples continued their journey, they came to a village where a woman named Martha welcomed Him into her home. John's Gospel tells us that this village was Bethany, near Jerusalem, and that Martha and Mary had a brother named Lazarus (John 11:1). Martha's sister Mary [[v39|sat at Jesus’ feet and heard his word]]. Sitting at a teacher's feet was the posture of a disciple. In that culture, it was unusual for a woman to take this place, which was normally reserved for men studying under a rabbi.

Martha was [[v40|distracted with much serving]], busy preparing food and hospitality for Jesus and likely His disciples, a large group. She came to Jesus and said, [[v40|Lord, don’t you care that my sister left me to serve alone? Ask her therefore to help me]]. Jesus' answer is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 10:20 — Rejoice That Your Names Are Written in Heaven

verse: Luke 10:20

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes when the seventy messengers returned from their mission. Jesus had sent them out in pairs to towns He planned to visit, with authority to heal the sick and announce that God's kingdom had come near. They came back full of excitement: [[v17|Lord, even the demons are subject to us in your name!]]

Jesus affirmed that their success was real and significant. He said He saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven, meaning that their victories over demons showed the defeat of Satan's power. He confirmed that He had given them authority over the enemy's power.

But then He redirected their joy: [[v20|Nevertheless, don’t rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven]].

The idea of names written in heaven appears throughout Scripture. Moses spoke of God's book (Exodus 32:32–33), Daniel mentioned people whose names are written in the book (Daniel 12:1), and Revelation speaks of the Lamb's book of life (Revelation 21:27). Having one's name written in heaven means belonging to God, being a citizen of His kingdom, and being assured of eternal life.

Jesus was not saying that the disciples' ministry success did not matter. He was saying it should not be the primary source of their joy. Ministry success can come and go; some missions succeed and others fail. But their relationship with God, their names written in heaven, was secure and permanent.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that a believer's deepest joy should come from belonging to God, not from achievements, even spiritual ones. It is possible to find identity and satisfaction in ministry success, spiritual gifts, or results. But these can lead to pride when things go well and despair when they do not. Jesus points His followers to something more secure: God's grace in choosing them and writing their names in heaven.

It also guards against a dangerous misunderstanding. Elsewhere, Jesus warned that some who did mighty works in His name, even casting out demons, would be rejected because He never knew them (Matthew 7:22–23). Spiritual power is not proof of salvation. What matters is a genuine relationship with God.

Finally, the verse shows that salvation is God's work. Names are written in heaven by God, not by human effort. This truth is a source of humble joy and security.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine where they find their joy. If their joy depends on how well their ministry, work, or spiritual life seems to be going, it will rise and fall with circumstances. Believers can root their joy in the unchanging truth that they belong to God.

It also encourages those who feel their service is unsuccessful. Pastors, missionaries, teachers, parents, and others who serve faithfully may see little visible fruit. They can rejoice that their names are written in heaven, and that their worth before God does not depend on their results.

Finally, this verse warns believers against pride in spiritual achievements. Gifts and successes come from God. Believers can be grateful for them while keeping their hearts focused on the greater gift of salvation.

### Luke 10:33–34 — The Samaritan Who Showed Compassion

verse: Luke 10:33-34

#### Simple Explanation

These verses describe the turning point of the parable of the Good Samaritan. Jesus told this story in response to a lawyer who asked, "Who is my neighbor?" In the story, a man traveling from Jerusalem to Jericho was attacked by robbers, stripped, beaten, and left half dead by the road. A priest came by and passed on the other side. A Levite did the same. Both were religious leaders who served in God's temple, yet both avoided the wounded man.

Then Jesus said, [[v33|But a certain Samaritan, as he traveled, came where he was]]. Jesus' listeners would have been shocked. Samaritans were despised by Jews as religious heretics and ethnic outsiders. The hostility went both ways. A Samaritan would have been the last person they expected to be the hero.

[[v33|When he saw him, he was moved with compassion]]. The priest and Levite also "saw" the man, but they passed by. The Samaritan saw him and was moved with deep compassion, the same word used for Jesus' compassion for the widow of Nain (Luke 7:13).

Then Jesus describes the Samaritan's actions in detail: he [[v34|came to him, and bound up his wounds, pouring on oil and wine]]. Oil soothed wounds, and wine acted as an antiseptic. He [[v34|set him on his own animal]], meaning the Samaritan walked so the injured man could ride. He [[v34|brought him to an inn, and took care of him]]. The next day, he gave the innkeeper two denarii, about two days' wages, and promised to repay any additional expenses when he returned.

The Samaritan's compassion was practical, personal, costly, and continuing. He risked his own safety on a dangerous road, used his own supplies, gave up his own comfort, spent his own money, and committed to future care for a stranger who was likely Jewish, someone from a people who despised him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This parable transforms the question, "Who is my neighbor?" The lawyer wanted to know the limits of his obligation, whom he was required to love. Jesus turned the question around. At the end, He asked, "Which of these three proved to be a neighbor?" The question is not "Who deserves my love?" but "Will I be a neighbor to anyone in need?" Love does not set boundaries based on ethnicity, religion, or social status.

It also exposes the danger of religion without compassion. The priest and Levite were respected religious figures, but they failed the test of love. The Samaritan, an outsider, fulfilled God's law. This echoes Jesus' teaching that God desires mercy, not sacrifice.

Many Christians throughout history have also seen in the Good Samaritan a picture of Jesus Himself. Humanity lies wounded and helpless by the road of life, and Jesus, rejected and despised, comes near, binds up wounds, and pays the cost of healing. While this is not the main point of the parable, it reflects the compassion Jesus showed throughout His ministry.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers to show compassion to anyone in need, regardless of who they are. Believers can ask themselves who might be lying "by the road" in their own lives: a neighbor who is struggling, a coworker in crisis, a stranger in trouble, or someone from a group they find difficult to love.

They also show that compassion must be practical. The Samaritan did not just feel sorry for the man; he acted. Believers can look for concrete ways to help: giving time, resources, practical assistance, and ongoing support. Compassion often costs something.

Finally, these verses warn against excuses. The priest and Levite may have had reasons, such as religious duties, fear, or busyness. Believers can examine whether they use similar excuses to avoid helping others. Jesus' command is clear: "Go and do likewise."

### Luke 10:41–42 — One Thing Is Needed

verse: Luke 10:41-42

#### Simple Explanation

These verses are Jesus' response to Martha, who had welcomed Him into her home. Martha was busy with all the work of hospitality, likely preparing a large meal for Jesus and His disciples. Her sister Mary sat at Jesus' feet, listening to His teaching. Martha became frustrated and came to Jesus, saying, [[v40|Lord, don’t you care that my sister left me to serve alone? Ask her therefore to help me]].

Martha's frustration is understandable. Hospitality was an important duty in that culture, and serving a large group was hard work. She was doing something good. But her complaint revealed that her service had become a burden that made her anxious and resentful, even toward Jesus.

Jesus answered gently, repeating her name: [[v41|Martha, Martha]]. Repeating a name in Scripture often expresses affection and concern (compare "Simon, Simon," Luke 22:31). Then He said, [[v41|you are anxious and troubled about many things]]. "Anxious" means worried, pulled in different directions. "Troubled" means upset or disturbed. Martha's mind was divided by many concerns.

[[v42|but one thing is needed]]. Out of all the things Martha was worried about, only one thing was truly necessary. [[v42|Mary has chosen the good part, which will not be taken away from her]]. Mary had chosen to sit at Jesus' feet and listen to His word. This was the "good part," and Jesus would not take it from her by sending her to help in the kitchen.

Jesus was not condemning Martha's service or saying that work does not matter. Elsewhere, Luke presents service positively (Luke 8:3; 22:27). Rather, He was saying that listening to Him comes first, and that service done in anxiety and distraction loses its joy.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses teach that relationship with Jesus is the most important thing in life. Activity, even good activity done for Jesus, cannot replace time spent with Him. Mary's posture at Jesus' feet shows the priority of listening to His word.

They also show that busyness can be a spiritual danger. Martha was doing good things, but she was "distracted." Her service had become a source of anxiety and comparison. When believers serve without first spending time with Jesus, their service can become a burden and their hearts can grow resentful.

Finally, Jesus' defense of Mary is significant. In that culture, women were not usually allowed to sit at a rabbi's feet as disciples. Jesus affirmed Mary's choice and would not send her back to traditional roles. He welcomed women as His learners and disciples.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to make time to sit at Jesus' feet. In busy lives full of responsibilities, it is easy to neglect prayer, Scripture, and quiet time with God. Believers can prioritize listening to Jesus, trusting that this is "the one thing needed."

They also invite believers to examine their service. Are they serving with joy, or with anxiety and resentment? If service has become a burden, it may be a sign that they need to return to Jesus' feet and be renewed in His presence.

Finally, these verses warn against comparing oneself with others. Martha compared her work with Mary's and felt unfairly treated. Believers can avoid judging others' devotion and instead focus on their own relationship with Jesus.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 10 presents three aspects of discipleship: going out on mission, loving one's neighbor, and listening to Jesus. All three are essential, and they work together.

The mission of the seventy reminds believers that Jesus sends His followers into the world to share the good news and show God's kingdom. The harvest is large, and workers are few. Believers can pray for more workers and be willing to go themselves. They can also remember that their deepest joy is not in ministry success but in belonging to God, with their names written in heaven.

The parable of the Good Samaritan challenges believers to be neighbors to anyone in need. Love does not ask, "Who deserves my help?" but "How can I help this person?" This love crosses boundaries of ethnicity, religion, and social status, and it is shown in practical, costly action. Believers can look for the wounded people along their own roads and respond with compassion.

The story of Martha and Mary reminds believers that sitting at Jesus' feet is the "one thing needed." Mission and service flow from relationship with Him. Without time in His presence, service can become anxious and burdensome. Believers can build regular time for listening to Jesus into their lives and serve from a place of rest rather than worry.

Taken together, the chapter shows a balanced life of discipleship: going out to share the good news, showing mercy to those in need, and returning regularly to sit at Jesus' feet.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord of the harvest, the harvest is great and the workers are few. Send out workers into Your harvest, and make me willing to go where You send me. Thank You that my name is written in heaven. Help me to find my deepest joy in belonging to You, not in my successes or achievements.

Give me the compassion of the Good Samaritan. Open my eyes to the wounded people along my road, and keep me from passing by on the other side. Help me to show mercy in practical, generous ways to anyone in need, even to those I find hard to love. And Lord, when I become anxious and troubled about many things, call me back to the one thing needed. Teach me to sit at Your feet and listen to Your word, so that my service flows from love and not from worry. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
