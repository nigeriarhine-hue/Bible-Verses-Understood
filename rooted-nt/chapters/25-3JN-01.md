# 3 John 1
theme: Faithful Hospitality and Humble Leadership
main: The elder writes to his beloved friend Gaius, praying that he may prosper and be in good health just as his soul prospers. He rejoices greatly at reports that Gaius is walking in the truth, saying that he has no greater joy than to hear that his children are walking in the truth. He commends Gaius for his faithful service to traveling believers, even strangers, who have testified about his love before the church, and urges him to send them on their way in a manner worthy of God. These missionaries went out for the sake of the Name, accepting nothing from unbelievers, so believers ought to support such people and become fellow workers for the truth. In contrast, Diotrephes, who loves to be first, refuses to welcome the elder's messengers, spreads wicked accusations, refuses to receive the brothers, and even throws out of the church those who want to welcome them. The elder urges Gaius not to imitate what is evil but what is good, for whoever does good is from God. He commends Demetrius, who is well spoken of by everyone and by the truth itself. He hopes to see Gaius soon and speak face to face, and he sends a blessing of peace.

## Chapter Background and Story

Third John is a personal letter, much like a note between friends. Yet it addresses an important matter: how churches should treat those who travel to spread the gospel, and how leaders should use their position.

In the early church, missionaries and teachers traveled from city to city, often with little money. They relied on believers in each place to give them food, lodging, and help for the next stage of their journey. This was not a small kindness. Without such hospitality, the spread of the gospel would have been much slower. Believers who welcomed these travelers became partners in their mission.

Second John warned against welcoming false teachers. Third John shows the other side: the importance of welcoming true servants of the gospel. Taken together, the two letters teach that hospitality should be generous and also discerning.

### Greeting and Joy (vv1–4)

[[v1|The elder to Gaius the beloved, whom I love in truth]]. The elder's affection for Gaius is warm and personal. He calls him "beloved" four times in this short letter (vv1, 2, 5, 11). Their friendship is rooted in shared faith, love "in truth."

The elder's prayer for Gaius and his joy over believers walking in truth are studied below.

### Faithful Hospitality (vv5–8)

Gaius's faithful service to traveling believers is studied below.

### The Problem of Diotrephes (vv9–10)

[[v9|I wrote to the assembly, but Diotrephes, who loves to be first among them, doesn’t accept what we say]]. The elder had written an earlier letter to the church, but Diotrephes refused to accept it. The root problem is named clearly: Diotrephes "loves to be first." He wanted control and prominence. This is the opposite of the servant leadership Jesus taught (Mark 10:42–45).

[[v10|Therefore, if I come, I will call attention to his deeds which he does, unjustly accusing us with wicked words. Not content with this, he doesn’t receive the brothers himself, and those who would, he forbids and throws out of the assembly]]. Diotrephes's behavior was harmful in several ways. He spread false and malicious accusations. He refused hospitality to the traveling missionaries. He prevented others from showing hospitality, and he expelled from the church those who tried. His love of position led him to use his power to control others.

The elder does not respond with secret maneuvering or harsh counterattacks. He intends to come in person and bring Diotrephes's actions into the open. Wrongdoing by leaders is not to be hidden but addressed honestly, with the truth brought to light.

### Imitate What Is Good (vv11–12)

The call to imitate what is good is studied below. [[v12|Demetrius has the testimony of all, and of the truth itself; yes, we also testify, and you know that our testimony is true]]. Demetrius may have been the carrier of this letter or one of the traveling missionaries. The elder commends him strongly. Everyone speaks well of him; his life matches the truth; and the elder himself adds his own testimony. In contrast to Diotrephes, Demetrius is a model of a trustworthy servant.

### Closing (vv13–14)

[[v13|I had many things to write to you, but I am unwilling to write to you with ink and pen;]] [[v14|but I hope to see you soon. Then we will speak face to face. Peace be to you. The friends greet you. Greet the friends by name]]. As in 2 John, the elder prefers personal conversation to writing. He closes with a blessing of peace. The words "Greet the friends by name" show the personal care that marked the early church. Believers were not anonymous members of a crowd, but known and loved individuals.

## Important Verses Explained

### 3 John 1:2–4 — No Greater Joy

verse: 3 John 1:2-4

#### Simple Explanation

The elder begins with a prayer for his friend: "Beloved, I pray that you may prosper in all things and be healthy, even as your soul prospers." This was a common way of beginning letters in the ancient world, but the elder fills it with Christian meaning. He prays for Gaius's overall well-being and physical health, and he notes that Gaius's soul is already prospering. Gaius is spiritually healthy, and the elder wishes that the rest of his life would match.

In verse 3, the elder explains his joy: "For I rejoiced greatly when brothers came and testified about your truth, even as you walk in truth." Traveling believers had returned with reports about Gaius. His life matched the truth he believed.

Verse 4 expresses the elder's deepest joy: "I have no greater joy than this: to hear about my children walking in truth." The elder likely calls Gaius and others his "children" because he had led them to faith or nurtured them spiritually. Nothing pleased him more than seeing them live faithfully.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Verse 2 has sometimes been used to teach that God always wills financial wealth and perfect health for believers. That reading goes beyond what the verse says. It is a friend's prayer for another's well-being, similar to wishing someone good health in a letter. It shows that God cares about the whole person, body and soul, and that it is right to pray for one another's health and circumstances. But it does not promise that faithful believers will never be poor or sick. Scripture also shows faithful believers enduring illness, hardship, and lack (2 Corinthians 12:7–10; Philippians 4:11–13; 2 Timothy 4:20).

The elder's priority is clear: the prosperity of the soul comes first. He measures Gaius's life by his faithfulness to the truth, not by his wealth or status.

His joy in verse 4 reveals the heart of a true spiritual leader. His greatest satisfaction is not personal success, influence, or recognition, but the faithfulness of those he cares for. This echoes Paul, who told the believers in Thessalonica, [[1 Thessalonians 2:20|For you are our glory and our joy]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can follow the elder's example by praying for the whole well-being of others: their health, their circumstances, and especially their spiritual lives. It is good to pray for practical needs, and it is even more important to pray that people's souls prosper.

This passage also encourages believers to examine what brings them the greatest joy. Parents, mentors, pastors, and friends can find deep joy in seeing those they love walk in truth. Investing in others' faith brings lasting rewards.

Finally, Gaius's example shows that faith is seen in how one lives. Others noticed that he walked in truth. Believers today can pray that their lives, too, will bear witness to the truth they believe.

### 3 John 1:5–8 — Fellow Workers for the Truth

verse: 3 John 1:5-8

#### Simple Explanation

The elder praises Gaius's hospitality: "Beloved, you do a faithful work in whatever you accomplish for those who are brothers and strangers." Gaius had welcomed traveling believers, even those he had never met before. His care for them was an act of faithfulness to God.

In verse 6, the elder notes that these travelers "have testified about your love before the assembly." Gaius's kindness had become known in the elder's church. The elder then urges him: "You will do well to send them forward on their journey in a way worthy of God." To "send them forward" meant providing what they needed for the next part of their trip: food, money, supplies, perhaps companions.

Verse 7 explains why they deserve support: "because for the sake of the Name they went out, taking nothing from the Gentiles." These missionaries traveled for the sake of Jesus' name. They refused to accept support from unbelievers, perhaps so that no one could accuse them of preaching for profit.

Verse 8 draws the conclusion: "We therefore ought to receive such, that we may be fellow workers for the truth." Those who support missionaries share in their work.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Hospitality is a deeply biblical virtue. Abraham welcomed strangers who turned out to be messengers from God (Genesis 18; Hebrews 13:2). Jesus said that whoever receives one of His messengers receives Him (Matthew 10:40). Hospitality is not merely good manners; it is a way of serving Christ.

The phrase "in a way worthy of God" sets a high standard. Believers should treat God's servants as they would want to treat God Himself. Support should be generous, thoughtful, and given with honor.

The idea of being "fellow workers for the truth" reveals something important about the body of Christ. Not everyone is called to travel and preach. But everyone can share in the mission. Those who give, host, pray, and encourage are partners in the work, and they share in its fruit. The mission of the church is a shared endeavor.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers today can practice this kind of hospitality in many ways: welcoming visiting missionaries, opening their homes to fellow believers, supporting those who serve in ministry, and showing kindness to strangers.

Supporting gospel work is not reserved for a few. Through prayer, giving, encouragement, and practical help, every believer can become a fellow worker for the truth. Small acts of generosity, a meal, a room, a gift, a note of encouragement, can strengthen those who labor for the gospel.

This passage also encourages believers to support those who serve with integrity, who go out "for the sake of the Name" rather than for personal gain.

### 3 John 1:11 — Imitate What Is Good

verse: 3 John 1:11

#### Simple Explanation

After describing the harmful behavior of Diotrephes, the elder gives Gaius a simple instruction: "Beloved, don’t imitate that which is evil, but that which is good." People naturally imitate those around them, especially leaders. The elder urges Gaius not to follow the example of Diotrephes, but to follow what is good.

He then gives a principle: "He who does good is of God." Doing good is a sign that a person belongs to God. Then he adds the opposite: "He who does evil hasn’t seen God." A person who habitually does evil shows that they do not truly know God.

These words echo the teaching of 1 John, where right actions, especially love, reveal whether a person truly knows God (1 John 3:10).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse recognizes the power of example. Diotrephes was a church leader, and his example could lead others astray. Leaders shape the culture of a community. That is why Scripture holds leaders to a high standard of character (1 Timothy 3:1–7; 1 Peter 5:2–3).

But this verse also places responsibility on every believer. Gaius was not obligated to imitate Diotrephes just because Diotrephes held a position of authority. Believers are called to imitate what is good, not simply whoever is in charge. Position does not make behavior right.

The connection between doing good and knowing God is a theme throughout John's writings. Those who have truly seen God, through faith in Jesus, are transformed by that encounter. Their lives begin to reflect His goodness. A life marked by persistent evil, especially by pride, control, and mistreatment of others, reveals that a person has not truly known God, whatever position they hold.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers today are surrounded by examples, some good and some harmful, in churches, workplaces, families, and public life. This verse calls them to be thoughtful about whom they imitate. Not every leader, influencer, or popular figure is worth following.

The contrast between Diotrephes and Demetrius gives a clear picture. One loved to be first and used power to control and exclude. The other was well spoken of by all and lived according to the truth. Believers can seek out and follow leaders who show humility, integrity, and love, and they can aim to be such examples for others.

This verse also gives courage to those who find themselves in communities where leaders behave badly. Believers are not required to follow harmful examples or remain silent about wrongdoing. Like the elder, they can bring wrong into the light in a truthful and appropriate way. Where a leader is mistreating or controlling people, believers should seek help from trusted leaders and, where needed, from outside authorities. And in every situation, they can choose to do what is good.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Third John shows how the truth of the gospel is lived out in everyday life. Gaius walked in the truth, and others could see it. The elder's greatest joy was to hear that those he loved were walking faithfully. Believers today can pray for one another's whole well-being, body and soul, and find their deepest joy in seeing others grow in faith.

The letter also highlights the importance of hospitality and partnership in the gospel. Gaius welcomed traveling believers, even strangers, and sent them on their way in a manner worthy of God. In doing so, he became a fellow worker for the truth. Believers today can share in God's mission through generous support of those who serve, open homes, practical help, and faithful prayer.

The example of Diotrephes is a sober warning. His love of being first led him to reject others, spread false accusations, and control the church. Pride and selfish ambition can damage any community, including churches. Leaders and members alike are called to the humility of Jesus, who came not to be served but to serve.

Finally, the letter calls believers to imitate what is good. Those who do good show that they belong to God. Like Demetrius, believers can seek to live in such a way that their lives bear witness to the truth, and like the elder, they can value the joy of personal fellowship, knowing and greeting one another by name.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, thank You for the joy of walking in the truth and for the friends You have given me in Your family. Help me to care for the whole well-being of others, and above all to pray that their souls prosper. Give me a generous and welcoming heart, so that I may serve Your people faithfully and become a fellow worker for the truth. Guard me from the love of being first, from pride and selfish ambition, and make me humble like Jesus, who came to serve. Help me to imitate what is good and not what is evil, and let my life bear a good testimony to Your truth. Thank You for the gift of fellowship, and teach me to know and love Your people by name. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
