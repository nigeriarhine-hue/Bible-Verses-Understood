# Luke 15
theme: Lost and Found: The Joy of God Over Repentant Sinners
main: When religious leaders grumble that Jesus welcomes sinners and eats with them, He answers with three parables about lost things: a sheep, a coin, and a son. In each, the one who searches rejoices when the lost is found, showing heaven's joy over one sinner who repents. The story of the father and his two sons reveals a father who runs to welcome a returning son and pleads with an angry older son to join the celebration.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 15 is one of the best-loved chapters in the Bible. It contains three parables, the last of which, often called the parable of the prodigal son, is one of the most famous stories Jesus ever told. All three parables answer a single complaint.

The setting is described in the first two verses: [[v1|Now all the tax collectors and sinners were coming close to him to hear him]]. Tax collectors were despised as collaborators who enriched themselves at others' expense. "Sinners" referred to people known for immoral lives or who did not keep the law as religious leaders expected. These people were drawn to Jesus.

But [[v2|The Pharisees and the scribes murmured, saying, “This man welcomes sinners, and eats with them.”]] The word "murmured" recalls Israel grumbling against God in the wilderness (Exodus 16:2). In that culture, eating with someone signaled acceptance and fellowship. The religious leaders believed that a holy teacher should separate himself from sinners. Jesus' table fellowship with them seemed to approve of their sin. Jesus answered with three parables that reveal God's heart toward the lost.

### The Lost Sheep (15:3–7)

Jesus asked which of them, having a hundred sheep and losing one, would not leave the ninety-nine in the open country and search for the lost one until he found it. A flock of a hundred was a medium-sized flock, probably belonging to a family or village. Shepherds counted their sheep each evening, and a missing sheep meant a real loss. The shepherd would leave the others in the care of other shepherds and search the wilderness.

When he finds it, [[v5|he carries it on his shoulders, rejoicing]]. A lost sheep would often lie down helpless and frightened, unable or unwilling to move. The shepherd carries it home himself. Then he calls his friends and neighbors to celebrate. Jesus concluded with the lesson that is studied below: there is more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine righteous people who need no repentance.

Jesus had addressed His critics directly: "Which of you men...?" Many of the Pharisees would have considered shepherds lowly and ritually unreliable people. Yet Jesus asked them to imagine themselves as shepherds, and He used the image to describe God, the great Shepherd of Israel (Psalm 23; Ezekiel 34:11–16).

### The Lost Coin (15:8–10)

The second parable features a woman with ten silver coins, called drachmas, each worth about a day's wage. Losing one was significant for a poor household. These coins may have been her savings or part of her dowry, sometimes worn as a headdress. Village houses had few small windows and dirt floors, so a lost coin would be hard to find. She lit a lamp, swept the house, and searched carefully until she found it. Then she called her friends and neighbors to rejoice with her.

Jesus said, [[v10|Even so, I tell you, there is joy in the presence of the angels of God over one sinner repenting]]. "In the presence of the angels" is a reverent way of referring to God Himself rejoicing. Notice that Jesus used a woman to represent God's searching love, pairing her with the male shepherd, as Luke often pairs men and women in his Gospel.

### The Lost Son (15:11–24)

The third parable is longer and more personal. A man had two sons. The younger said, [[v12|Father, give me my share of your property]]. Under Jewish law, the younger of two sons would inherit one-third of the estate (Deuteronomy 21:17 gave the firstborn a double share). But inheritance normally came at the father's death. Asking for it early was deeply insulting, almost like wishing his father dead. It also meant breaking up the family property, which might include land and livestock that would have to be sold. Remarkably, the father agreed and divided his property between the sons.

Not long after, the younger son gathered everything, turned it into cash, and traveled to [[v13|a far country]], a Gentile land far from family and faith. There he wasted his money in reckless, wild living. When everything was gone, a severe famine struck. He became desperate and hired himself out to a citizen of that country, who sent him to feed pigs. For a Jewish young man, this was utter degradation; pigs were unclean animals (Leviticus 11:7). He was so hungry that he longed to eat the pods the pigs were eating, probably carob pods, a cheap animal feed. No one gave him anything.

Then [[v17|he came to himself]]. This turning point is studied below. He decided to return to his father, confess his sin, and ask to be treated as a hired servant.

He got up and went home. [[v20|But while he was still far off, his father saw him and was moved with compassion, and ran, fell on his neck, and kissed him]]. The father's response is studied below. The son began his prepared speech, but the father interrupted before he could ask to become a servant. He told his servants to bring the best robe, a ring for his hand, and sandals for his feet, and to kill the fattened calf for a celebration. The robe signified honor, the ring authority and family membership, and the sandals showed he was a son, since servants often went barefoot. A fattened calf was saved for the most important occasions and could feed an entire village. The father explained, [[v24|for this, my son, was dead and is alive again. He was lost and is found]].

### The Older Son (15:25–32)

Meanwhile, the older son was working in the field. As he came near the house, he heard music and dancing. A servant told him that his brother had returned and that the father had killed the fattened calf. The older son became angry and refused to go in.

The father came out and pleaded with him, just as he had gone out to meet the younger son. The older son complained, [[v29|Behold, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed a commandment of yours, but you never gave me a goat, that I might celebrate with my friends]]. He referred to his brother as [[v30|this your son]], refusing to call him "my brother," and accused him of wasting the father's wealth on prostitutes, a detail not mentioned earlier.

The father's answer is studied below. The parable ends without telling us whether the older son went in to the celebration. Jesus leaves the story open, because the older son represents the Pharisees and scribes who were grumbling about Jesus welcoming sinners. The question was whether they would join the celebration of God's grace.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 15:7 — Joy in Heaven Over One Sinner Who Repents

verse: Luke 15:7

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes the parable of the lost sheep. Jesus told this parable because the Pharisees and scribes were complaining that He welcomed sinners and ate with them. They believed a holy teacher should avoid such people. Jesus responded by asking them to imagine being a shepherd who has a hundred sheep and loses one. Would he not leave the ninety-nine and search for the lost one until he found it? When he finds it, he carries it home on his shoulders, rejoicing, and calls his friends and neighbors to celebrate with him.

Then Jesus applied the parable: [[v7|I tell you that even so there will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents, than over ninety-nine righteous people who need no repentance]].

"Joy in heaven" refers to God's own joy and the joy of all in His presence. When a sinner repents, God celebrates. This was a striking picture of God. The religious leaders thought God would be pleased by separation from sinners; Jesus said God rejoices when sinners are found and brought home.

[[v7|one sinner who repents]]. The lost sheep represents a sinner, someone who has wandered away from God. Repentance means turning back to God. In the parable, the sheep does not find its own way home; the shepherd searches for it and carries it. This shows that repentance is a response to God's seeking grace.

[[v7|than over ninety-nine righteous people who need no repentance]]. This phrase contains gentle irony. Everyone needs repentance (Romans 3:23). The "righteous people who need no repentance" likely refers to those who think they do not need it, like the Pharisees who were grumbling. They saw themselves as the ninety-nine safe sheep. Jesus' words invited them to recognize that heaven's joy is focused on the lost being found.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that God actively seeks the lost. He is like a shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine to search for one sheep. God takes the initiative in salvation. Jesus later summarized His mission: [[Luke 19:10|For the Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost]].

It also reveals that God's heart is full of joy over repentance. God does not grudgingly accept repentant sinners; He celebrates. This corrects the image of God as harsh or reluctant to forgive. The prophet Zephaniah described God rejoicing over His people with singing (Zephaniah 3:17).

Finally, the verse challenges those who see themselves as righteous. If God rejoices over sinners who repent, His people should share that joy rather than resent it. Self-righteousness can make people blind to God's heart and to their own need.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse offers hope to anyone who feels lost. God is not waiting with anger; He is searching with love. Those who have wandered from God can turn back to Him, knowing that heaven will rejoice at their return.

It also invites believers to share God's joy over repentance. When someone comes to faith or returns to God, believers can celebrate wholeheartedly, regardless of that person's past.

Finally, this verse calls believers to join God's search for the lost. Like the shepherd, they can reach out to people who have wandered from God, showing patience and love, and helping bring them home.

### Luke 15:17–18 — "When He Came to Himself"

verse: Luke 15:17-18

#### Simple Explanation

These verses describe the turning point in the life of the younger son in Jesus' parable. He had demanded his inheritance from his father while his father was still alive, a deeply insulting request. Then he traveled to a distant country and wasted everything in reckless living. When a severe famine struck, he had nothing left. He hired himself out to a local citizen who sent him to feed pigs, unclean animals in Jewish law. He was so hungry that he wanted to eat the pigs' food, but no one gave him anything. He had reached rock bottom.

Then [[v17|But when he came to himself]]. This phrase means he came to his senses, he woke up to reality. Up to this point, he had been living in a kind of illusion, thinking that freedom from his father and money would bring happiness. Now he saw clearly.

He reasoned, [[v17|How many hired servants of my father’s have bread enough to spare, and I’m dying with hunger!]] Even his father's hired workers, day laborers who had the least security, had more than enough food. He was starving.

Then he decided: [[v18|I will get up and go to my father, and will tell him, “Father, I have sinned against heaven and in your sight.”]] He planned to admit his sin honestly. "Against heaven" means against God; "in your sight" means against his father. He recognized that his actions had wronged both God and his father. In the next verse, he planned to say he was no longer worthy to be called a son and to ask to be made a hired servant.

His repentance involved several steps: recognizing his true condition, remembering his father's goodness, deciding to return, and planning to confess his sin honestly without excuses.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses describe what genuine repentance looks like. It begins with "coming to oneself," seeing one's situation truthfully. Sin often involves self-deception, believing that life apart from God will bring freedom and joy. Repentance begins when that illusion is broken.

The son's motivation is worth noticing. His first thought was hunger, a practical need. His motives were not perfectly pure. Yet the father received him anyway. God welcomes people who come to Him even with mixed motives, and He transforms them through His grace.

These verses also show that true repentance involves honest confession. The son did not blame others or make excuses. He said simply, "I have sinned." This echoes David's confession in Psalm 51:4. Honest confession is the doorway to restoration.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite anyone who has wandered from God to "come to themselves." Sometimes it takes hardship, emptiness, or loss to make people see the truth about their lives. Those who find themselves in a "far country," whether through addiction, broken relationships, or simply drifting from God, can stop, consider their situation honestly, and remember the Father's goodness.

They also encourage honest confession. Believers can bring their sins to God without excuses or blame, simply saying, "I have sinned." God welcomes honest hearts.

Finally, these verses show that it is never too late to return. The son had wasted everything and fallen as low as he could imagine. Yet the way home was still open. No one has gone too far to come back to God.

### Luke 15:20 — The Father Who Ran

verse: Luke 15:20

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the emotional heart of the parable. The younger son had wasted his inheritance, fallen to the lowest point of his life, and decided to return home to confess his sin and ask to be a hired servant. He had a speech prepared. He may have been nervous about how his father would respond. In that culture, a son who shamed his family this way could expect rejection, and the village might also treat him with contempt.

[[v20|He arose and came to his father]]. He acted on his decision.

[[v20|But while he was still far off, his father saw him]]. The father saw him from a distance, suggesting that he had been watching and waiting, hoping for his son's return.

[[v20|and was moved with compassion]]. The father's first response was not anger but deep compassion, the same word used of Jesus' compassion for the widow of Nain and of the Samaritan's compassion for the wounded man.

[[v20|and ran]]. In that culture, a dignified older man did not run. Running required lifting one's long robes and exposing one's legs, which was considered undignified. Yet the father ran. He set aside his own dignity to reach his son. Some interpreters suggest he also ran to reach his son before the villagers could mock or reject him, protecting him from shame.

[[v20|fell on his neck, and kissed him]]. He embraced his son and kissed him repeatedly, a sign of forgiveness and reconciliation (compare Esau and Jacob, Genesis 33:4). The father welcomed him before the son could even finish his confession.

When the son began his speech, the father interrupted him, calling for the best robe, a ring, sandals, and a feast. The son had planned to ask to be a servant; the father restored him fully as a son.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of God toward repentant sinners. God is not a reluctant judge waiting for sinners to prove themselves. He is a Father who watches, longs, and runs to welcome them home. Jesus was answering the Pharisees' complaint by showing that His welcome of sinners reflected the Father's own heart.

It also shows the extravagance of God's grace. The son expected to be a servant; the father restored him as a son. God does not merely tolerate repentant sinners; He restores them to full relationship as His children. Paul writes that believers have received [[Romans 8:15|the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, “Abba! Father!”]]

Finally, the father's running suggests the humility of God's love. Just as the father set aside his dignity to embrace his son, God in Christ humbled Himself to come to sinners. The ultimate expression of this is the cross, where Jesus bore shame to bring sinners home.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse assures anyone who is afraid to return to God that He is waiting with compassion. Whatever they have done, the Father is ready to run to them and embrace them. They do not need to clean themselves up first; they can simply come home.

It also helps believers understand their identity. Through Christ, they are not servants trying to earn their place, but beloved children, fully restored. They can live with the security of being welcomed and loved.

Finally, this verse calls believers to reflect the Father's heart toward others. When people who have wandered return to faith or turn to God for the first time, believers can welcome them warmly, without suspicion or condescension, as the father welcomed his son.

### Luke 15:31–32 — The Father's Plea to the Older Son

verse: Luke 15:31-32

#### Simple Explanation

These verses are the father's response to his older son. While the household celebrated the younger son's return, the older son came in from working in the field and heard music and dancing. When he learned that his father had thrown a feast for his wayward brother, he became angry and refused to go in.

The father came out and pleaded with him, just as he had gone out to meet the younger son. The older son complained bitterly. He had served his father for years, never disobeyed, and never received even a young goat for a party with his friends. But when "this son of yours" came home after wasting the father's money on prostitutes, the father killed the fattened calf. His words revealed resentment, pride, and a sense that he had earned more than he received. He also showed that he thought of himself as a servant rather than a son ("I have served you").

The father answered gently: [[v31|Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours]]. He addressed him with an affectionate word for "child." He reminded him that he had always enjoyed the father's presence and that everything the father had belonged to him. The older son had not lost anything by his brother's return. His inheritance was secure.

Then the father explained: [[v32|But it was appropriate to celebrate and be glad, for this, your brother, was dead, and is alive again. He was lost, and is found]]. Notice the father's words: "this, your brother." The older son had said "this son of yours," refusing to acknowledge his brother. The father reminded him of their relationship. Celebration was "appropriate," the right and fitting response, because a brother who was as good as dead had come back to life.

The parable ends here. We are not told whether the older son went in to the celebration. Jesus left the ending open.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal that the older son was also lost, though he never left home. He was physically near his father, but his heart was far from him. He saw his relationship with his father as service and reward rather than love. This describes the Pharisees and scribes who grumbled that Jesus welcomed sinners. They were religious and obedient outwardly, but they did not share the Father's heart.

The verses also show that God's love reaches out to the self-righteous as well as to the openly sinful. The father went out to both sons. God invites religious people who resent His grace to come in and join the celebration.

Finally, the open ending invites a response. The older son represents anyone who thinks they deserve God's favor because of their obedience, and who resents God's generosity toward others. The question remains: will they come in and rejoice?

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers who have been faithful for a long time. It is possible to serve God outwardly while losing sight of His love and becoming resentful of His grace to others. Believers can examine whether they see themselves as servants earning rewards or as beloved children who already share everything the Father has.

They also call believers to celebrate when others come to God, regardless of their past. Believers can welcome repentant sinners as brothers and sisters, not as people who must prove themselves first.

Finally, these verses offer comfort to those who have always stayed close to God but sometimes feel overlooked. The Father says, "You are always with me, and all that is mine is yours." Their faithfulness is not forgotten, and their inheritance is secure. Joy at others' return does not diminish God's love for them.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 15 reveals the heart of God more clearly than almost any other chapter in the Bible. God is a shepherd who searches for lost sheep, a woman who sweeps the house for a lost coin, and a father who runs to welcome his wayward son. He rejoices when the lost are found. Anyone who feels far from God can take courage: He is searching for them and longs for their return.

The younger son's story shows what repentance looks like: coming to one's senses, remembering the Father's goodness, getting up, and confessing honestly. It also shows that God's welcome is far greater than anyone expects. He does not make repentant sinners into servants; He restores them as sons and daughters. Believers can rest in this identity and live as beloved children.

The older son's story warns against self-righteousness. It is possible to stay close to God outwardly while resenting His grace to others. Believers who have served faithfully can guard against bitterness and comparison, remembering that everything the Father has is theirs. They can join the celebration when others come home.

Finally, the chapter calls believers to share God's heart. Like the shepherd and the woman, they can seek those who are lost. Like the father, they can welcome returning sinners with open arms. And like heaven itself, they can rejoice over every sinner who repents.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, thank You for searching for me like a shepherd seeks a lost sheep and a woman searches for a lost coin. Thank You that there is joy in heaven over one sinner who repents. When I have wandered into a far country, help me to come to my senses, remember Your goodness, and return to You with honest confession.

Thank You for running to meet me, embracing me, and restoring me as Your child, not as a servant. Help me to live in the security of Your love. Guard me from the resentment of the older son. When others return to You, help me to rejoice with You and welcome them as my brothers and sisters. Give me Your heart for the lost, and help me to join You in seeking and welcoming them home. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
