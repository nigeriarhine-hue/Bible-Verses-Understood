# Acts 23
theme: "Cheer Up, Paul": God's Presence and Providence in the Midst of Plots
main: Before the Jewish council, Paul declares that he has lived before God with a good conscience, is struck on the high priest's orders, and responds sharply before apologizing out of respect for God's law. When he identifies himself as a Pharisee on trial for the hope of the resurrection, the council divides. That night, the Lord stands beside Paul and tells him to take courage, because he must testify in Rome. More than forty men vow not to eat until they kill Paul, but his nephew uncovers the plot, and the Roman commander sends Paul under heavy guard to the governor Felix in Caesarea.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 23 continues the story of Paul's arrest in Jerusalem. The Roman commander, Claudius Lysias, still did not understand why the Jewish leaders were so hostile to Paul. In chapter 22, he planned to have Paul flogged to find out, but stopped when he learned that Paul was a Roman citizen. Now he brought Paul before the Sanhedrin, the Jewish ruling council, to discover the charges.

This chapter shows the danger Paul faced and God's protection of him. Through a vision, through the courage of a young relative, and through the actions of a Roman commander, God kept Paul safe so that he could testify in Rome.

### Paul before the Council (23:1–5)

Paul began, [[v1|Brothers, I have lived before God in all good conscience until today]]. This statement is studied below. The high priest, Ananias, immediately ordered those standing near Paul to strike him on the mouth. This Ananias (a different man from the disciple in Damascus) was high priest from about A.D. 47 to 59. Josephus describes him as greedy and violent. He was later killed by Jewish rebels at the start of the war against Rome in A.D. 66.

Paul responded sharply: [[v3|God will strike you, you whitewashed wall! Do you sit to judge me according to the law, and command me to be struck contrary to the law?]] A "whitewashed wall" was a wall that looked solid but was weak underneath, painted over to hide its cracks (Ezekiel 13:10–16). Jesus had called the Pharisees [[Matthew 23:27|whitened tombs]]. Paul pointed out the hypocrisy of a judge who broke the law while claiming to judge by it. The Law required fair trials (Leviticus 19:15; Deuteronomy 25:1–2). Paul's words proved prophetic; Ananias died violently some years later.

Those standing nearby were shocked: [[v4|Do you malign God’s high priest?]] Paul's response is studied below.

### Pharisees and Sadducees Divided (23:6–10)

Paul recognized that the council included both Sadducees and Pharisees. He called out, [[v6|Men and brothers, I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees. Concerning the hope and resurrection of the dead I am being judged!]] Paul was not merely using clever tactics. The resurrection was at the heart of his message. He was on trial because he proclaimed that Jesus had risen from the dead, and that through Him there would be a resurrection of the dead. As a Pharisee, Paul shared the Pharisees' belief in the resurrection; now he knew it had begun in Jesus.

His statement divided the council. [[v8|For the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, nor angel, nor spirit; but the Pharisees confess all of these]]. Some of the Pharisees' scribes stood up and said, [[v9|We find no evil in this man. But if a spirit or angel has spoken to him, let’s not fight against God!]] Their words echoed Gamaliel's warning in Acts 5:39. The argument became so violent that the commander feared Paul would be torn apart, so he ordered his soldiers to take Paul back to the barracks.

### The Lord Stands by Paul (23:11)

That night, the Lord appeared to Paul. This encouragement is studied below.

### The Plot to Kill Paul (23:12–22)

The next morning, more than forty men [[v12|banded together and bound themselves under a curse, saying that they would neither eat nor drink until they had killed Paul]]. They went to the chief priests and elders, asking them to request that the commander bring Paul to the council again, so that the conspirators could ambush and kill him on the way. That religious leaders cooperated with an assassination plot shows how far their hatred had gone.

How God revealed the plot is studied below. The commander took Paul's nephew aside, heard the plot, and told him not to tell anyone.

### Sent to Caesarea (23:23–35)

The commander acted quickly. He ordered two centurions to prepare [[v23|two hundred soldiers to go as far as Caesarea, with seventy horsemen and two hundred men armed with spears, at the third hour of the night]], about nine in the evening. Nearly half of the Jerusalem garrison was sent to protect one prisoner. They provided horses for Paul and took him to Felix, the governor.

The commander wrote a letter to Felix. It is the only letter in Acts from a Roman official. He explained that Paul had been seized by the Jews and was about to be killed when he rescued him, [[v27|having learned that he was a Roman]]. This was a slight bending of the truth; he had learned Paul was a Roman only later, when he was about to flog him. He wrote that Paul was accused [[v29|about questions of their law, but not to be charged with anything worthy of death or of imprisonment]]. Like Gallio in Corinth, a Roman official found Paul innocent of any crime under Roman law.

The soldiers brought Paul by night to Antipatris, about forty miles from Jerusalem. The next day, the foot soldiers returned to Jerusalem while the horsemen continued with Paul to Caesarea, about twenty-five miles farther. Felix read the letter, asked what province Paul was from, and said, [[v35|I will hear you fully when your accusers also arrive]]. He ordered Paul to be kept under guard in Herod's palace, the governor's headquarters built by Herod the Great.

Felix was governor of Judea from about A.D. 52 to 59. The Roman historian Tacitus wrote that he "exercised the power of a king with the mind of a slave." He was known for cruelty and corruption.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 23:1 — A Good Conscience Before God

verse: Acts 23:1

#### Simple Explanation

The Roman commander had brought Paul before the Sanhedrin, the highest Jewish court. These were the same leaders who had condemned Jesus and Stephen. Paul knew some of them; he had once worked with them to persecute the church.

[[v1|Paul, looking steadfastly at the council]]. Paul looked directly at the council members. He was not intimidated or ashamed.

[[v1|said, “Brothers, I have lived before God in all good conscience until today.”]] Paul addressed them as "brothers," fellow Jews. Then he made a bold statement: he had lived his life before God with a clear conscience.

What did Paul mean? He did not claim to be sinless. He knew he had persecuted the church, and he called himself the chief of sinners (1 Timothy 1:15). But he meant that he had always acted according to what he believed was right before God. Before his conversion, he persecuted the church sincerely, believing it was God's will. After his conversion, he followed Jesus with the same sincerity. And now, having received forgiveness through Christ, his conscience was clear before God.

Paul later told Felix, [[Acts 24:16|In this I also practice always having a conscience void of offense toward God and men]].

The high priest's response was to have Paul struck on the mouth, perhaps because he considered Paul's claim arrogant, or because he resented Paul's confidence.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the importance of a clear conscience. The conscience is the inner sense of right and wrong that God has given every person. Paul wrote that Gentiles show the work of the law written in their hearts, [[Romans 2:15|their conscience testifying with them]]. Living with a clear conscience means living in a way that one believes is pleasing to God.

It also shows that a sincere conscience is not always a correct one. Paul had once persecuted the church with a clear conscience, because his conscience was misinformed. The conscience must be shaped by God's truth. Paul later wrote that he was [[1 Corinthians 4:4|not justified by this]], meaning that a clear conscience does not prove one is right; only God is the final judge.

Finally, the verse shows the freedom that comes from forgiveness. Paul could speak of a clear conscience because Christ had cleansed him. Hebrews speaks of the blood of Christ cleansing [[Hebrews 9:14|your conscience from dead works to serve the living God]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to live with a clear conscience. Believers can seek to do what is right before God and others, confessing and making things right when they fail. A clear conscience brings peace and boldness.

It also encourages believers to shape their conscience by Scripture. Since the conscience can be mistaken, believers need God's word to inform and correct it. Regular reading of Scripture helps believers develop a conscience that agrees with God's truth.

Finally, the verse offers freedom to those burdened by guilt. Through Christ, believers' consciences can be cleansed. Those who have confessed their sins and trusted Christ can stand before God without shame, knowing that they are forgiven.

### Acts 23:5 — Respect for Those in Authority

verse: Acts 23:5

#### Simple Explanation

When Paul declared that he had lived with a good conscience before God, the high priest Ananias ordered him to be struck on the mouth. This was a violation of the law, which required a fair hearing. Paul responded angrily: "God will strike you, you whitewashed wall! Do you sit to judge me according to the law, and command me to be struck contrary to the law?"

Those standing nearby were shocked: [[v4|Do you malign God’s high priest?]]

[[v5|Paul said, “I didn’t know, brothers, that he was high priest.”]] Why didn't Paul know? There are several possibilities. Paul had been away from Jerusalem for many years and may not have recognized Ananias. The meeting may have been informal, without the high priest wearing his official robes or sitting in his usual place. Paul may have had poor eyesight (Galatians 4:15; 6:11). Or Paul may have been speaking ironically, meaning that a man who acted like that did not behave like a high priest. Most interpreters take Paul's words at face value: he did not realize who had given the order.

[[v5|For it is written, ‘You shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people.’]] Paul quoted Exodus 22:28. As soon as he realized he had spoken harshly to the high priest, he acknowledged that he should not have done so, because God's law commands respect for those in authority.

Paul's response is remarkable. He had been treated unjustly, yet he quickly acknowledged his error and submitted to God's word.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the importance of respecting authority, even when those in authority act wrongly. Scripture calls believers to honor those in positions of authority (Romans 13:1–7; 1 Peter 2:17). This does not mean approving of their wrongdoing or obeying them when they command sin, but it does mean treating the office with respect.

It also shows the humility of a mature believer. Paul did not justify himself or make excuses. He recognized his mistake and quoted Scripture against himself. James writes that the wisdom from above is [[James 3:17|peaceful, gentle, reasonable]].

Finally, the verse shows that even great believers sometimes react in anger. Paul was human. Under pressure and injustice, he spoke harshly. Jesus, when struck before the high priest, responded calmly (John 18:22–23). Paul's quick acknowledgment shows the importance of humility and correction.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to speak respectfully about those in authority, including government leaders, employers, and church leaders, even when they disagree with them. Believers can challenge injustice while still showing respect.

It also encourages believers to acknowledge their mistakes quickly. When believers speak or act wrongly, they can admit it, apologize, and seek to make it right. Humility is a mark of spiritual maturity.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that respecting authority does not mean accepting injustice silently. Paul pointed out that the high priest was violating the law. Believers can stand for justice while maintaining respect, speaking the truth with grace.

### Acts 23:11 — "Cheer Up, Paul"

verse: Acts 23:11

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been through a terrible series of events. He had been attacked by a mob in the temple, nearly killed, arrested, bound in chains, nearly flogged, struck on the mouth, and caught in a violent argument in the council so fierce that the commander feared he would be torn apart. Now he was alone in the Roman barracks at night. He did not know what would happen next. He may have wondered whether his mission was over.

[[v11|The following night, the Lord stood by him]]. In Paul's loneliest moment, the Lord came to him. Jesus did not send an angel or a message; He Himself stood by Paul.

[[v11|and said, “Cheer up, Paul]]. The words can also be translated "Take courage." Jesus spoke words of comfort and encouragement. He knew Paul's fears and discouragement.

[[v11|for as you have testified about me at Jerusalem, so you must testify also at Rome.]] Jesus affirmed that Paul's witness in Jerusalem had been faithful. Paul had testified to the crowd and to the council. Then Jesus gave a promise: Paul "must" testify in Rome. This was God's plan, and it would not fail. Paul had longed to go to Rome (19:21; Romans 1:10–15). Now Jesus promised that he would.

This promise sustained Paul through everything that followed: two years in prison in Caesarea, trials before governors and a king, a dangerous voyage, and a shipwreck. Through it all, Paul knew he would reach Rome because Jesus had promised.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the faithful presence of Jesus with His servants. Jesus had promised, [[Hebrews 13:5|I will in no way leave you, neither will I in any way forsake you]]. In Paul's darkest moments, Jesus came to stand beside him. Paul later wrote of a time when everyone deserted him: [[2 Timothy 4:17|But the Lord stood by me and strengthened me]].

It also shows that God's purposes cannot be stopped. Paul faced mobs, councils, and assassination plots, but God had determined that he would testify in Rome. No human opposition could prevent it. The word "must" expresses divine necessity.

Finally, the verse shows that faithful witness is valued by Jesus. Jesus affirmed Paul's testimony in Jerusalem, even though it had been rejected. Faithfulness, not success, is what Jesus honors.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse brings comfort to believers who are discouraged, lonely, or afraid. Jesus stands by His people in their darkest moments. Believers can turn to Him in prayer, trusting that He is present and that He cares.

It also encourages believers to trust God's purposes. When circumstances seem to threaten God's plans for their lives, believers can trust that God's purposes will be accomplished. Nothing can stop what God has determined.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to remain faithful even when their witness seems unfruitful. Paul's testimony in Jerusalem was rejected, yet Jesus affirmed it. Believers can trust that faithful witness is never wasted, even when they do not see the results.

### Acts 23:16 — A Nephew Overhears the Plot

verse: Acts 23:16

#### Simple Explanation

More than forty men had sworn an oath not to eat or drink until they had killed Paul. They plotted with the chief priests and elders to ask the Roman commander to bring Paul to the council again, so they could ambush and kill him on the way. It was a well-organized conspiracy. Paul was in the barracks, unaware of the danger.

[[v16|But Paul’s sister’s son heard they were lying in wait, and he came and entered into the barracks and told Paul]]. This is the only mention in the New Testament of Paul's family. We learn that Paul had a sister, and that her son was in Jerusalem. We do not know how he heard about the plot; perhaps he overheard conversations, or perhaps his family had connections with the religious leaders. Somehow, the news reached him, and he acted.

The young man went into the barracks and told Paul. Paul called a centurion and asked him to take his nephew to the commander. The commander took the young man by the hand, led him aside, and listened privately. Notice the commander's kindness to the youth. The young man explained the plot and urged the commander not to agree to the request.

The commander believed him and acted immediately, sending Paul to Caesarea that night under heavy guard.

God had promised that Paul would testify in Rome. Here God fulfilled His promise through ordinary means: a young man who overheard a conversation and had the courage to act.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows God's providence working through ordinary people and events. There was no angel, earthquake, or miraculous escape this time. God used a young man's attentiveness and courage. Joseph told his brothers that God had used their actions for good (Genesis 50:20). God is sovereign over all events, both extraordinary and ordinary.

It also shows that God can use anyone. Paul's nephew is not even named. He was young and probably unknown. Yet God used him to save the life of the great apostle. God often works through the faithfulness of unknown people.

Finally, the verse shows how God's promises and human action work together. Jesus had promised that Paul would testify in Rome. That promise was fulfilled through the nephew's courage, Paul's wise response, and the commander's decision. God's sovereignty does not make human action unimportant; it works through it.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to look for God's hand in ordinary events. God does not always work through miracles. He often works through conversations, timing, and the actions of ordinary people. Believers can learn to recognize His providence in everyday life.

It also encourages young people and those who feel insignificant. Paul's nephew was a young man whose name we do not know, yet God used him to save Paul's life. Believers of every age can be used by God when they act with courage and integrity.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to act when they see danger or injustice. The nephew could have stayed silent, but he took a risk to warn Paul. Believers can speak up to protect others, especially those who are vulnerable.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 23 encourages believers to live with a clear conscience before God, shaped by His word and cleansed by Christ's forgiveness. Paul stood before the council with confidence because his conscience was clear. Believers can pursue integrity in all areas of life, confessing quickly when they fail.

The chapter also shows the importance of humility and respect. When Paul spoke harshly to the high priest, he quickly acknowledged his error and quoted Scripture against himself. Believers can show respect to those in authority, even when they disagree, and can humbly admit when they are wrong.

Acts 23 offers deep comfort in times of discouragement. In Paul's loneliest moment, the Lord stood beside him and said, "Cheer up." Believers can trust that Jesus stands beside them in their darkest hours, and that His purposes for their lives will be fulfilled.

Finally, the chapter shows God's providence working through ordinary means. A young man overheard a plot, and a Roman commander acted to protect Paul. God fulfilled His promise without a miracle, through the courage of an unnamed person. Believers can look for God's hand in everyday events and can be ready to act with courage when He gives them the opportunity.
