# Matthew 27
theme: The King Crucified
main: Jesus is handed over to Pilate, rejected in favor of Barabbas, mocked as a king, and crucified between two robbers. Judas dies in despair, the crowds and leaders taunt Jesus to save Himself, and Jesus cries out the words of Psalm 22 before giving up His spirit. At His death the temple curtain is torn, the earth shakes, and a Roman centurion confesses Him as the Son of God. His body is buried in a rich man's tomb, which is sealed and guarded.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 27 records the most important day in human history from Matthew's perspective: the crucifixion of Jesus. During the previous night, Jesus had been arrested in Gethsemane, tried before the Jewish council led by Caiaphas, and condemned for claiming to be the Messiah and the Son of God, while Peter denied Him in the courtyard (Matthew 26). Now, early in the morning, the council finalized its decision and handed Jesus over to the Roman governor.

The Jewish council could condemn Jesus by its own law, but under Roman rule it did not have the authority to carry out executions (John 18:31). Only the Roman governor could do that. The council therefore needed to present Jesus to Pilate in a way that would convince him that Jesus deserved death under Roman law. A charge of blasphemy would not interest Pilate, but a claim to be "King of the Jews" could be presented as rebellion against Caesar.

Throughout this chapter, Matthew emphasizes three themes: Jesus' innocence, His identity as King and Son of God, and the fulfillment of Scripture. He also shows a range of human responses: Judas's despair, Pilate's cowardice, the crowd's manipulation, the soldiers' mockery, the leaders' contempt, and finally the centurion's confession and the women's faithful presence.

### Jesus Handed Over to Pilate (27:1–2)

When morning came, the chief priests and elders formally decided to put Jesus to death. They bound Him and handed Him over to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor (prefect) of Judea from A.D. 26 to 36. Historical sources describe Pilate as harsh and insensitive to Jewish customs, but also wary of complaints reaching Rome. An inscription bearing his name was discovered at Caesarea in 1961, confirming his historical role. Governors normally lived in Caesarea on the coast but came to Jerusalem during festivals to keep order.

### The Death of Judas (27:3–10)

When Judas saw that Jesus had been condemned, he was seized with remorse. He brought back the thirty pieces of silver to the chief priests and elders and said, [[v4|I have sinned in that I betrayed innocent blood]]. His confession is one more testimony to Jesus' innocence. But the leaders replied coldly, [[v4|What is that to us? You see to it]]. They had used Judas and now discarded him. Judas threw the money into the temple and went away and hanged himself.

The chief priests, who had paid the money to arrange Jesus' death, now became scrupulous about the law. They said it was not lawful to put the money into the temple treasury because it was [[v6|the price of blood]]. They used it to buy a potter's field as a burial place for foreigners, and it became known as the Field of Blood. (Acts 1:18–19 gives a complementary account of Judas's death and the field.)

Matthew says this fulfilled what was spoken by Jeremiah the prophet. The wording draws mainly on Zechariah 11:12–13, where thirty pieces of silver, the price set on God's rejected shepherd, are thrown to the potter in the temple. But Matthew also has in mind passages in Jeremiah about a potter's house and the purchase of a field (Jeremiah 18–19; 32:6–15), especially Jeremiah 19, where a potter's vessel is broken in the Valley of Hinnom, renamed a valley of slaughter. Jewish writers sometimes combined prophecies and named only the more prominent prophet. Matthew is showing that the betrayal and its aftermath fit the pattern of God's rejected shepherd and the judgment that follows.

### Jesus Before Pilate (27:11–26)

Pilate asked Jesus, [[v11|Are you the King of the Jews?]] Jesus answered, [[v11|So you say]], affirming it but in a way that suggested Pilate did not fully understand what kind of king He was. When the chief priests and elders accused Him, Jesus said nothing. Pilate was amazed that He did not defend Himself.

It was Pilate's custom at the festival to release one prisoner chosen by the crowd. There was a notorious prisoner named Barabbas. (Some manuscripts give his full name as "Jesus Barabbas," which would make Pilate's question even more striking: which Jesus do you want?) Mark and Luke tell us Barabbas had been involved in an insurrection and murder (Mark 15:7). Pilate asked the crowd whether they wanted Barabbas or Jesus, knowing that the leaders had handed Jesus over out of envy.

While Pilate was sitting on the judgment seat, his wife sent him a message: [[v19|Have nothing to do with that righteous man, for I have suffered many things today in a dream because of him]]. Matthew alone records this. Even a Gentile woman recognized Jesus' innocence.

Meanwhile, the chief priests and elders persuaded the crowd to ask for Barabbas and to have Jesus executed. When Pilate asked again, they shouted for Barabbas. When he asked what to do with Jesus, they cried, [[v22|Let him be crucified!]] Pilate asked what evil Jesus had done, but they only shouted louder.

Seeing that a riot was starting, Pilate took water and washed his hands before the crowd, saying, [[v24|I am innocent of the blood of this righteous person. You see to it]]. Hand-washing was a symbolic act declaring innocence (Deuteronomy 21:6–9). But Pilate was not innocent. He had the authority to release Jesus and chose not to, out of fear of unrest. Washing his hands could not remove his responsibility.

The people answered, [[v25|May his blood be on us and on our children!]] This verse has been tragically misused throughout history to blame the Jewish people as a whole for Jesus' death and to justify persecution. Such use is a grave misreading. The words were spoken by a particular crowd in Jerusalem, stirred up by its leaders, on a particular day. The New Testament teaches that Jesus' death was the result of human sin in general, involving Jewish leaders, Roman authorities, His own disciples, and ultimately all people, and that His death happened according to God's plan to save (Acts 4:27–28). Jesus Himself prayed for those who crucified Him (Luke 23:34), and the apostles preached forgiveness to the very people of Jerusalem (Acts 2:36–41). Matthew may also intend a deeper irony: Jesus' blood, which the crowd called down in judgment, is the blood [[Matthew 26:28|poured out for many for the remission of sins]].

Pilate released Barabbas and had Jesus flogged. Roman flogging was brutal, done with whips embedded with pieces of bone or metal, and it sometimes killed prisoners before crucifixion. Then Pilate handed Him over to be crucified.

### The Soldiers Mock Jesus (27:27–31)

The governor's soldiers took Jesus into the Praetorium, the governor's headquarters, and gathered the whole cohort around Him, possibly several hundred soldiers. They stripped Him and put a scarlet robe on Him, likely a soldier's red cloak, imitating a royal purple robe. They twisted a crown out of thorns and put it on His head, put a reed in His right hand as a mock scepter, and knelt before Him, saying, [[v29|Hail, King of the Jews!]] They spat on Him and struck Him on the head with the reed. Then they put His own clothes back on Him and led Him away to be crucified.

The irony is profound. The soldiers mocked Jesus as a false king, but Matthew's readers know He truly is the King. The crown of thorns, meant as a joke, became a symbol of the King who wore the curse of human sin.

### The Crucifixion (27:32–44)

As they went out, they forced a man from Cyrene (in North Africa) named Simon to carry Jesus' cross. Jesus, weakened by the flogging, apparently could not carry the crossbeam all the way. Mark notes that Simon was the father of Alexander and Rufus (Mark 15:21), suggesting that his sons became known in the early church.

They came to Golgotha, which means "Place of a Skull," probably a rocky area just outside the city walls. They offered Jesus wine mixed with gall, a bitter substance, perhaps intended as a mild sedative. Jesus tasted it but refused to drink, choosing to face His suffering with full awareness. The scene recalls Psalm 69:21.

They crucified Him. Crucifixion was a Roman form of execution designed to be slow, painful, and humiliating, reserved for slaves, rebels, and the worst criminals. The victim was nailed or tied to a cross and left to die of exhaustion and suffocation, sometimes over days. Matthew does not describe the physical details; his readers knew them all too well.

The soldiers divided His clothes by casting lots, fulfilling Psalm 22:18. They sat down to keep watch. Above His head they placed the written charge: [[v37|THIS IS JESUS, THE KING OF THE JEWS]]. Two robbers (or rebels) were crucified with Him, one on His right and one on His left, the very places James and John had requested in His kingdom (Matthew 20:21).

People passing by shook their heads and mocked Him, using His own words against Him: [[v40|You who destroy the temple and build it in three days, save yourself! If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross!]] These words echo the devil's temptation in the wilderness: "If you are the Son of God" (Matthew 4:3, 6). The chief priests, scribes, and elders also mocked Him: [[v42|He saved others, but he can’t save himself]]. Their words were unintentionally true: precisely because He was saving others, He would not save Himself. They added, [[v43|He trusts in God. Let God deliver him now, if he wants him]], echoing Psalm 22:8, the psalm of the righteous sufferer. Even the robbers crucified with Him insulted Him.

### The Death of Jesus (27:45–56)

From the sixth hour (noon) until the ninth hour (3 p.m.), darkness covered the land. In Scripture, darkness often signals God's judgment (Exodus 10:21–23; Amos 8:9). About three o'clock, Jesus cried out with a loud voice, [[v46|Eli, Eli, lima sabachthani?]], which means, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" This is studied below.

Some bystanders thought He was calling Elijah, perhaps mishearing "Eli." One ran and offered Him sour wine on a sponge. Others said to wait and see whether Elijah would come to save Him. Then Jesus cried out again with a loud voice and [[v50|yielded up his spirit]]. Matthew's wording suggests that Jesus gave up His life willingly; it was not simply taken from Him.

At that moment, extraordinary things happened. The curtain of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom. The earth shook, rocks split, tombs were opened, and many holy people who had died were raised. Matthew says they came out of the tombs after Jesus' resurrection and appeared to many in Jerusalem. This unusual event, found only in Matthew, signals that Jesus' death and resurrection have broken the power of death. Interpreters have discussed how to understand it, but Matthew presents it as a sign that the resurrection age has begun. The torn curtain and the centurion's confession are studied below.

Many women were watching from a distance, women who had followed Jesus from Galilee and cared for His needs. Among them were Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Joseph, and the mother of Zebedee's sons. While the male disciples had fled, these women remained.

### The Burial (27:57–61)

When evening came, a rich man from Arimathea named Joseph, who had become a disciple of Jesus, went to Pilate and asked for Jesus' body. Normally the bodies of crucified criminals were left on crosses or thrown into common graves. Jewish law required that the body of an executed person be buried before nightfall (Deuteronomy 21:22–23), especially before the Sabbath. Joseph took a risk by publicly identifying with Jesus. Pilate granted his request.

Joseph wrapped the body in clean linen and placed it in his own new tomb cut into the rock. He rolled a large stone in front of the entrance. Isaiah 53:9 says the Servant was buried [[Isaiah 53:9|with a rich man in his death]]. Mary Magdalene and the other Mary sat opposite the tomb, watching. They knew exactly where Jesus was buried.

### The Guard at the Tomb (27:62–66)

The next day, the Sabbath, the chief priests and Pharisees went to Pilate. They remembered that Jesus had said He would rise after three days. Calling Him [[v63|that deceiver]], they asked Pilate to secure the tomb so that His disciples could not steal the body and claim He had risen. Pilate told them to take a guard and make it as secure as they could. They sealed the stone and posted guards. Ironically, their efforts to prevent a fraudulent resurrection claim would provide evidence for the real resurrection: a sealed, guarded tomb that would be found empty.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 27:46 — [[Matthew 27:46|My God, My God, Why Have You Forsaken Me?]]

verse: Matthew 27:46

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records one of the most solemn moments in the Bible. Jesus had been hanging on the cross since about nine in the morning (Mark 15:25). From noon until three in the afternoon, darkness covered the land. Throughout these hours He had been mocked by passersby, religious leaders, soldiers, and even the robbers crucified with Him. Now, around three o'clock, He cried out with a loud voice.

His words were in Aramaic, the everyday language of Jesus and His disciples, with "Eli" ("my God") in Hebrew form: [[v46|Eli, Eli, lima sabachthani?]] Matthew translates them: [[v46|My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?]] These words are the opening line of Psalm 22, a psalm of David that describes a righteous man suffering intense agony and mockery, crying out to God, and then ending in confidence and praise as God delivers him and the nations turn to the Lord.

Matthew has already shown connections between Psalm 22 and the crucifixion. The mockers shook their heads (Psalm 22:7), said [[Matthew 27:43|He trusts in God. Let God deliver him now]] (compare Psalm 22:8), and the soldiers divided His garments by casting lots (Psalm 22:18). Now Jesus Himself prays the psalm's opening words.

The cry expresses real anguish. Jesus, who had always lived in perfect communion with His Father and who addressed Him as "My Father" in Gethsemane, now cries "My God," expressing a profound experience of abandonment. Some bystanders misunderstood, thinking He was calling for Elijah.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Christians have reflected deeply on what this cry means. Many understand it to express the reality that Jesus was bearing the judgment for sin on behalf of others. Paul writes, [[2 Corinthians 5:21|For him who knew no sin he made to be sin on our behalf, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God]]. On the cross, Jesus experienced the separation from God that sin brings, so that those who trust in Him would never be separated from God. The darkness over the land points to this judgment.

At the same time, interpreters caution against concluding that the Trinity was broken or that the Father stopped loving the Son. Jesus still addressed Him as "My God," a cry of faith even in agony. By quoting the beginning of Psalm 22, Jesus may also have been pointing to the whole psalm, which moves from abandonment to vindication: [[Psalm 22:24|neither has he hidden his face from him; but when he cried to him, he heard]]. Jesus' cry is both a real experience of forsakenness and a prayer of trust.

The verse also shows that Jesus fully entered human suffering, including the experience of feeling abandoned by God. Believers who feel forsaken in their own suffering can know that Jesus understands that experience from the inside.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse shows that it is not wrong to bring the deepest anguish to God in honest words. Jesus prayed the words of a psalm of lament, a prayer that asks God "why." Believers who are suffering, grieving, or feeling abandoned can bring their cries to God, using the psalms as Jesus did. Honest lament is an act of faith, because it is addressed to God.

It also gives believers deep assurance. Because Jesus experienced forsakenness on the cross, those who trust in Him will never be forsaken by God. Hebrews quotes God's promise, [[Hebrews 13:5|I will in no way leave you, neither will I in any way forsake you]]. Even when believers feel far from God, they can trust that He has not abandoned them.

Finally, this verse calls believers to gratitude and worship. The cost of their salvation was immense. Jesus endured not only physical pain but spiritual anguish to bring them to God. Remembering this can deepen love for Him and strengthen resolve to follow Him.

### Matthew 27:51 — The Temple Curtain Torn in Two

verse: Matthew 27:51

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes what happened at the very moment Jesus died. He had cried out with a loud voice and yielded up His spirit. Immediately, [[v51|the veil of the temple was torn in two from the top to the bottom]].

The temple in Jerusalem had curtains separating its sacred spaces. The most important curtain separated the Holy Place from the Most Holy Place, the innermost room where God's presence was believed to dwell in a special way. In the original tabernacle, this room held the ark of the covenant (Exodus 26:31–33). Only the high priest could enter it, and only once a year, on the Day of Atonement, bringing the blood of a sacrifice to atone for the sins of the people (Leviticus 16). The curtain was a powerful symbol of the separation between a holy God and sinful people. According to later Jewish tradition, it was very thick and very large.

Matthew says the curtain was torn [[v51|from the top to the bottom]]. This detail suggests that the tearing was God's act, not a human one. No person standing on the ground could tear such a curtain from the top.

Matthew adds that the earth shook and the rocks split. Earthquakes in the Old Testament often accompanied God's appearances and acts of judgment (Exodus 19:18; Psalm 18:7). Jesus' death was a world-shaking event.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The tearing of the curtain shows that Jesus' death opened the way into God's presence. The book of Hebrews explains this meaning: believers can now enter the Most Holy Place [[Hebrews 10:20|by the way which he dedicated for us, a new and living way, through the veil, that is to say, his flesh]]. Jesus' body, torn on the cross, is the true curtain through which people come to God. The barrier of sin has been removed by His sacrifice.

The torn curtain also signals that the temple's sacrificial system had reached its fulfillment. Jesus had predicted the temple's destruction (Matthew 24:2), and He had said that something greater than the temple was here (Matthew 12:6). His death accomplished what all the temple's sacrifices pointed to. Some interpreters also see the torn curtain as a sign of judgment on the temple itself, which would be destroyed about forty years later.

Finally, this event reveals that access to God is now open to everyone who comes through Christ, not only priests, and not only Israel. In the very next verses, a Gentile centurion confesses Jesus as the Son of God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to approach God with confidence. Because the curtain has been torn, they do not need a human priest or a sacrifice to come into God's presence. Hebrews encourages believers, [[Hebrews 4:16|Let’s therefore draw near with boldness to the throne of grace]]. Prayer is a privilege made possible by Jesus' death.

It also reminds believers that their access to God depends entirely on Jesus. They do not come to God on the basis of their own goodness, but through what Christ has done. This brings both humility and assurance.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to welcome others into God's presence. The barrier between God and people has been removed for all who come through Christ. Believers can share this good news with anyone, regardless of background, inviting them to come near to God through Jesus.

### Matthew 27:54 — [[Matthew 27:54|Truly This Was the Son of God]]

verse: Matthew 27:54

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records the reaction of the Roman soldiers who carried out Jesus' crucifixion. A centurion was an officer in charge of about a hundred soldiers. He and his men had been responsible for the execution. They had likely participated in mocking Jesus, dividing His clothing, and keeping watch over Him as He died. To them, He was just another condemned man, a Jewish prisoner accused of claiming to be a king.

But they had seen how Jesus died. They had watched the darkness cover the land, heard His loud cry, and felt the earthquake. Matthew says that when they saw the earthquake and everything that happened, [[v54|were terrified]]. Then they said, [[v54|Truly this was the Son of God!]]

The irony is striking. The religious leaders had mocked Jesus for claiming to be the Son of God (v. 43). The passersby had challenged Him: "If you are the Son of God, come down from the cross!" (v. 40). Jesus did not come down. Yet it was precisely in His death, not by escaping it, that these Gentile soldiers recognized who He was.

For a Roman soldier, "son of a god" might have meant a divine or extraordinary man, a phrase sometimes used for emperors. But Matthew's readers understand that the centurion spoke a greater truth than he may have realized. The Father had declared Jesus His beloved Son at His baptism (Matthew 3:17) and transfiguration (Matthew 17:5). Peter had confessed Him as the Son of the living God (Matthew 16:16). Now, at the cross, Gentile soldiers confess the same.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that the cross reveals who Jesus truly is. Many expected that if Jesus were really the Son of God, He would come down from the cross and prove it by saving Himself. Instead, the cross itself is where His identity as God's Son is most clearly displayed: the Son who obeys the Father to the point of death and gives His life for others.

The confession of the Gentile soldiers also anticipates the spread of the gospel to all nations. At the beginning of Matthew, Gentile magi came to worship the newborn King of the Jews. Now, at the end, Gentile soldiers confess Him as the Son of God. Soon, the risen Jesus will send His disciples to make disciples of all nations (Matthew 28:19).

Finally, this verse shows that no one is beyond the reach of God's revelation. These soldiers had been instruments of Jesus' execution. Yet they came to recognize Him. God can open the eyes of even the most unlikely people.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites readers to look at the cross and recognize who Jesus is. Many people today have ideas about Jesus as a good teacher, a prophet, or a moral example. The cross reveals that He is the Son of God who gave Himself for the world. Believers can reflect on the cross regularly, allowing it to deepen their understanding of Jesus and their love for Him.

It also encourages believers to hope for those who seem far from God. If Roman soldiers who crucified Jesus could confess Him as the Son of God, then anyone can come to faith. Believers can keep praying for and sharing Christ with people who seem hostile or indifferent.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that God's power is often revealed in what looks like weakness. The world expected strength to come by escaping the cross. God revealed His Son through the cross. Believers can trust that God works through humility, sacrifice, and suffering in ways that the world does not expect.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 27 invites readers to stand at the foot of the cross and consider their own response to Jesus. The chapter presents many responses: Judas's despair, the leaders' hatred, Pilate's cowardice, the crowd's manipulation, the soldiers' mockery, the bystanders' taunts, the centurion's confession, Joseph's courage, and the women's faithful presence. Every reader can ask which of these responses reflects their own heart and choose the path of confession, courage, and faithfulness.

The contrast between Judas and Peter is especially important. Both failed Jesus seriously. Judas felt remorse but went to the religious leaders, who offered no help, and he died in despair. Peter wept bitterly but was later restored by the risen Jesus. Believers who have failed can learn from this: remorse alone is not enough, but turning back to Jesus brings forgiveness and restoration.

Pilate's story warns against trying to avoid responsibility. He knew Jesus was innocent, but he gave in to pressure and tried to wash his hands of the decision. Believers face many situations where it is tempting to go along with the crowd or avoid taking a stand for what is right. Washing one's hands does not remove responsibility. Integrity requires courage.

The mockery at the cross reveals that Jesus chose not to save Himself so that He could save others. Believers can respond with gratitude and worship, remembering the cost of their salvation. They can also bring their own suffering, abandonment, and grief to God, as Jesus did in the words of Psalm 22, trusting that He hears.

Finally, the torn curtain and the centurion's confession show the results of Jesus' death: open access to God for all who come through Christ, and a gospel that reaches all nations. Believers can approach God with confidence, share the good news with anyone, and follow the example of Joseph and the women, who stayed faithful when others fled.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, I stand at the foot of Your cross in awe and gratitude. You were betrayed, condemned though innocent, mocked as a king, and crucified between robbers. You chose not to save Yourself so that You could save me. Thank You for bearing the darkness and the forsakenness I deserved, so that I will never be forsaken.

Forgive me for the times I have acted like Pilate, avoiding responsibility, or like the crowd, going along with what is wrong. When I fail, keep me from despair and draw me back to You. Thank You for tearing the curtain and opening the way into God's presence. Help me to draw near with confidence. Like the centurion, I confess that You are truly the Son of God. Give me the courage of Joseph and the faithfulness of the women who stayed near You. Help me to live in grateful love for the King who wore a crown of thorns for me. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
