# Romans 9
theme: God's Faithful Word, Sovereign Mercy, and Paul's Grief for Israel
main: Paul expresses deep and unceasing sorrow for his fellow Israelites, who have received great privileges from God, including the Messiah Himself, yet many have not believed. He insists that God's word has not failed, because God's promises have always worked through His choice and calling, not merely physical descent, as seen with Isaac and Jacob. God is not unjust; He shows mercy as He chooses, like a potter with clay, and He has called a people from both Jews and Gentiles, as the prophets foretold. Paul concludes that Gentiles have obtained righteousness by faith, while many in Israel stumbled because they pursued it by works rather than by faith in the stone God laid in Zion.

## Chapter Background and Story

Romans 9–11 forms a distinct section of the letter. Paul has just ended chapter 8 with the triumphant declaration that nothing can separate believers from God's love. But a serious question arises: if God's promises are so secure, what about Israel? God made great promises to Abraham's descendants, yet most of Paul's fellow Jews had not accepted Jesus as the Messiah. Had God's word failed? Could Gentile believers trust God's promises if He had apparently abandoned Israel?

In these three chapters, Paul wrestles with this question. Chapter 9 emphasizes God's sovereign freedom and faithfulness. Chapter 10 emphasizes human responsibility to believe. Chapter 11 shows that God has not rejected Israel and that His plan includes a future for them. The section ends with a hymn of praise to God's wisdom (11:33–36). These chapters must be read together.

These chapters have been the subject of intense discussion throughout church history, especially regarding election and predestination. They have also sometimes been misused to justify hostility toward Jewish people, which is a grievous distortion. Paul writes with tears for his own people and affirms God's continuing love for them (11:28–29).

### Paul's Sorrow for Israel (9:1–5)

Paul begins with a solemn oath of sincerity and a statement of grief, studied below. He then lists Israel's privileges: [[v4|who are Israelites; whose is the adoption, the glory, the covenants, the giving of the law, the service, and the promises]]. Israel was adopted as God's son (Exodus 4:22). God's glory dwelt among them in the tabernacle and temple. They received the covenants with Abraham, Moses, and David; the Law at Sinai; the worship of the temple; and the promises of the prophets. The climax of this list is studied below.

### God's Word Has Not Failed (9:6–13)

[[v6|But it is not as though the word of God has come to nothing. For they are not all Israel that are of Israel]]. Paul explains that God's promises were never based merely on physical descent. Not all of Abraham's physical descendants were children of the promise. Abraham had two sons, Ishmael and Isaac, but God said, [[v7|your offspring will be accounted as from Isaac]] (Genesis 21:12). [[v8|That is, it is not the children of the flesh who are children of God, but the children of the promise are counted as heirs]].

Paul then gives a second example: Rebekah's twins, Jacob and Esau. [[v11|For being not yet born, neither having done anything good or bad, that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him who calls]], [[v12|it was said to her, “The elder will serve the younger.”]] (Genesis 25:23). God's choice did not depend on their works. [[v13|Even as it is written, “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.”]] (Malachi 1:2–3).

The phrase "Esau I hated" has troubled many readers. In Hebrew idiom, "love" and "hate" in such comparisons often mean "chose" and "did not choose," or "loved more" and "loved less" (compare Genesis 29:30–31; Luke 14:26). In Malachi, the words refer to the nations descended from Jacob (Israel) and Esau (Edom). God chose Israel to carry His covenant purpose; He did not choose Edom for that role. The point is that God's choice is free and based on His purpose, not on human merit.

### Is God Unjust? (9:14–29)

[[v14|What shall we say then? Is there unrighteousness with God? May it never be!]] Paul quotes God's words to Moses, studied below.

Paul gives the example of Pharaoh: [[v17|For this very purpose I caused you to be raised up, that I might show in you my power, and that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth]] (Exodus 9:16). [[v18|So then, he has mercy on whom he desires, and he hardens whom he desires]]. In Exodus, Pharaoh hardened his own heart, and God also hardened it (Exodus 8:15; 9:12). God's hardening confirmed Pharaoh's own stubborn choices.

Paul anticipates an objection: [[v19|Why does he still find fault? For who withstands his will?]] If God hardens people, how can He blame them? Paul responds with the image of the potter and the clay, drawn from Isaiah 29:16, 45:9, and Jeremiah 18:1–6: [[v20|But indeed, O man, who are you to reply against God? Will the thing formed ask him who formed it, “Why did you make me like this?”]] [[v21|Or hasn’t the potter a right over the clay, from the same lump to make one part a vessel for honor, and another for dishonor?]] Paul does not fully answer the philosophical question. Instead, he reminds his readers that God is the Creator and humans are creatures. Humility is required before the mystery of God's ways. In Jeremiah 18, the potter reshapes the clay in response to Israel's repentance or rebellion, showing that God's sovereignty works together with His responsiveness to human choices.

Paul then speaks of God's patience: [[v22|What if God, willing to show his wrath and to make his power known, endured with much patience vessels of wrath prepared for destruction]], [[v23|and that he might make known the riches of his glory on vessels of mercy, which he prepared beforehand for glory]]? God patiently endures those who oppose Him, giving time for repentance (2:4). And He shows the riches of His glory to those on whom He has mercy, [[v24|us, whom he also called, not from the Jews only, but also from the Gentiles]].

Christians have understood this passage in different ways. Many in the Reformed tradition see it as teaching that God sovereignly chooses individuals for salvation, and that this choice rests on His mercy alone. Many in Wesleyan, Arminian, Catholic, and Orthodox traditions see Paul speaking mainly about God's choice of peoples and roles in His plan, such as Israel and the church, and emphasize that God desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4) and that people are responsible to believe, as chapter 10 makes clear. All agree that salvation is by God's mercy, not human merit, and that God is just.

Paul quotes Hosea to show that God has called Gentiles to be His people: [[v25|I will call them ‘my people,’ which were not my people; and her ‘beloved,’ who was not beloved.]] And he quotes Isaiah to show that only a remnant of Israel would be saved at that time: [[v27|If the number of the children of Israel are as the sand of the sea, it is the remnant who will be saved]].

## Important Verses Explained

### Romans 9:2–3 — Great Sorrow and Unceasing Pain

verse: Romans 9:2-3

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had just finished Romans 8 with a triumphant declaration: nothing can separate believers from the love of God in Christ. One might expect Paul to continue in joy. Instead, he suddenly expresses deep grief.

He begins with a solemn affirmation: [[v1|I tell the truth in Christ. I am not lying, my conscience testifying with me in the Holy Spirit]]. Paul wants his readers to know that what he is about to say is completely sincere. Some might have thought that Paul, the apostle to the Gentiles, had turned his back on his own people. He insists that this is not so.

[[v2|that I have great sorrow and unceasing pain in my heart]]. Paul's grief is "great" and "unceasing." It is not a passing sadness but a constant ache.

Why? [[v3|For I could wish that I myself were accursed from Christ for my brothers’ sake, my relatives according to the flesh]]. Paul says he could almost wish to be "accursed," cut off from Christ, if it would save his fellow Jews. This is an astonishing statement. Paul has just said that nothing can separate believers from Christ's love. Yet he loves his people so much that he would be willing to give up his own salvation for them, if that were possible.

Paul's words echo Moses, who prayed when Israel sinned with the golden calf: [[Exodus 32:32|Yet now, if you will, forgive their sin—and if not, please blot me out of your book which you have written]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal the heart of a true servant of God. Paul's theology of God's sovereignty did not make him cold or indifferent to the lost. Instead, it was joined with deep compassion and grief. Belief in God's sovereign purposes should never make believers careless about those who do not know Christ.

They also reflect the heart of Christ. Jesus wept over Jerusalem because its people did not recognize Him (Luke 19:41–44). And Jesus did what Paul could only wish: He became a curse for others, bearing God's judgment so that they could be saved (Galatians 3:13).

Finally, these verses show Paul's love for the Jewish people. Paul never stopped loving his own people. Any use of Romans to justify hostility toward Jews contradicts Paul's own heart. He prayed for their salvation (10:1) and believed that God had not rejected them (11:1–2).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers to care deeply about those who do not know Christ. It is easy to become comfortable in one's own salvation and forget about others. Paul's grief invites believers to pray for family members, friends, and neighbors who do not yet believe.

They also encourage believers to love people even when they reject the gospel. Paul did not become bitter toward those who opposed him. He loved them and longed for their salvation.

Finally, these verses call believers to reject every form of antisemitism. Paul loved his Jewish people deeply. Christians are called to honor the Jewish people, from whom the Messiah came, and to treat them with respect and love.

### Romans 9:5 — Christ Who Is Over All, God Blessed Forever

verse: Romans 9:5

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was listing the privileges God had given Israel: adoption as God's children, the glory of God's presence, the covenants, the Law, the temple worship, and the promises. Now he names the greatest privileges of all.

[[v5|of whom are the fathers]]. The "fathers" are the patriarchs: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Israel descended from these men, to whom God made His great promises.

[[v5|and from whom is Christ as concerning the flesh]]. The Messiah Himself came from Israel. Jesus was born a Jew, a descendant of Abraham and David. "As concerning the flesh" refers to His human ancestry. As a man, Jesus was an Israelite.

[[v5|who is over all, God, blessed forever. Amen]]. This phrase is one of the clearest statements in Paul's letters that Christ is God. Grammatically, the most natural reading is that Christ "is over all, God, blessed forever." Some translations punctuate the verse differently, making the last part a separate praise to God the Father, but the World English Bible and most translations follow the reading that applies these words to Christ. The early church fathers widely read it this way.

Paul's point is that Israel's greatest privilege is that the Messiah, who is God Himself, came from their people. This makes their unbelief all the more painful to Paul.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse affirms both the humanity and the divinity of Christ. He came from Israel "according to the flesh," fully human. And He is "over all, God, blessed forever," fully divine. This truth is at the heart of Christian faith. John writes, [[John 1:14|The Word became flesh, and lived among us]].

It also shows the honor God gave to Israel. The Savior of the world came from the Jewish people. Jesus said to the Samaritan woman, [[John 4:22|for salvation is from the Jews]]. Christians owe a great debt to Israel.

Finally, the verse leads to worship. Paul cannot speak of Christ without praising Him: "blessed forever. Amen." Theology should always lead to doxology.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to worship Jesus as God. He is not merely a great teacher or prophet. He is "over all, God, blessed forever." Believers can bring Him the worship and trust that belong to God alone.

It also encourages believers to appreciate the Jewish roots of their faith. Jesus was Jewish. The Scriptures, the covenants, and the promises came through Israel. Believers can study the Old Testament and honor the heritage they have received.

Finally, the verse reminds believers that true theology leads to praise. When believers learn more about who Jesus is, their response should be worship.

### Romans 9:15–16 — Mercy on Whom He Has Mercy

verse: Romans 9:15-16

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had just explained that God chose Jacob over Esau before they were born, before they had done anything good or bad, so that His purpose would stand, based not on works but on His calling. This raised an obvious question: [[v14|Is there unrighteousness with God?]] Is it fair for God to choose one person over another?

Paul's answer is emphatic: [[v14|May it never be!]] God is never unjust.

Then Paul quotes God's words to Moses: [[v15|For he said to Moses, “I will have mercy on whom I have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I have compassion.”]] These words come from Exodus 33:19. Moses had asked to see God's glory after Israel sinned with the golden calf. God responded by revealing His goodness and proclaiming His name, including His freedom to show mercy. Israel did not deserve mercy; they had just worshiped an idol. Yet God chose to show compassion.

[[v16|So then it is not of him who wills, nor of him who runs, but of God who has mercy]]. Salvation does not depend on human desire ("him who wills") or human effort ("him who runs"). It depends on God's mercy.

The key word is "mercy." Mercy, by definition, is not deserved. If anyone deserved God's favor, it would not be mercy. God is not unjust when He shows mercy, because no one has a claim on it. Justice would mean everyone receiving what they deserve. Mercy means some receiving what they do not deserve.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that salvation is entirely God's gracious work. No one can earn it or demand it. Paul wrote earlier that all have sinned (3:23). If God gave everyone what they deserved, no one would be saved. That anyone is saved is a display of mercy.

They also show God's freedom. God is not obligated to show mercy to anyone. When He does, it is a free gift. This truth humbles believers, because their salvation is not based on anything in themselves.

As discussed in the chapter background, Christians differ on how God's sovereign mercy relates to human choice. Some emphasize God's choice of individuals; others emphasize God's mercy offered to all and the human responsibility to respond. Paul himself holds both truths together: in chapter 9 he emphasizes God's sovereignty, and in chapter 10 he emphasizes that "whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved" (10:13).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses humble believers. Their salvation is not the result of their own goodness, wisdom, or effort. It is the result of God's mercy. This truth leaves no room for pride.

They also fill believers with gratitude. God did not have to show them mercy, but He did. Believers can thank Him daily for His undeserved kindness.

Finally, these verses give hope. Since salvation depends on God's mercy rather than human effort, no one is too sinful to be saved. Believers can pray for God's mercy on those who seem far from Him.

### Romans 9:30–32 — Righteousness by Faith, Not by Works

verse: Romans 9:30-32

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been explaining God's sovereign choice and mercy. Now he draws a conclusion that highlights human responsibility as well.

[[v30|What shall we say then? That the Gentiles, who didn’t follow after righteousness, attained to righteousness, even the righteousness which is of faith]]. The Gentiles had not been seeking a right relationship with God through the Law. They had been worshiping idols and living without God's revelation. Yet many of them obtained righteousness, the righteousness that comes by faith in Christ.

[[v31|but Israel, following after a law of righteousness, didn’t arrive at the law of righteousness]]. Many in Israel had been earnestly pursuing righteousness through the Law. They were zealous for God. Yet they did not reach their goal.

[[v32|Why? Because they didn’t seek it by faith, but as it were by works of the law]]. The problem was not their zeal but their method. They tried to become righteous by keeping the Law, rather than by trusting God. Righteousness cannot be earned; it must be received by faith.

[[v32|They stumbled over the stumbling stone]]. The "stumbling stone" is Christ. Paul quotes Isaiah 8:14 and 28:16 in the next verse: [[v33|Behold, I lay in Zion a stumbling stone and a rock of offense; and no one who believes in him will be disappointed]]. Jesus, the crucified Messiah, was a stumbling block to many in Israel (1 Corinthians 1:23). They expected a Messiah who would vindicate their righteousness, not one who died for sinners. But those who trust in Him will never be put to shame.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that human responsibility is real. Chapter 9 emphasizes God's sovereignty, but here Paul makes clear that Israel stumbled "because they didn't seek it by faith." People are responsible for how they respond to God. Paul continues this theme in chapter 10.

They also show the surprising nature of grace. Those who were not seeking God found Him, while those who were seeking Him in the wrong way did not. Jesus told parables with the same theme: the tax collector went home justified rather than the Pharisee (Luke 18:9–14).

Finally, these verses show that Christ is either a foundation or a stumbling stone. For those who trust Him, He is a secure foundation. For those who reject Him, He becomes a stone of stumbling. Peter writes, [[1 Peter 2:7|For you who believe therefore is the honor, but for those who are disobedient]], He is a stone of stumbling.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses warn believers against trying to earn God's favor. Even sincere, zealous people can miss God if they rely on their own works rather than faith in Christ. Believers can examine whether they are trusting in their own goodness or in Christ alone.

They also encourage believers to share the gospel with those who seem far from God. The Gentiles were not seeking righteousness, yet they found it. People who seem uninterested in God may be among those who respond to the gospel.

Finally, these verses give assurance. "No one who believes in him will be disappointed." Those who trust in Christ will never be put to shame. Believers can rest securely on Him as their foundation.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Romans 9 begins with Paul's deep sorrow for his fellow Israelites. His understanding of God's sovereignty did not make him indifferent; it was joined with tears and longing for their salvation. Believers can ask God for a similar compassion for those who do not yet know Christ, and they can firmly reject any attitude of contempt toward the Jewish people, from whom the Messiah came.

The chapter affirms that God's word has not failed. God's promises have always worked through His choice and calling, not merely through human descent or effort. Believers can trust that God is faithful to His promises, even when His ways are hard to understand.

Romans 9 emphasizes God's sovereign mercy. Salvation depends not on human desire or effort but on God who shows mercy. Believers can respond with humility, recognizing that their salvation is entirely a gift, and with gratitude for mercy they did not deserve. Where the chapter raises difficult questions about God's choices, believers can approach them with humility before the Creator, remembering that Christians have understood these passages in different ways while agreeing that God is both just and merciful.

Finally, the chapter shows that righteousness is received by faith, not earned by works. Many who pursued righteousness through the Law stumbled over Christ, while many who were not seeking God found righteousness through faith. Believers can trust in Christ as their sure foundation, knowing that no one who believes in Him will be put to shame, and they can share this good news with everyone.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord God, You are faithful, and Your word never fails. Thank You for the privileges You gave to Israel, and for sending the Messiah through them, Jesus Christ, who is over all, God blessed forever. Give me a heart like Paul's, full of love and longing for those who do not yet know You. Guard me from any contempt toward the Jewish people, and help me to honor the heritage of faith I have received through them. Thank You that my salvation depends on Your mercy, not on my own desire or effort. Keep me humble and grateful. When I struggle to understand Your ways, help me to trust You as the wise and good Potter. Thank You that no one who believes in Jesus will be put to shame. Make Him my sure foundation. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
