# Matthew 21
theme: The King Comes to His City and His Temple
main: Jesus enters Jerusalem as the humble King foretold by Zechariah, welcomed by crowds shouting "Hosanna." He clears the temple of trade, heals the blind and lame there, and curses a fruitless fig tree as a sign of judgment on empty religion. When the religious leaders challenge His authority, He answers with parables showing that genuine obedience, not outward profession, marks those who belong to God, and that rejecting God's Son brings serious consequences.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 21 begins the final week of Jesus' earthly life, often called Holy Week or Passion Week. Chapters 21–28 cover only about a week, yet they make up nearly a third of Matthew's Gospel, showing how central these events are. Jesus has traveled from Galilee through Perea and Jericho, and now He approaches Jerusalem for the Passover festival. The city was crowded with pilgrims, possibly hundreds of thousands, and expectations were high. Many hoped the Messiah would appear and deliver Israel from Roman rule.

Jesus' actions in this chapter are deliberate and symbolic. He enters the city in a way that fulfills prophecy, He acts with authority in the temple, and He confronts the religious leaders. Each action raises the same question the crowd asks in verse 10: [[v10|Who is this?]]

### The Triumphal Entry (21:1–11)

As Jesus and His disciples approached Jerusalem, they came to Bethphage on the Mount of Olives, east of the city. Jesus sent two disciples into the village to bring a donkey and her colt, telling them that if anyone asked, they should say, [[v3|The Lord needs them]]. This may reflect prior arrangements or Jesus' supernatural knowledge; either way, Jesus is directing events.

Matthew explains that this fulfilled the prophecy of Zechariah 9:9 (combined with a phrase from Isaiah 62:11): [[v5|Tell the daughter of Zion, behold, your King comes to you, humble, and riding on a donkey, on a colt, the foal of a donkey]]. Conquering kings usually rode war horses; a king coming in peace might ride a donkey. Zechariah's prophecy continues by describing a king who brings peace to the nations. Jesus was claiming to be Israel's King, but a humble King of peace, not a military conqueror. Matthew mentions both the donkey and the colt; the young colt may have been more willing to be ridden with its mother nearby.

The disciples put their cloaks on the animals, and Jesus sat on them. A very large crowd spread their cloaks on the road, an act of honor for a king (as in 2 Kings 9:13), and others cut branches from trees and spread them on the road. John tells us these were palm branches (John 12:13), a symbol of Jewish national victory. The crowds shouted, [[v9|Hosanna to the son of David! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Hosanna in the highest!]] These words come from Psalm 118, one of the psalms sung by pilgrims at Passover.

When Jesus entered the city, all Jerusalem was stirred, asking, "Who is this?" The crowds answered, [[v11|This is the prophet, Jesus, from Nazareth of Galilee]]. Their answer was true but incomplete. Within a week, many in the city would call for His crucifixion.

### Cleansing the Temple (21:12–17)

Jesus entered the temple courts and drove out those who were buying and selling. The temple complex was huge, and its outer area, the Court of the Gentiles, was the only place where non-Jews could come to worship. Here merchants sold animals for sacrifice, and money changers exchanged foreign currency for the Tyrian shekels required for the temple tax. These services had a practical purpose for pilgrims who traveled long distances. But the trade had filled the space intended for prayer, and there is evidence that some of these businesses, connected with the high priestly families, overcharged worshipers.

Jesus overturned the tables of the money changers and the seats of those selling doves. Doves were the sacrifice offered by the poor (Leviticus 12:8), so those exploiting the trade in doves were taking advantage of the most vulnerable worshipers. Jesus quoted Isaiah 56:7, [[v13|My house shall be called a house of prayer]], and Jeremiah 7:11, saying they had made it [[v13|a den of robbers]]. This action is studied in detail below.

Then the blind and the lame came to Jesus in the temple, and He healed them. According to 2 Samuel 5:8, a saying arose that the blind and the lame would not enter the house. Jesus welcomed them. Children were shouting in the temple courts, [[v15|Hosanna to the son of David!]] The chief priests and scribes were indignant and asked Jesus whether He heard what the children were saying. Jesus answered by quoting Psalm 8:2: [[v16|Out of the mouth of children and nursing babies, you have perfected praise]]. In that psalm, the praise is directed to God. Jesus was accepting praise that belongs to God. He then left the city and spent the night in Bethany, where His friends Mary, Martha, and Lazarus lived.

### The Fig Tree (21:18–22)

The next morning, as Jesus returned to the city, He was hungry. He saw a fig tree by the road with leaves but no fruit. Fig trees in that region often produced small early fruit along with their leaves, so a tree in full leaf suggested there should be something to eat. Finding nothing, Jesus said, [[v19|Let there be no fruit from you forever!]] and the tree withered immediately.

This was an acted parable. In the Old Testament, the fig tree sometimes symbolized Israel (Jeremiah 8:13; Hosea 9:10; Micah 7:1). The tree had the appearance of life but no fruit, like the temple worship Jesus had just confronted: busy and impressive but lacking the fruit of genuine faithfulness. Jesus' action warned of judgment on a religion that looks alive but produces nothing for God.

The disciples were amazed. Jesus told them that if they had faith and did not doubt, they could do even greater things, such as telling a mountain to be thrown into the sea. The "mountain" may have been a reference to the Mount of Olives or the temple mount itself. He added, [[v22|All things, whatever you ask in prayer, believing, you will receive]]. This promise is about prayer that trusts God fully, and it must be understood alongside Jesus' other teaching that prayer seeks God's will (Matthew 6:10; 26:39). It is not a formula to obtain anything a person wants, but an encouragement to pray with confident dependence on God.

### The Question of Authority (21:23–27)

When Jesus returned to the temple and began teaching, the chief priests and elders challenged Him: [[v23|By what authority do you do these things? Who gave you this authority?]] They were the official authorities over the temple, and Jesus had acted without their permission.

Jesus answered with a question: was John's baptism from heaven or from human origin? The leaders discussed it among themselves. If they said "from heaven," Jesus would ask why they had not believed John. If they said "from men," they feared the crowd, who considered John a prophet. So they answered, [[v27|We don’t know]]. Jesus replied that He would not tell them by what authority He acted. His question was not a dodge. John had pointed to Jesus as the Coming One. If they had accepted John's authority, they would have known the answer to their own question.

### The Parable of the Two Sons (21:28–32)

Jesus then told a parable. A man had two sons. He told the first to go work in the vineyard. The son said, "I will not," but later changed his mind and went. The father told the second son the same thing. That son said, "I'm going, sir," but did not go. Jesus asked which son did his father's will. The leaders answered, "The first."

Jesus applied the parable directly: [[v31|the tax collectors and the prostitutes are entering into God’s Kingdom before you]]. Tax collectors and prostitutes had openly lived in disobedience, like the first son's initial refusal, but many had repented when John preached and believed him. The religious leaders had professed obedience to God, like the second son, but had not repented or believed. This parable is studied below.

### The Parable of the Tenants (21:33–46)

Jesus told another parable. A landowner planted a vineyard, put a wall around it, dug a winepress, and built a watchtower, details that echo Isaiah 5:1–7, where the vineyard represents Israel. He leased it to tenant farmers and went away. At harvest time, he sent servants to collect his share of the fruit. The tenants beat one, killed another, and stoned another. He sent more servants, and they did the same. Finally he sent his son, thinking, [[v37|They will respect my son]]. But the tenants said, [[v38|This is the heir. Come, let’s kill him and seize his inheritance]]. They threw him out of the vineyard and killed him.

Jesus asked what the owner would do. The leaders answered that he would destroy those evil men and lease the vineyard to others who would give him the fruit. Jesus then quoted Psalm 118:22–23: [[v42|The stone which the builders rejected was made the head of the corner]]. The "builders" were Israel's leaders; the rejected stone was Jesus, who would become the cornerstone of God's new work. He declared that God's kingdom would be taken away from them and given to [[v43|a nation producing its fruit]]. Anyone who falls on this stone will be broken, and anyone on whom it falls will be crushed.

The meaning is clear in context. The servants represent the prophets God sent to Israel, many of whom were rejected and persecuted. The son represents Jesus Himself. The tenants are the religious leaders, who would soon kill Him. The "nation producing its fruit" refers to the community of all who follow Jesus, both Jews and Gentiles. This parable was directed at the leaders who rejected Jesus, not at the Jewish people as a whole; Jesus' first followers were Jews. Christians have sometimes misused passages like this to justify contempt for Jewish people, which is a grave distortion of the text.

The chief priests and Pharisees realized He was talking about them. They wanted to arrest Him, but they feared the crowds, who considered Him a prophet.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 21:9 — "Hosanna to the Son of David!"

verse: Matthew 21:9

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records the shouts of the crowds as Jesus rode into Jerusalem on a donkey. Thousands of pilgrims were traveling to the city for Passover, the festival celebrating God's deliverance of Israel from Egypt. Many had followed Jesus from Galilee and had seen His miracles, including the healing of two blind men at Jericho just before. As Jesus rode toward the city, the crowd in front and the crowd behind Him began to shout.

"Hosanna" is a Hebrew expression meaning "Save, please!" or "Save now!" It comes from Psalm 118:25. Over time, it had also become a shout of praise and celebration. The crowd shouted, "Hosanna to the son of David!" acknowledging Jesus as the Messiah, the heir of King David who would restore Israel's kingdom.

[[v9|Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!]] comes from Psalm 118:26. This psalm was sung by pilgrims as they approached Jerusalem and the temple, especially at Passover. Originally it may have been a blessing on the king or on worshipers entering the temple. Now the crowd applied it to Jesus. "In the name of the Lord" means coming with God's authority, as His representative.

[[v9|Hosanna in the highest!]] means something like "Save us, you who live in the highest heaven!" or "Praise in the highest!"

The crowd's welcome expressed genuine hope that Jesus was the Messiah. But many likely expected Him to lead a political revolution against Rome. When Jesus did not do that, many turned against Him. Within days, a crowd in Jerusalem would be shouting, "Let him be crucified!" (Matthew 27:22–23).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows Jesus publicly accepting the title of Messiah. For most of His ministry, He had avoided public declarations about His identity. Now, at the end, He deliberately fulfilled Zechariah's prophecy and accepted the crowd's praise. He was presenting Himself to Jerusalem as its King.

But Jesus was a different kind of king than many expected. He came humble, riding a donkey, not a war horse. He came to save, but not primarily from Rome. The crowd's cry, "Save now!", would be answered in a way they did not expect: through His death on the cross, where He would save His people from their sins (Matthew 1:21).

Christians have long used this verse in worship, especially on Palm Sunday, the Sunday before Easter. It reminds believers that Jesus is the King who comes in God's name, and it invites them to welcome Him with praise. At the same time, it is a warning about the fickleness of enthusiasm. Praise that depends on Jesus fulfilling our expectations can quickly turn into rejection when He does not.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to welcome Jesus as King. Praising Him in worship is fitting and good. But true welcome goes beyond enthusiasm; it means accepting Him as the King He truly is, not the king we want Him to be.

It also calls believers to examine their expectations of Jesus. Many people follow Jesus hoping He will solve their problems in a particular way, bring them success, or align with their political views. When He does not meet those expectations, they may become disillusioned. Jesus came to save from sin and to establish God's kingdom in His own way. Believers can trust Him even when His ways differ from their hopes.

Finally, the cry "Hosanna" ("Save now!") can be a genuine prayer for believers. In times of need, they can call on Jesus as the Savior who came in God's name. He hears that cry, and He has already accomplished the greatest salvation through the cross.

### Matthew 21:13 — A House of Prayer

verse: Matthew 21:13

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words after driving out the merchants and money changers from the temple courts. The temple in Jerusalem was the center of Jewish worship, the place where God's presence was believed to dwell in a special way. Its outer court, the Court of the Gentiles, was the only area where non-Jews could come to pray to the God of Israel.

During Passover, this court was filled with people selling animals for sacrifice and changing money for the temple tax. Pilgrims needed animals that met the law's requirements, and they needed the correct currency. But the trade had taken over the space meant for prayer, and some merchants exploited worshipers by charging excessive prices. Those selling doves, the sacrifice of the poor, were especially taking advantage of vulnerable people.

Jesus overturned their tables and drove them out. Then He quoted two passages from the prophets. The first was Isaiah 56:7: [[v13|My house shall be called a house of prayer]]. In Isaiah, the full verse says the temple would be a house of prayer [[Isaiah 56:7|for all peoples]]. God intended His house to be a place where all nations could come to pray. By filling the Court of the Gentiles with commerce, the temple authorities were blocking the very people God wanted to welcome.

The second quotation came from Jeremiah 7:11: [[v13|but you have made it a den of robbers]]. In Jeremiah's time, the people of Judah practiced injustice and idolatry during the week and then came to the temple thinking they were safe because of it. Jeremiah warned that the temple had become like a cave where robbers hide after committing their crimes. God would judge it, and the first temple was destroyed by Babylon in 586 B.C. Jesus' words carried the same warning.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals God's purpose for worship: a place where people meet Him in prayer, open to all nations. It also reveals His anger at worship that has been corrupted by greed and exploitation. Religious activity can become a cover for injustice, and God takes this very seriously.

Jesus' action also showed His authority over the temple. As the Son of God, He had the right to cleanse His Father's house. This act anticipated His later teaching that He Himself would replace the temple as the place where people meet God (John 2:19–21). After His death and resurrection, the New Testament teaches that believers, individually and together, become God's temple through the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 3:16; 6:19). The temple in Jerusalem was destroyed by the Romans in A.D. 70, about forty years after Jesus' warning.

Finally, Jesus' cleansing of the temple shows that zeal for God can include righteous anger against injustice. Jesus was not out of control; His actions were deliberate and purposeful. His anger was directed at the exploitation of worshipers and the obstruction of prayer.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls churches and believers to keep worship centered on prayer and on God Himself. Churches can examine whether their activities, programs, or finances have crowded out prayer and genuine encounter with God. Worship should be a place where people meet God, not a place driven by commercial interests or personal gain.

It also calls for concern for outsiders. The Court of the Gentiles was meant for those outside Israel. Churches can ask whether they welcome outsiders, newcomers, the poor, and people from other cultures, or whether their practices make it hard for such people to come and worship.

Finally, this verse warns against using religion as a cover for wrongdoing. Believers can examine whether they practice dishonesty, exploitation, or injustice while maintaining an appearance of devotion. Jesus' words call for integrity: worship and daily life must match. Since believers are now God's temple, they can also ask whether their own lives are "houses of prayer," places where God is honored rather than spaces filled with other priorities.

### Matthew 21:31 — Doing the Father's Will

verse: Matthew 21:31

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is Jesus' application of the parable of the two sons. He told this parable to the chief priests and elders after they challenged His authority in the temple. They had refused to say whether John the Baptist's ministry came from God, because either answer would expose them.

In the parable, a father asked his first son to work in the vineyard. The son refused but later changed his mind and went. The father asked the second son, who said, "I'm going, sir," but never went. Jesus asked, [[v31|Which of the two did the will of his father?]] The leaders answered correctly: "The first." Words without action are worthless; action after repentance is what matters.

Then Jesus applied it: [[v31|the tax collectors and the prostitutes are entering into God’s Kingdom before you]]. Tax collectors were considered traitors and thieves; prostitutes were viewed as the most morally compromised members of society. Both groups had openly said "no" to God by their lifestyles. But when John the Baptist came preaching repentance, many of them believed him and changed. Like the first son, they had refused at first but then obeyed.

The religious leaders were like the second son. They said "yes" to God by their religious profession, careful observance, and positions of leadership. But when God sent John, they did not believe him. Even after seeing tax collectors and prostitutes repent, they still did not change their minds (v. 32).

The phrase "entering into God's Kingdom before you" may mean "instead of you" or "ahead of you." Either way, it was a shocking statement to religious leaders who assumed they were closest to God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that God values genuine repentance and obedience over outward religious profession. Saying the right words, holding the right positions, or maintaining a respectable reputation does not make someone right with God. What matters is responding to God's call with a changed heart and life. This echoes Jesus' warning in Matthew 7:21 that not everyone who says "Lord, Lord" will enter the kingdom, but only those who do the Father's will.

It also shows the hope available to people with the worst reputations. Those whom society, and religious people, had written off were entering the kingdom. No past is too dark for God's grace when someone truly repents. Throughout the Gospels, Jesus welcomed tax collectors and sinners, and many of them responded with faith.

At the same time, the verse is a warning to the religious. Familiarity with God's things can harden the heart. The religious leaders had seen God's work through John and through Jesus, and they still refused to repent. The greatest danger is not past sin but present refusal to turn to God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to examine whether their actions match their words. It is possible to sing, pray, and speak about obeying God while ignoring what He has clearly asked. Believers can ask where they have said "yes" to God without following through, whether in forgiving someone, serving others, giving generously, or changing a sinful habit.

It also encourages those who have said "no" to God in the past. Whatever someone's history, they can still change their mind and obey. God welcomes those who repent, regardless of how long they have resisted.

Finally, this verse warns against spiritual pride. Believers can avoid looking down on people whose lives seem far from God, remembering that such people may respond to God's grace more readily than those who are comfortable in their religion. Humility, repentance, and obedience are the marks of those who truly do the Father's will.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 21 presents Jesus as the King who comes to His city and His temple, and it calls every reader to respond to Him. The crowds welcomed Him with praise but did not understand the kind of King He was. Believers can welcome Jesus with genuine worship while letting Him define His kingship. He is the humble King who brings peace and who saves through the cross, not a king who exists to fulfill our political or personal agendas.

The cleansing of the temple challenges the way worship and money mix. Jesus was angered that a place of prayer for all nations had become a marketplace that exploited the poor. Churches and believers can examine whether their worship is centered on God, whether they welcome outsiders, and whether their practices treat people fairly. Individual believers, as temples of the Holy Spirit, can also ask whether their lives are marked by prayer or crowded with other priorities.

The fig tree and the parables warn against religion that looks impressive but bears no fruit. The religious leaders had the appearance of devotion but rejected God's messengers and His Son. Believers can look for evidence of genuine fruit in their lives: repentance, obedience, love, and faithfulness. Saying the right words is not enough; doing the Father's will is what matters.

Finally, the chapter offers hope to those who have said "no" to God. Tax collectors and prostitutes were entering the kingdom because they repented and believed. No one is beyond God's grace. At the same time, the chapter warns that rejecting Jesus, the cornerstone, has serious consequences. The right response is to receive Him as King, turn from sin, and bear the fruit of His kingdom.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the humble King who came to Jerusalem riding on a donkey, bringing peace and salvation. I welcome You with praise, and I ask You to be the King You truly are in my life, not the king I would design for myself. Save me, Lord, and help me to trust Your ways when they are different from my expectations.

Cleanse my heart as You cleansed the temple. Remove the greed, distraction, and empty religion that crowd out prayer, and make my life a house of prayer. Help me and Your church to welcome people of every nation and background. Keep me from being like the son who said yes and did not go. Give me a repentant heart that turns to You and does the Father's will. Thank You that tax collectors and sinners are welcomed into Your kingdom, and that Your grace reaches me too. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
