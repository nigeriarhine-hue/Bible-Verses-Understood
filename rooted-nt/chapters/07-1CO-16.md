# 1 Corinthians 16
theme: Generous Giving, Open Doors, and Courage Rooted in Love
main: Paul closes his letter with practical instructions. He asks the Corinthians to set aside money regularly on the first day of each week for the collection for poor believers in Jerusalem, to be carried by approved representatives. He shares his travel plans, explaining that he will remain in Ephesus because a great door for effective work has opened despite many opponents. He asks them to welcome Timothy and explains Apollos's plans. Then he gives a short, powerful charge: be watchful, stand firm in the faith, be courageous and strong, and do everything in love. He commends faithful servants like Stephanas, sends greetings, adds a personal note in his own hand, and closes with the ancient prayer, "Come, Lord!"

## Chapter Background and Story

Letters in the ancient world often ended with travel plans, instructions, greetings, and a farewell. Paul follows this pattern, but even his practical closing words are full of spiritual meaning. This chapter shows Paul as an organizer, a planner, a friend, and a pastor who cared about the practical details of church life.

The collection for Jerusalem mentioned here was a major project of Paul's ministry. The believers in Jerusalem were poor, perhaps because of famine, persecution, and the economic hardships of the time. Paul gathered gifts from the Gentile churches of Galatia, Macedonia, and Achaia to help them (Romans 15:25–27; 2 Corinthians 8–9). This gift expressed the unity of Jewish and Gentile believers in one body. Paul writes more about it in 2 Corinthians 8–9.

### The Collection for the Saints (16:1–4)

[[v1|Now concerning the collection for the saints]]. This is another topic the Corinthians had asked about. Paul's instructions about regular giving are studied below. He adds: [[v3|When I arrive, I will send whoever you approve with letters to carry your gracious gift to Jerusalem]]. Paul did not want to handle the money himself without accountability. The church would choose trustworthy representatives to carry it. [[v4|If it is appropriate for me to go also, they will go with me]]. Paul was careful about financial integrity (2 Corinthians 8:20–21).

### Paul's Travel Plans (16:5–12)

Paul planned to visit Corinth after passing through Macedonia, and perhaps to spend the winter there. [[v7|For I do not wish to see you now in passing, but I hope to stay a while with you, if the Lord permits]]. As always, Paul held his plans humbly, subject to the Lord's will. In fact, his plans later changed, causing some Corinthians to question his reliability (2 Corinthians 1:15–17). The reason he was staying in Ephesus is studied below.

[[v10|Now if Timothy comes, see that he is with you without fear, for he does the work of the Lord, as I also do]]. Timothy was young (1 Timothy 4:12) and perhaps timid (2 Timothy 1:7). The Corinthian church could be difficult. Paul urged them to welcome him and not to look down on him. [[v11|Therefore let no one despise him]].

[[v12|Now concerning Apollos the brother, I strongly urged him to come to you with the brothers, but it was not at all his desire to come now]]. Notice how Paul speaks of Apollos: "the brother." Some Corinthians had divided into factions around Paul and Apollos (1:12), but Paul and Apollos were not rivals. Paul urged Apollos to visit, though Apollos decided to wait, perhaps to avoid stirring up the divisions further.

### Final Exhortations (16:13–18)

Paul's charge to the church is studied below. He then commends the household of Stephanas, [[v15|the first fruits of Achaia]], who had [[v15|set themselves to serve the saints]]. Paul asks the church to [[v16|be in subjection to such, and to everyone who helps in the work and labors]]. Those who serve faithfully deserve respect. Stephanas, Fortunatus, and Achaicus had come to Paul from Corinth, perhaps carrying the church's letter. [[v18|For they refreshed my spirit and yours. Therefore acknowledge those who are like that]]. Faithful believers who refresh others should be honored.

### Greetings and Farewell (16:19–24)

[[v19|The assemblies of Asia greet you. Aquila and Priscilla greet you warmly in the Lord, together with the assembly that is in their house]]. Priscilla and Aquila, who had worked with Paul in Corinth (Acts 18:2–3), were now in Ephesus, hosting a church in their home. [[v20|All the brothers greet you. Greet one another with a holy kiss]].

[[v21|This greeting is by me, Paul, with my own hand]]. Paul usually dictated his letters to a scribe (Romans 16:22), but he often wrote the final greeting himself, as a personal touch and a sign of authenticity (Galatians 6:11; Colossians 4:18; 2 Thessalonians 3:17). The closing prayer is studied below. [[v23|The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with you]]. [[v24|My love to all of you in Christ Jesus. Amen]]. After all his corrections and rebukes, Paul's final word is love.

## Important Verses Explained

### 1 Corinthians 16:2 — Regular, Proportionate Giving

verse: 1 Corinthians 16:2

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was collecting money from the Gentile churches to help the poor believers in Jerusalem. He had given the same instructions to the churches in Galatia, and now he gave them to the Corinthians.

[[v2|On the first day of every week]]. The first day of the week, Sunday, was the day believers gathered to worship, because it was the day of Jesus' resurrection (Acts 20:7). Paul asked them to set aside their gifts each week. This made giving a regular part of worship.

[[v2|let each one of you save as he may prosper]]. Every believer was to participate: "each one of you." No one was excluded, whether rich or poor. And each was to give "as he may prosper," in proportion to their income. Those who had more would give more; those who had less would give less. Giving was to be proportionate, not a fixed amount for everyone.

The phrase "save" or "put aside" suggests that believers set the money aside, perhaps at home or in a common fund, so that it would be ready when Paul arrived.

[[v2|that no collections are made when I come]]. Paul did not want to have to pressure people to give when he arrived. Regular, planned giving would mean the gift was ready. It also meant giving would be thoughtful and generous rather than rushed and emotional.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that giving is an act of worship. By connecting giving with the first day of the week, Paul made it part of believers' regular worship. Giving is a way of honoring God with what He has provided.

It also shows principles for Christian giving: it should be regular, personal, proportionate, and planned. Paul later wrote, [[2 Corinthians 9:7|Let each man give according as he has determined in his heart, not grudgingly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver]].

Finally, the verse shows that giving expresses the unity of the church. The Gentile believers in Corinth were giving to Jewish believers in Jerusalem whom they had never met. Generosity crossed ethnic and geographic boundaries, showing that believers are one body.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to give regularly. Rather than giving only when they feel moved, believers can plan their giving as part of their worship and budget.

It also encourages proportionate giving. Each believer can give according to what God has provided. This means that giving is possible for everyone, whatever their income.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to support those in need beyond their own community. Believers can give to help the poor, support persecuted Christians, and assist believers around the world.

### 1 Corinthians 16:9 — A Great Door and Many Adversaries

verse: 1 Corinthians 16:9

#### Simple Explanation

Paul was writing from Ephesus. He planned to visit Corinth, but not immediately. He explained, [[v8|But I will stay at Ephesus until Pentecost]].

Why? [[v9|for a great and effective door has opened to me]]. God had given Paul a great opportunity in Ephesus. The image of a "door" is used elsewhere in Paul's letters for opportunities to preach the gospel (2 Corinthians 2:12; Colossians 4:3). During Paul's time in Ephesus, the gospel spread throughout the province of Asia (Acts 19:10). The door was "great," a large opportunity, and "effective," leading to real results.

[[v9|and there are many adversaries]]. At the same time, Paul faced many opponents. Acts 19 describes the opposition in Ephesus, including a riot led by silversmiths whose business was threatened (Acts 19:23–41). Paul may also have faced other dangers there (1 Corinthians 15:32; 2 Corinthians 1:8–9).

Notice that Paul mentions the open door and the opponents together. For Paul, opposition was not a sign that he should leave. It was often a sign that God was at work. Where the gospel advances, opposition often arises.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that God opens doors for His work. Opportunities for the gospel come from Him. Jesus says to the church in Philadelphia, [[Revelation 3:8|Behold, I have set before you an open door, which no one can shut]].

It also shows that opportunity and opposition often go together. When God is working powerfully, the enemy often resists. Believers should not be surprised or discouraged by opposition.

Finally, the verse shows Paul's wisdom in discerning God's leading. Paul did not leave Ephesus simply because there was opposition. He stayed because the door was open. Believers can look for where God is opening doors and walk through them, even when it is difficult.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to look for open doors. God may give believers opportunities to share their faith, serve others, or start new ministries. Believers can pray for open doors and be ready to walk through them.

It also encourages believers not to be discouraged by opposition. When believers face resistance in serving God, it may be a sign that God is at work. Believers can persevere, trusting Him.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to discern God's leading. Not every difficulty is a sign to quit, and not every opportunity is from God. Believers can seek God's wisdom through prayer, Scripture, and counsel to know when to stay and when to move on.

### 1 Corinthians 16:13–14 — Stand Firm, Be Strong, Do All in Love

verse: 1 Corinthians 16:13-14

#### Simple Explanation

As Paul brought his letter to a close, he gave a short, powerful set of commands that sum up much of what he had written.

[[v13|Watch!]] Be alert, awake, on guard. The Corinthians faced dangers: false teaching, division, immorality, and pride. They needed to be watchful. Jesus also told His followers to watch (Mark 13:37).

[[v13|Stand firm in the faith!]] Do not be shaken or moved. Hold on to the truth of the gospel. Paul had just written about standing firm in the resurrection hope (15:1, 58).

[[v13|Be courageous!]] The Greek word means to act like a mature person, to be brave. The Corinthians had been acting like spiritual children (3:1; 14:20). Paul called them to maturity and courage.

[[v13|Be strong!]] Be strengthened, empowered. Strength comes not from oneself but from the Lord (Ephesians 6:10).

These four commands sound like a military charge, calling soldiers to readiness and courage. But Paul immediately balances them:

[[v14|Let all that you do be done in love]]. Everything, all their watchfulness, firmness, courage, and strength, must be done in love. Without love, strength can become harshness, firmness can become stubbornness, and courage can become pride. Paul had written a whole chapter on love (chapter 13). Now he summarizes: let everything be done in love.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show the balance of Christian character. Believers are called to be strong and courageous, standing firm for the truth. But they are also called to love. Strength without love is harsh; love without strength is weak. Christ combined both perfectly.

They also summarize the whole letter. The Corinthians needed to be watchful against error, firm in the gospel, mature rather than childish, strong in the Lord, and above all loving. These commands address every problem Paul had confronted.

Finally, these verses show that love is the governing principle of the Christian life. Paul writes elsewhere, [[Colossians 3:14|Above all these things, walk in love, which is the bond of perfection]].

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to be spiritually alert. Believers can be watchful against temptation, false teaching, and the gradual drift away from God.

They also encourage believers to stand firm and be courageous. In a world that often opposes Christian faith, believers need courage to hold to the truth and live it out.

Finally, these verses remind believers that everything must be done in love. When believers stand for truth, defend the faith, or confront wrong, they can do so with love: speaking the truth in love (Ephesians 4:15), with gentleness and respect (1 Peter 3:15).

### 1 Corinthians 16:22 — "Come, Lord!"

verse: 1 Corinthians 16:22

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had written his final greeting with his own hand. Then he added a solemn statement and a prayer.

[[v22|If any man doesn’t love the Lord Jesus Christ, let him be cursed]]. This is a serious warning. To "be cursed" (anathema) means to be under God's judgment. Paul had written a letter full of correction. Now he reminds the Corinthians that what ultimately matters is love for the Lord Jesus. Those who do not love Him are outside His blessing. This warning would have challenged anyone in Corinth who was merely going through the motions without genuine love for Christ.

[[v22|Come, Lord!]] Paul then prays a short prayer. In the original, it is an Aramaic phrase, "Maranatha," which Paul did not translate. It may be read as "Our Lord, come!" or "Our Lord has come." Most interpreters understand it as a prayer for Christ's return. Its use in Aramaic, the language of the earliest Jewish believers, shows that this was one of the earliest Christian prayers, probably used in worship. Revelation ends with the same prayer: [[Revelation 22:20|Amen! Yes, come, Lord Jesus]].

This prayer shows that the early believers longed for Christ's return. They lived with expectation. Paul had just written about the resurrection that will happen at Christ's coming (15:23, 51–52). Now he prays for that day to come.

Notice also that the prayer was addressed to Jesus as "Lord." The earliest Aramaic-speaking Jewish believers prayed to Jesus, showing that they regarded Him as divine.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that love for Jesus is the heart of the Christian faith. It is not enough to know about Jesus or belong to a church. Jesus asked Peter, "Do you love me?" (John 21:15–17). Love for Christ is the mark of a true disciple.

It also shows that Christian hope is centered on Christ's return. The early church prayed eagerly for Jesus to come back. This hope gave them courage in suffering and purpose in daily life.

Finally, the verse shows that the earliest believers worshiped Jesus. Praying "Come, Lord" in Aramaic shows that from the very beginning, Jewish believers addressed Jesus as Lord in prayer.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine their love for Jesus. Believers can ask whether their faith is driven by genuine love for Him, or whether it has become routine.

It also encourages believers to pray for Christ's return. Believers can join the early church in praying "Come, Lord!" with longing and expectation.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to live in light of His coming. Believers who long for Christ's return will want to live faithfully, love others, and share the gospel while there is time.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

1 Corinthians 16 shows that practical matters are part of faithful Christian living. Paul's instructions about giving show that generosity should be regular, proportionate, planned, and part of worship. Believers can give faithfully to support the church and help those in need, including believers far away, and those who handle gifts should do so with integrity and accountability.

The chapter also shows that God opens doors for His work, often alongside opposition. Paul stayed in Ephesus because a great door had opened, even though there were many adversaries. Believers can look for opportunities God provides and persevere through resistance, holding their plans humbly, "if the Lord permits."

1 Corinthians 16 calls believers to be watchful, stand firm in the faith, be courageous, be strong, and do everything in love. This short charge summarizes the whole letter. Believers can stand for truth with courage while making love the governing principle of all they do. They can also honor those who serve faithfully and refresh others, like Stephanas and his household.

Finally, the chapter ends with a prayer: "Come, Lord!" Love for the Lord Jesus is the heart of the Christian faith, and hope in His return gives believers courage and purpose. Believers can examine their love for Christ, pray eagerly for His coming, and live each day in light of that hope, held by the grace of the Lord Jesus.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for the practical wisdom of this letter and for Your care for every part of Your people's lives. Make me a generous giver, setting aside regularly what You have given me to bless others. Open doors for Your gospel, and give me courage to walk through them even when there is opposition. Help me to be watchful, to stand firm in the faith, to be courageous and strong, and above all, to do everything in love. Thank You for those who serve faithfully and refresh my spirit; help me to honor them and to be like them. Fill my heart with love for You, Lord Jesus, and with longing for Your return. Come, Lord! May Your grace be with me and with all Your people. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
