# 1 Timothy 5
theme: Caring for God's Family with Honor and Justice
main: Paul instructs Timothy to treat members of the church as family: older men as fathers, younger men as brothers, older women as mothers, and younger women as sisters, with complete purity. He gives detailed guidance for caring for widows, urging families to care for their own relatives and declaring that anyone who fails to provide for their household has denied the faith. The church is to support widows who are truly alone and devoted to God, while younger widows are encouraged to remarry. Paul then turns to elders: those who lead well, especially in preaching and teaching, deserve double honor and fair support, accusations against them must be confirmed by witnesses, and those who persist in sin must be rebuked publicly. Timothy is to act without partiality, not to appoint leaders hastily, and to keep himself pure, trusting that both sins and good works will eventually come to light.

## Chapter Background and Story

First Timothy 5 deals with practical questions of church life. How should a young leader relate to people of different ages and sexes? How should the church care for vulnerable members, especially widows? And how should leaders be honored, supported, and, when necessary, held accountable?

Widows were among the most vulnerable people in the ancient world. Women often depended on husbands or sons for financial support. When a husband died and there was no family to help, a widow could fall into poverty. From the beginning, the church cared for widows (Acts 6:1; 9:39). But by the time of this letter, questions had arisen. Some families were neglecting their own widowed relatives and leaving the church to support them. Some younger widows on the church's support list were becoming idle and spreading gossip. Paul gives guidance so that the church can care well for those in genuine need while encouraging families to fulfill their responsibilities.

The second half of the chapter addresses elders. Since false teaching had arisen partly through leaders, Timothy needed guidance on how to honor faithful elders and how to deal fairly with accusations against them.

### The Church as Family (5:1–2)

Paul's instructions on relating to others as family are studied below.

### Caring for Widows (5:3–16)

[[v3|Honor widows who are widows indeed]]. "Honor" includes respect and, in this context, financial support. "Widows indeed" are those who are truly alone and without family to care for them.

[[v4|But if any widow has children or grandchildren, let them learn first to show piety toward their own family and to repay their parents, for this is acceptable in the sight of God]]. Caring for aging parents and grandparents is a form of godliness. It repays them for the care they once gave. The principle of family responsibility is studied below.

[[v5|Now she who is a widow indeed and desolate, has her hope set on God and continues in petitions and prayers night and day]]. The true widow has placed her hope in God. Like Anna in the temple (Luke 2:36–37), she devotes herself to prayer. [[v6|But she who gives herself to pleasure is dead while she lives]]. In contrast, a widow who lives for self-indulgence is spiritually dead even though she is physically alive.

[[v9|Let no one be enrolled as a widow under sixty years old, having been the wife of one man]], [[v10|being approved by good works, if she has brought up children, if she has been hospitable to strangers, if she has washed the saints’ feet, if she has relieved the afflicted, and if she has diligently followed every good work]]. There seems to have been an official list of widows who received ongoing support and who, in turn, served the church through prayer and good works. To be enrolled, a widow had to be at least sixty, faithful in marriage, and known for good works such as hospitality, humble service, and care for the suffering. "Washed the saints' feet" refers to humble acts of service, following Jesus' example (John 13:14).

[[v11|But refuse younger widows, for when they have grown wanton against Christ, they desire to marry]], [[v12|having condemnation, because they have rejected their first pledge]]. Enrolled widows may have pledged to remain unmarried and devote themselves to the Lord's service. Younger widows who made such a pledge might later want to remarry, breaking their promise. Paul is not condemning remarriage; in verse 14 he encourages it. His concern is the breaking of a pledge.

[[v13|Besides, they also learn to be idle, going about from house to house. Not only idle, but also gossips and busybodies, saying things which they ought not]]. Some younger widows supported by the church had become idle and were spreading gossip, possibly including the false teaching circulating in Ephesus.

[[v14|I desire therefore that the younger widows marry, bear children, rule the household, and give no occasion to the adversary for insulting]]. Paul encourages younger widows to remarry and build households. This directly counters the false teachers who forbade marriage (1 Timothy 4:3). [[v15|For already some have turned away after Satan]].

[[v16|If any man or woman who believes has widows, let them relieve them, and don’t let the assembly be burdened, that it might relieve those who are widows indeed]]. Believers with widowed relatives should care for them, so that the church's resources can go to those who have no one else.

### Honoring and Correcting Elders (5:17–25)

Paul's teaching on supporting and holding elders accountable is studied below.

[[v22|Lay hands hastily on no one. Don’t be a participant in other people’s sins. Keep yourself pure]]. "Lay hands" refers to appointing leaders. Timothy should not rush to appoint anyone. If he appoints an unqualified person who then does harm, he shares in the responsibility.

[[v23|Be no longer a drinker of water only, but use a little wine for your stomach’s sake and your frequent infirmities]]. This personal note shows Paul's care for Timothy's health. Timothy may have been avoiding wine entirely, perhaps to distance himself from those who drank excessively or in reaction to the false teachers' asceticism. Water in the ancient world was often unsafe. Paul advises a little wine as medicine. This verse also shows that physical health matters and that using ordinary means, like medicine, is not a lack of faith.

[[v24|Some men’s sins are evident, preceding them to judgment, and some also follow later]]. [[v25|In the same way also there are good works that are obvious, and those that are otherwise can’t be hidden]]. Some people's sins are obvious; others only come to light over time. The same is true of good works. This is why Timothy should not appoint leaders hastily. Over time, the truth about a person's character will become clear.

## Important Verses Explained

### 1 Timothy 5:1–2 — Treating One Another as Family

verse: 1 Timothy 5:1-2

#### Simple Explanation

Paul gives Timothy guidance on relating to different members of the church. "Don’t rebuke an older man, but exhort him as a father." The word "rebuke" here means to speak harshly or to strike with words. Timothy, as a younger leader, should not speak harshly to older men. Instead, he should appeal to them respectfully, as he would to his own father.

"The younger men as brothers." Younger men should be treated as equals and companions, with brotherly affection.

"The elder women as mothers." Older women deserve the respect, honor, and care one would give one's own mother.

"The younger as sisters, in all purity." Younger women should be treated as sisters, with complete moral purity. Timothy was to avoid any hint of impropriety, flirtation, or exploitation.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage reflects the deep truth that the church is a family. Believers are brothers and sisters in Christ, children of one Father (Ephesians 2:19; 1 Timothy 3:15). Jesus said that whoever does the will of God is His brother, sister, and mother (Mark 3:35). This family identity shapes how believers treat one another: with respect, affection, and care.

The instruction "in all purity" is especially important. Leaders hold positions of trust, and those who are younger or vulnerable must be protected. Treating younger women as sisters means seeking their good and protecting them, never using a position of spiritual authority to pursue personal desires. Tragically, leaders in the church have sometimes abused their positions, causing deep harm. This verse sets a clear standard: any such behavior is a violation of the family love that should mark God's household.

Paul's guidance about correcting older men shows wisdom. Even when correction is needed, it should be given with respect. Leadership in the church is not about dominating others but about serving them as family.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can think of their church as a family and treat its members accordingly. This means showing respect and care to older members, friendship and support to peers, and protection and purity toward those who are younger or vulnerable.

When correction is needed, believers can approach it as family members would, with gentleness, respect, and a desire for the person's good, rather than with harshness.

Churches can also take practical steps to ensure that relationships between leaders and members are safe and pure, such as clear boundaries, accountability, transparency, and policies that protect children and vulnerable adults. These practices honor the family nature of the church.

### 1 Timothy 5:8 — Providing for One's Own

verse: 1 Timothy 5:8

#### Simple Explanation

Paul makes a strong statement about family responsibility. "But if anyone doesn’t provide for his own, and especially his own household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever."

"Provide for" means to think ahead and care for the needs of others. "His own" refers to relatives in general, and "his own household" refers especially to immediate family members living in the same home.

Those who neglect their family's needs have "denied the faith." Their actions contradict the faith they profess. And they are "worse than an unbeliever," because even many unbelievers care for their families. In the Greek and Roman world, caring for aging parents was a widely accepted duty. A believer who failed to do so would fall below the moral standard of the surrounding culture.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that faith is not merely a matter of words or religious activity. It must be expressed in practical care for others, beginning at home. James 2:15–17 makes a similar point: if a brother or sister lacks food and clothing and someone says, "Go in peace," without giving them what they need, that faith is dead.

Jesus Himself criticized religious leaders who used religious vows as an excuse to avoid supporting their parents (Mark 7:9–13). The fifth commandment, "Honor your father and mother," includes caring for them in their old age.

This verse applies to those who are able to provide but choose not to. It does not condemn those who, because of poverty, illness, disability, or other circumstances, are unable to provide all that their families need. In those situations, the church and wider community are called to help.

It also does not require anyone to remain in or return to an abusive situation in order to "provide." Caring for family members who have caused serious harm may look different, sometimes involving support at a safe distance or through others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can consider how they are caring for their families, including aging parents, children, spouses, and extended relatives in need. This may involve financial support, practical help, time, or emotional care.

Caring for aging parents can be demanding, and caregivers often need support themselves. Churches can come alongside families who are caring for elderly or disabled relatives, offering practical help, respite, and encouragement.

This verse also reminds believers that faith begins at home. Generosity to the wider world is good, but it should not come at the expense of neglecting one's own family.

### 1 Timothy 5:17–18 — Double Honor for Faithful Elders

verse: 1 Timothy 5:17-18

#### Simple Explanation

Paul turns to church leaders. "Let the elders who rule well be counted worthy of double honor." Elders who lead faithfully and effectively deserve "double honor," which likely includes both respect and financial support. The word "honor" was used in verse 3 to include material support for widows.

"Especially those who labor in the word and in teaching." Elders who work hard at preaching and teaching deserve special recognition. "Labor" suggests hard, exhausting work.

Verse 18 supports this with two quotations. "You shall not muzzle the ox when it treads out the grain." This comes from Deuteronomy 25:4. In ancient farming, oxen walked over harvested grain to separate it from the stalks. The Law said they should not be muzzled, so they could eat as they worked. Paul uses this same verse in 1 Corinthians 9:9 to argue that those who work for the gospel should be supported.

"The laborer is worthy of his wages." This is a saying of Jesus, recorded in Luke 10:7. Remarkably, Paul places it alongside a quotation from the Old Testament and introduces both with "the Scripture says." This suggests that Jesus' words, and perhaps Luke's Gospel, were already being recognized as Scripture.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage teaches that faithful leaders should be supported. Those who devote themselves to preaching, teaching, and caring for God's people should not be left in want. Paul himself sometimes gave up this right for the sake of the gospel (1 Corinthians 9:12–18), but he affirmed it as a principle.

At the same time, this honor is for those who "rule well." It is not automatic. Leaders who are faithful, diligent, and godly deserve honor. Those who misuse their position do not.

The use of Jesus' words as Scripture is significant for understanding how the early church viewed the teachings of Jesus. From very early on, His words carried the same authority as the Old Testament.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Churches can honor faithful leaders through respect, encouragement, prayer, and fair financial support. Pastors and teachers who labor faithfully in the word should not be underpaid or overworked to the point of exhaustion.

Believers can also express appreciation to those who teach and lead them, through words of thanks, practical help, or simply praying for them.

At the same time, honor should not become blind loyalty. Leaders are worthy of honor when they lead well. Honoring leaders includes holding them to the high standards God sets, as the following verses show.

### 1 Timothy 5:19–21 — Fair Accountability Without Partiality

verse: 1 Timothy 5:19-21

#### Simple Explanation

Paul gives guidance for handling accusations against elders. "Don’t receive an accusation against an elder except at the word of two or three witnesses." Leaders are often targets of criticism, sometimes unfair. This instruction protects them from malicious gossip or unfounded attacks. It follows the Old Testament principle that a matter must be established by two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15).

Verse 20: "Those who sin, reprove in the sight of all, that the rest also may be in fear." When an elder is found to have sinned, and especially when they persist in it, the matter should be addressed openly before the church. Leaders are not above accountability. Public correction shows that sin is taken seriously and warns others.

Verse 21: "I command you in the sight of God, and the Lord Jesus Christ, and the chosen angels, that you observe these things without prejudice, doing nothing by partiality." Paul gives this charge with great solemnity. Timothy must handle these matters fairly, without favoritism. He must not protect a leader because of friendship, popularity, or status, nor condemn someone because of personal dislike.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses balance two concerns: protecting leaders from false accusations and protecting the church from leaders who sin. Both matter. A church that believes every accusation harms innocent leaders. A church that protects leaders at all costs harms those who suffer from their sin.

The requirement of "two or three witnesses" must be understood rightly. It means that accusations should be carefully investigated and confirmed by evidence, not that only accusations with multiple eyewitnesses should be taken seriously. Many forms of serious wrongdoing, especially sexual abuse, often happen in private, with no other witnesses. In such cases, evidence can include patterns of behavior, multiple independent reports, and other corroborating information. Crimes such as abuse must be reported to civil authorities, who are equipped to investigate, and churches must never use this verse as a reason to dismiss or silence victims. Protecting the vulnerable is a clear biblical priority.

The command to act "without partiality" is crucial. Throughout Scripture, God condemns favoritism in judgment (Leviticus 19:15; James 2:1–9). Church leaders must be held to the same standard as everyone else, and in some ways a higher one (James 3:1).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Churches need clear, fair processes for handling accusations against leaders. These processes should protect people from false accusations while ensuring that genuine wrongdoing is investigated thoroughly and addressed openly. Independent investigation and outside accountability can help ensure fairness.

Believers can resist gossip and rumors about leaders, while also refusing to ignore credible concerns. If someone has been harmed by a leader, their account deserves to be taken seriously, and they deserve care, protection, and support.

Finally, these verses call for courage. Addressing sin in leaders is difficult, especially when those leaders are popular or powerful. But faithfulness to God and love for the church require it.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

First Timothy 5 shows how the church can live as God's family. Members are to treat one another as fathers, mothers, brothers, and sisters, with respect, affection, and complete purity. This family identity shapes every relationship and calls leaders especially to protect those who are younger or vulnerable.

The chapter gives careful guidance for caring for widows, which reveals principles that apply to every form of care for the vulnerable. Families have a primary responsibility to care for their own, and failing to do so contradicts the faith. The church should support those who are truly alone, while wisely encouraging those who are able to build their own lives. Faith is expressed in practical care, beginning at home.

Paul also teaches that faithful leaders deserve double honor, including respect and fair support, especially those who labor in preaching and teaching. Jesus' own words, "The laborer is worthy of his wages," support this principle.

At the same time, leaders are not above accountability. Accusations against them should be carefully investigated, and those who persist in sin should be corrected openly, without partiality or favoritism. This principle must never be used to silence victims or dismiss reports of abuse; serious wrongdoing must be taken seriously and reported to the proper authorities. Churches that honor faithful leaders and hold all leaders accountable reflect God's justice and protect His people.

## Chapter Prayer

Father, thank You that Your church is a family. Help me to treat others in Your household with respect, affection, and purity, as fathers and mothers, brothers and sisters. Give me a heart to care for those who are vulnerable, especially widows, the elderly, and those who are alone. Help me to provide faithfully for my own family and to support those who care for aging or disabled loved ones. Thank You for faithful leaders who labor in Your word; help me to honor and support them. Give Your church wisdom and courage to hold leaders accountable, without partiality, and to protect and care for anyone who has been harmed. Keep me pure, and help me to live in such a way that my good works honor You. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
