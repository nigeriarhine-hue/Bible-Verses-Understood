# Matthew 14
theme: Two Kings, Two Banquets, and the Lord of the Sea
main: Matthew contrasts the corrupt banquet of Herod, where John the Baptist is executed to keep a careless oath, with the banquet in the wilderness, where Jesus feeds thousands out of compassion. That night Jesus walks on the stormy sea, calls Peter to come to Him, rescues him when he sinks, and is worshiped by His disciples as the Son of God.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 14 follows the parables of the kingdom and the rejection of Jesus at Nazareth (Matthew 13). It begins a section in which Jesus increasingly withdraws from public conflict and focuses on teaching His disciples. The chapter is built around a striking contrast between two rulers. Herod Antipas, who held the title of king in the popular sense, throws a lavish birthday feast that ends in murder. Jesus, the true King, hosts a simple meal in a deserted place that ends in abundance and healing.

### The Death of John the Baptist (14:1–12)

Herod the tetrarch was Herod Antipas, a son of Herod the Great (who tried to kill the infant Jesus in Matthew 2). After his father's death, Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea from 4 B.C. to A.D. 39. "Tetrarch" originally meant a ruler of a quarter of a territory, and it came to mean a minor ruler under Roman authority. People sometimes called him "king" (v. 9), though Rome never gave him that title.

When Herod heard reports about Jesus, he said, [[v2|This is John the Baptizer. He is risen from the dead]]. His guilty conscience made him fear that the prophet he had killed had come back. Matthew then tells the story in a flashback. Herod had arrested John because John told him, [[v4|It is not lawful for you to have her]]. Herod had divorced his first wife to marry Herodias, who was his brother's wife (and also his niece). Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21 forbade marrying a brother's wife while the brother was alive. John spoke the truth to a powerful man, knowing the danger.

Herod wanted to kill John but was afraid of the people, who considered John a prophet. At Herod's birthday celebration, the daughter of Herodias danced before the guests and pleased Herod. (The historian Josephus names her Salome.) Herod, probably drunk and eager to impress his guests, promised with an oath to give her anything she asked. Prompted by her mother, she asked for the head of John the Baptist on a platter. Herod was grieved, but because of his oaths and his guests, he ordered John beheaded. The head was brought on a platter, like another dish at the feast, and given to the girl, who gave it to her mother.

John's disciples came, took his body, buried it, and went to tell Jesus. John's death foreshadowed Jesus' own: both were faithful messengers of God, executed by rulers who knew they were innocent but acted to protect their reputations.

### Feeding the Five Thousand (14:13–21)

When Jesus heard the news, He withdrew by boat to a deserted place. He may have been grieving His cousin and forerunner, and He may also have been withdrawing from Herod's territory to avoid premature conflict. But the crowds heard where He was going and followed on foot around the lake. When Jesus landed and saw them, [[v14|He had compassion on them and healed their sick]].

As evening came, the disciples urged Jesus to send the crowds away so they could buy food in the villages. Jesus answered, [[v16|They don’t need to go away. You give them something to eat]]. The disciples had only five loaves and two fish. These were probably small barley loaves, the bread of the poor, and dried or pickled fish. Jesus told them to bring the food to Him. He had the people sit down on the grass, took the loaves and fish, looked up to heaven, blessed God, broke the loaves, and gave them to the disciples, who distributed them to the crowd.

Everyone ate and was satisfied. The disciples gathered twelve baskets of leftovers, one for each of the twelve tribes of Israel or one for each disciple. The number fed was about five thousand men, besides women and children, so the total may have been well over ten thousand.

This miracle carries rich Old Testament echoes. God fed Israel with manna in the wilderness (Exodus 16), and the prophet Elisha fed a hundred men with twenty barley loaves, with some left over (2 Kings 4:42–44). Psalm 23 speaks of the shepherd who makes his sheep lie down in green pastures. The actions of taking, blessing, breaking, and giving bread also anticipate the Last Supper (Matthew 26:26). Matthew presents Jesus as the shepherd-king who provides for His people.

### Jesus Walks on the Water (14:22–33)

Immediately after the meal, Jesus made His disciples get into the boat and cross the lake ahead of Him while He dismissed the crowd. John's Gospel tells us that the crowd wanted to make Jesus king by force (John 6:15), which may explain why He sent the disciples away so quickly. Then He went up the mountain alone to pray.

Meanwhile, the boat was far from land, battered by waves because the wind was against it. During [[v25|the fourth watch of the night]], between three and six in the morning, Jesus came to them walking on the sea. The disciples had been rowing against the wind for hours. When they saw Him, they were terrified and cried out, [[v26|It’s a ghost!]] Jesus immediately spoke to them: [[v27|Cheer up! It is I! Don’t be afraid]].

Peter answered, [[v28|Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you on the waters]]. Jesus said, "Come." Peter got out of the boat and walked on the water toward Jesus. But when he saw the wind, he became afraid, began to sink, and cried out, [[v30|Lord, save me!]] Jesus immediately reached out His hand, caught him, and said, [[v31|You of little faith, why did you doubt?]] When they climbed into the boat, the wind stopped.

The disciples in the boat worshiped Jesus, saying, [[v33|You are truly the Son of God!]] This is a significant moment. In Matthew 8, after Jesus calmed a storm, they asked, "What kind of man is this?" Now they answer their own question.

### Healings at Gennesaret (14:34–36)

After crossing the lake, they landed at Gennesaret, a fertile plain on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee. When the people recognized Jesus, they sent word throughout the region and brought all their sick to Him. They begged to touch even the fringe of His garment, as the woman with the bleeding had done (Matthew 9:20), and all who touched it were healed. The chapter ends as it began its second half, with Jesus' compassionate healing.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 14:14 — Compassion in a Time of Grief

verse: Matthew 14:14

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes right after Jesus hears that John the Baptist has been executed by Herod. John was Jesus' relative, the prophet who had prepared His way and baptized Him. His death was brutal and unjust. Matthew tells us that when Jesus heard the news, [[v13|he withdrew from there in a boat to a deserted place apart]]. He seems to have wanted time alone, perhaps to grieve, to pray, and to step away from Herod's attention.

But the crowds heard where He was going and followed Him on foot from the towns around the lake. When Jesus stepped out of the boat, He did not find the quiet place He had sought. Instead, a great crowd was waiting.

Matthew tells us how Jesus responded: [[v14|He had compassion on them and healed their sick]]. The word for compassion is the same strong word used in Matthew 9:36, describing a deep inner response of pity and love. Jesus did not resent the crowd's interruption. He saw their need and responded with care, healing the sick among them. Later in the day, His compassion extended to feeding them all.

This verse shows Jesus' heart in a moment when He might have been expected to focus on His own grief. Instead of turning the crowd away, He gave Himself to meet their needs.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the depth of Jesus' compassion. He was fully human and surely felt sorrow at John's death. Elsewhere He wept at the grave of His friend Lazarus (John 11:35). Yet His grief did not close His heart to others. His compassion flowed even in a time of personal loss.

The verse also shows Jesus as the true shepherd of God's people. The crowds were like sheep without a shepherd (Matthew 9:36), and Herod, their earthly ruler, had just shown himself to be a destroyer rather than a shepherd. Jesus' care for the people stands in sharp contrast to Herod's self-centered feast. The true King is the one who gives Himself for the needs of others.

It is worth noticing that Jesus did seek solitude, and after feeding the crowd He went up the mountain alone to pray (v. 23). His compassion for others did not mean He neglected His own need for time with the Father. Both belong together in a healthy life of service.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse offers comfort to those who are grieving. Jesus knows what it is to lose someone, and He does not stand at a distance from human sorrow. Believers can bring their grief honestly to Him.

It also offers an example for believers who serve others while carrying their own burdens. Compassion does not require pretending that everything is fine. Jesus grieved, sought solitude, and still met the needs in front of Him. Believers can ask God for the strength to love others even in difficult seasons, while also making room for rest, prayer, and their own healing.

Finally, the pattern of Jesus' day, withdrawal, service, and then prayer, offers wisdom for sustainable compassion. People who constantly give without receiving from God can become exhausted. Regular time alone with God prepares believers to respond to interruptions and needs with genuine compassion rather than resentment.

### Matthew 14:27 — [[Matthew 14:27|It Is I. Don't Be Afraid]].

verse: Matthew 14:27

#### Simple Explanation

These words are spoken by Jesus to His disciples in the middle of the night on the Sea of Galilee. After feeding the five thousand, Jesus sent the disciples ahead by boat while He dismissed the crowd and went up the mountain to pray. During the night, the wind turned against them and the waves battered the boat. They were stuck in the middle of the lake, rowing hard and making little progress.

In the early hours of the morning, between three and six, Jesus came toward them, walking on the water. In the darkness and the storm, the disciples did not recognize Him. They thought He was a ghost, and they screamed in fear. Many people in that time believed in spirits that haunted the sea.

Jesus immediately spoke to calm them: [[v27|Cheer up! It is I! Don’t be afraid]]. "Cheer up" means "take courage." "It is I" in Greek is literally "I am" (ego eimi). In ordinary speech this simply means "It's me." But in the Old Testament, God revealed His name to Moses as [[Exodus 3:14|I AM]], and in Isaiah God repeatedly says "I am he" to comfort His people (Isaiah 43:10–13; 51:12). Jesus is walking on the sea, something the Old Testament says only God does: God alone [[Job 9:8|treads on the waves of the sea]]. In this setting, Jesus' words carry a deeper meaning: the One who is coming to them across the waters is the very presence of God.

His first concern was their fear. Before calming the storm, He calmed their hearts with His presence and His word.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals Jesus' identity as the Son of God who shares God's power over creation. The disciples' worship at the end of the scene, [[v33|You are truly the Son of God!]], shows that they began to understand this.

It also reveals how God meets His people in their fear. The phrase "Do not be afraid" appears often in Scripture, usually when God or His messengers appear to people in frightening circumstances. The basis for courage is not that the danger is small, but that God is present. Isaiah 43:2 promises, [[Isaiah 43:2|When you pass through the waters, I will be with you]].

The setting matters too. The disciples were in the storm because they had obeyed Jesus' instruction to cross the lake. Obedience does not guarantee calm waters. But Jesus was watching over them from the mountain, and He came to them at the right time. His delay was not abandonment.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse speaks to believers who feel caught in a storm, whether in health, finances, relationships, or uncertainty about the future. Like the disciples, they may struggle for hours, even years, against contrary winds. Jesus' words invite them to take courage, not because the storm is unreal, but because He is present.

It also reminds believers that fear can distort how they see God's presence. The disciples saw Jesus and thought He was a ghost. In difficult times, people sometimes misread God's work or fail to recognize Him. Listening to His word in Scripture helps believers recognize His presence when circumstances are confusing.

Finally, believers can hold onto the truth that Jesus sees them in the storm. He watched the disciples from the mountain and came to them. Believers can pray honestly in hard times, trusting that He knows where they are and will come to them in His time and in His way.

### Matthew 14:30–31 — Sinking Faith and the Saving Hand

verse: Matthew 14:30-31

#### Simple Explanation

These verses describe what happened after Peter stepped out of the boat. When Jesus came to the disciples walking on the water and said, "It is I," Peter responded boldly: [[v28|Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you on the waters]]. Peter wanted to be where Jesus was and to share in what Jesus was doing. Jesus said, "Come," and Peter climbed over the side of the boat and actually walked on the water toward Jesus.

Then [[v30|when he saw that the wind was strong, he was afraid]]. The wind had been blowing all along, but Peter's attention shifted from Jesus to the danger around him. Fear took over, and he began to sink. In desperation, he cried out, [[v30|Lord, save me!]] This short prayer echoes the disciples' cry in the earlier storm (Matthew 8:25).

Jesus responded [[v31|Immediately]]. He reached out His hand and caught Peter. Then He said, [[v31|You of little faith, why did you doubt?]] "Little faith" does not mean no faith. Peter had enough faith to step out of the boat, which none of the others did. But his faith wavered when he looked at the storm. The word translated "doubt" means to waver or be divided, pulled in two directions. Peter was torn between trust in Jesus and fear of the wind.

Jesus' question is not harsh condemnation but gentle correction. He had already saved Peter before asking it. When they climbed into the boat, the wind stopped.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show both the possibility and the fragility of faith. Peter's step onto the water shows that faith in Jesus can lead people to do what they could never do on their own. His sinking shows how quickly fear can overwhelm faith when attention shifts from Jesus to circumstances.

The most important truth in these verses is that Jesus saves those who call on Him, even when their faith is weak. Peter did not rescue himself, and Jesus did not let him drown as a lesson. Jesus reached out immediately. Paul writes that [[Romans 10:13|whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved]]. Salvation depends not on the strength of our faith but on the strength of the One we trust.

This scene also anticipates Peter's later story. He would again be bold, promising never to deny Jesus, and would again fail when fear took hold (Matthew 26:69–75). And again, Jesus would restore him. Peter's life shows that failures of faith need not be final when we cry out to Jesus.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers to take steps of faith in response to Jesus' call. Following Him may involve stepping out of comfortable places, such as serving in new ways, forgiving someone, taking a risk for the sake of the gospel, or obeying God when the outcome is uncertain. Like Peter, believers can step out when Jesus says, "Come."

They also show how important it is to keep one's focus on Jesus. Difficulties do not disappear when we trust Him, but faith grows stronger when we focus on His power and presence rather than on the size of the problem. Regular prayer, remembering God's past faithfulness, and meditating on Scripture help keep attention on Him.

Finally, these verses offer hope when faith falters. Everyone experiences moments of fear and doubt. The right response is not to hide or despair but to cry out, "Lord, save me!" Jesus reaches out to those who call on Him. Believers can learn from their moments of sinking, trusting that the same hand that caught Peter will catch them.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 14 sets two kinds of leadership side by side. Herod's feast shows power driven by pride, fear of embarrassment, and self-indulgence. He knew John was a prophet, but he executed him rather than lose face before his guests. Jesus' meal in the wilderness shows power used for compassion and provision. Believers who have any kind of influence, whether in families, workplaces, churches, or communities, can ask which kind of leadership their lives resemble. Do they protect their image at the expense of what is right, or do they use their influence to serve others?

John the Baptist's courage is also an example. He told Herod the truth about his sin, even though it cost him his freedom and his life. Few believers face such extreme consequences, but many face pressure to stay silent about what is right. Speaking truth with courage and love remains part of faithful discipleship.

The feeding of the five thousand teaches that Jesus can do much with little. The disciples had only five loaves and two fish, far too little for the need. But when they brought what they had to Jesus, He multiplied it. Believers often feel their resources, abilities, or time are insufficient to make a difference. The call is to bring what they have to Jesus and let Him use it.

Finally, the storm and Peter's walk on the water speak to fear and faith. Jesus comes to His people in their storms, speaks words of courage, and reaches out to save those who call on Him. Believers can step out in faith when He calls, keep their eyes on Him in difficulty, and cry out to Him when they begin to sink. Like the disciples in the boat, their experiences of His rescue can lead them to deeper worship of Him as the Son of God.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for John the Baptist's faithful courage, and help me speak truth with love even when it is costly. Guard me from the pride and fear of embarrassment that led Herod to do what he knew was wrong. Teach me to use whatever influence I have to serve others, as You did.

Thank You for Your compassion, even in Your own grief, and for feeding the hungry crowd from so little. I bring You what I have, small as it is; use it for Your purposes. When storms rise and the wind is against me, help me hear Your voice saying, "Take courage, it is I; do not be afraid." Give me faith to step out when You call, and when fear makes me sink, let me cry out to You and feel Your hand catching me. Like Your disciples in the boat, I worship You as the Son of God. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
