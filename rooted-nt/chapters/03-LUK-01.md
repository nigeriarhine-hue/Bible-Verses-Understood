# Luke 1
theme: God Remembers His Promises: Two Miraculous Births Announced
main: Luke opens his carefully researched account by telling how the angel Gabriel announced two births: John, son of the elderly priest Zechariah and his wife Elizabeth, who would prepare the way for the Lord; and Jesus, conceived by the Holy Spirit in the virgin Mary, who would reign forever on David's throne. Mary responds with humble trust and a song of praise to the God who lifts up the lowly, and Zechariah, his voice restored, praises God for remembering His covenant and sending salvation.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke is the longest of the four Gospels, and it is the first of two volumes by the same author; the second is the book of Acts. Together they make up more than a quarter of the New Testament. Early Christian tradition identifies the author as Luke, a physician and companion of the apostle Paul (Colossians 4:14; 2 Timothy 4:11; Philemon 24). Luke was likely a Gentile, and he wrote in polished Greek for a broad audience. He gives special attention to people often overlooked in the ancient world: women, the poor, Samaritans, Gentiles, and sinners. He also emphasizes prayer, the Holy Spirit, and joy.

Luke 1 is unique among the Gospels. It tells the stories of John the Baptist's and Jesus' births side by side, showing that both were part of God's plan. The chapter is filled with Old Testament echoes. Elderly, childless couples receiving miraculous children recall Abraham and Sarah (Genesis 18) and Hannah, mother of Samuel (1 Samuel 1–2). The songs of Mary and Zechariah are woven from the language of the Psalms and the Prophets. Luke wants his readers to see that God is fulfilling the promises He made to Israel.

### Luke's Purpose (1:1–4)

Luke begins with a formal introduction, the kind found in Greek historical and scientific writings of the time. He notes that many had already written accounts of the events [[v1|which have been fulfilled among us]], based on the testimony of eyewitnesses and servants of the word. Luke himself had investigated everything carefully from the beginning and decided to write an orderly account for [[v3|most excellent Theophilus]]. This prologue is studied below.

### Zechariah and Elizabeth (1:5–25)

Luke sets the story [[v5|in the days of Herod, the king of Judea]], Herod the Great, who ruled from 37 to 4 B.C. A priest named Zechariah belonged to the priestly division of Abijah. King David had organized the priests into twenty-four divisions (1 Chronicles 24), and each served in the temple for one week twice a year. Zechariah's wife, Elizabeth, was also from a priestly family, a descendant of Aaron. Both were [[v6|righteous before God]], living faithfully according to God's commands. But they had no children, because Elizabeth was unable to conceive, and both were now old.

In that culture, childlessness was often considered a disgrace and sometimes even assumed to be a sign of God's disfavor. Luke makes clear that this was not the case for Zechariah and Elizabeth: they were righteous. Their story recalls Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, and Hannah, all of whom waited long for children whom God gave in His time.

While Zechariah's division was serving, he was chosen by lot to enter the temple and burn incense on the altar in the Holy Place. There were so many priests that this was likely a once-in-a-lifetime honor. Incense was offered every morning and evening, and its rising smoke symbolized the prayers of God's people (Psalm 141:2). Outside, a crowd of worshipers was praying.

Suddenly an angel of the Lord appeared, standing at the right side of the altar. Zechariah was terrified. The angel said, [[v13|Don’t be afraid, Zacharias, because your request has been heard. Your wife, Elizabeth, will bear you a son, and you shall call his name John]]. "John" means "the LORD is gracious." The angel described the child's future: he would bring joy to many, be great in God's sight, never drink wine or strong drink (like a Nazirite, Numbers 6:1–4), and be filled with the Holy Spirit even before birth. He would turn many Israelites back to God and go before the Lord [[v17|in the spirit and power of Elijah]], fulfilling Malachi 4:5–6, to prepare a people for the Lord.

Zechariah asked how he could be sure of this, since he and his wife were old. The angel identified himself: [[v19|I am Gabriel, who stands in the presence of God]]. Gabriel had appeared to the prophet Daniel centuries earlier (Daniel 8:16; 9:21). Because Zechariah did not believe, he would be unable to speak until the promise was fulfilled.

The people waiting outside wondered why Zechariah was taking so long. When he finally came out, he could not speak to give the customary priestly blessing. They realized he had seen a vision. After his service ended, he went home. Elizabeth became pregnant and stayed in seclusion for five months, saying, [[v25|Thus has the Lord done to me in the days in which he looked at me, to take away my reproach among men]].

### The Announcement to Mary (1:26–38)

In Elizabeth's sixth month of pregnancy, God sent Gabriel to Nazareth, a small, obscure village in Galilee. Gabriel came to a virgin named Mary, who was pledged to be married to Joseph, a descendant of King David. Jewish betrothal was a binding commitment, usually arranged when the girl was quite young, often in her early to middle teens. The marriage would be completed about a year later when the bride moved to her husband's home.

The contrast with the previous scene is striking. Zechariah was an elderly priest in the temple in Jerusalem, the holiest place in Israel. Mary was a young woman in an insignificant village, with no religious position. Yet Gabriel greeted her, [[v28|Rejoice, you highly favored one! The Lord is with you]]. "Highly favored" means that she had received grace from God, not that she was the source of grace.

Mary was greatly troubled and wondered what this greeting meant. Gabriel said, [[v30|Don’t be afraid, Mary, for you have found favor with God]]. She would conceive and give birth to a son and name Him Jesus. He would be great, would be called the Son of the Most High, and God would give Him the throne of His father David. He would reign over the house of Jacob forever, and His kingdom would never end. These words fulfill God's promise to David that his descendant would reign forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16).

Mary asked, [[v34|How can this be, seeing I am a virgin?]] Her question differs from Zechariah's. He asked for proof; she asked how it would happen. Gabriel explained: [[v35|The Holy Spirit will come on you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you]]. The word "overshadow" recalls the cloud of God's presence that covered the tabernacle (Exodus 40:35). Therefore, the child would be called holy, the Son of God. Gabriel also told her that her relative Elizabeth, who had been called barren, was six months pregnant, and concluded, [[v37|For nothing spoken by God is impossible]].

Mary's response is studied below.

### Mary Visits Elizabeth (1:39–56)

Mary hurried to the hill country of Judea to visit Elizabeth, likely a journey of several days. When Mary greeted her, the baby leaped in Elizabeth's womb, and Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit. She exclaimed, [[v42|Blessed are you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb!]] and asked why she was so favored that [[v43|the mother of my Lord]] should come to her. Elizabeth, inspired by the Spirit, recognized the child Mary carried as her Lord. Even the unborn John responded with joy. Elizabeth added, [[v45|Blessed is she who believed]], contrasting Mary's faith with her husband's doubt.

Mary responded with a song of praise, often called the Magnificat from its first word in Latin. It is studied below. Mary stayed with Elizabeth about three months, likely until John's birth, and then returned home.

### The Birth and Naming of John (1:57–66)

Elizabeth gave birth to a son, and her neighbors and relatives rejoiced with her. On the eighth day, they came to circumcise the child, as the law required (Genesis 17:12; Leviticus 12:3), and they assumed he would be named after his father. Elizabeth said, [[v60|Not so; but he will be called John]]. They objected, since no one in the family had that name, and they made signs to Zechariah, suggesting that he may have been deaf as well as mute. He asked for a writing tablet and wrote, [[v63|His name is John]]. Immediately his mouth was opened, and he began to speak, praising God.

Fear came on all the neighbors, and these events were talked about throughout the hill country of Judea. People wondered, [[v66|What then will this child be?]], because the Lord's hand was clearly with him.

### Zechariah's Song (1:67–80)

Zechariah was filled with the Holy Spirit and prophesied. His song, often called the Benedictus from its first word in Latin, praises God for visiting and redeeming His people. God had raised up [[v69|a horn of salvation]] in the house of David. "Horn" was an image of strength, like the horns of a powerful animal. God was keeping His promises spoken by the prophets, showing mercy, remembering His covenant with Abraham, and rescuing His people so that they could serve Him without fear, in holiness and righteousness.

Then Zechariah turned to his son. John would be called a prophet of the Most High, going before the Lord to prepare His way and to give His people knowledge of salvation through the forgiveness of sins. The song ends with a beautiful image of God's tender mercy, studied below.

The chapter closes with a summary: the child grew and became strong in spirit, and he lived in the wilderness until he appeared publicly to Israel.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 1:3–4 — Certainty About What You Have Been Taught

verse: Luke 1:3-4

#### Simple Explanation

These verses are part of Luke's introduction to his Gospel, a single, carefully constructed sentence in Greek (vv. 1–4). In verse 1, Luke acknowledged that many others had already written accounts of the events concerning Jesus. In verse 2, he explained that these accounts were based on the testimony of [[v2|those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and servants of the word]], people who had seen Jesus' ministry firsthand and had become messengers of the gospel.

Now Luke explains his own work: [[v3|it seemed good to me also, having traced the course of all things accurately from the first, to write to you in order]]. Several important claims are packed into this sentence. Luke "traced" or investigated the events, suggesting research and inquiry. He did so "accurately," carefully checking information. He went back "from the first," to the very beginning of the story. And he wrote "in order," an organized account, though not necessarily in strict chronological sequence in every detail.

He wrote for [[v3|most excellent Theophilus]]. The name means "friend of God" or "loved by God." "Most excellent" was a title of respect used for officials of high rank (Acts 24:3; 26:25). Theophilus may have been a Roman official or a wealthy patron who sponsored Luke's writing. Some have suggested the name represents any reader who loves God, though most scholars think he was a real person.

Luke's purpose: [[v4|that you might know the certainty concerning the things in which you were instructed]]. Theophilus had already been taught about Jesus, perhaps as a new believer or an interested inquirer. Luke wanted him to have confidence that what he had learned was reliable.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that the Christian faith is rooted in history. Luke did not present a collection of myths or private visions. He wrote about real events that happened in real places, among real people, and he based his account on eyewitness testimony and careful investigation. Throughout Luke and Acts, he anchors events in history by naming rulers and officials (Luke 2:1–2; 3:1–2).

They also show that faith and careful thinking go together. Luke did not ask Theophilus to believe without reason. He provided evidence and an orderly account so that Theophilus could have certainty. Christianity invites people to examine its claims.

Finally, the verses show that the gospel is meant to be passed on faithfully. The eyewitnesses delivered the message, Luke carefully recorded it, and Theophilus received it. This chain of faithful transmission continues as believers today read Scripture and pass on its message to others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers who have doubts or questions about their faith. Like Theophilus, they can seek certainty by studying the Gospels carefully, learning about their historical background, and considering the testimony of the eyewitnesses. Faith does not require ignoring questions; it can grow stronger through honest investigation.

They also encourage believers to value careful study of Scripture. Luke took great care to write accurately. Believers can read the Bible with similar care, paying attention to context, history, and the order of events, rather than reading verses in isolation.

Finally, these verses remind believers that they are part of a long chain of faithful witnesses. They can pass on what they have received to others, especially to new believers who, like Theophilus, need to know the certainty of what they have been taught.

### Luke 1:38 — "Behold, the Servant of the Lord"

verse: Luke 1:38

#### Simple Explanation

These words are Mary's response to the angel Gabriel's announcement. Gabriel had told her that she would conceive and give birth to a son named Jesus, who would be called the Son of the Most High and would reign on David's throne forever. When Mary asked how this could happen since she was a virgin, Gabriel explained that the Holy Spirit would come upon her and the power of the Most High would overshadow her. He told her that her elderly relative Elizabeth was also pregnant, and he declared, "For nothing spoken by God is impossible."

Mary's answer is simple and profound: [[v38|Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to your word]].

"Servant" translates a word that means a female slave or bondservant. Mary described herself as someone who belonged entirely to God and was ready to do His will. This was a statement of humble submission.

[[v38|let it be done to me according to your word]] expressed her acceptance of God's plan. She did not fully understand how it would happen or what it would cost. Being pregnant before her marriage to Joseph was complete could bring suspicion, shame, and the risk that Joseph would end the betrothal. Under the law, she could even face severe consequences if accused of unfaithfulness. Yet Mary trusted God and accepted His word.

Mary's response contrasts with Zechariah's. When Gabriel announced John's birth to Zechariah, an elderly priest serving in the temple, Zechariah asked for proof and was made unable to speak because of his unbelief. Mary, a young woman from an insignificant village, responded with faith.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse presents Mary as a model of faith and surrender to God. Her words echo the prayer Jesus would later teach, "Let your will be done" (Matthew 6:10), and His own prayer in Gethsemane, "not what I desire, but what you desire" (Mark 14:36). Mary shows that true faith involves both trusting God's promises and submitting to His will.

The verse also reveals how God works through humble people. Mary had no wealth, position, or fame. She lived in Nazareth, a village of little importance. Yet God chose her for the most important role in history: to be the mother of the Messiah. God often chooses the lowly to accomplish His purposes, as Mary herself celebrated in her song (Luke 1:48, 52).

Christians have honored Mary throughout history for her faith and obedience. Catholic and Orthodox traditions give her special veneration; Protestant traditions emphasize her as an example of faith. All Christians can agree that her response to God's word is a beautiful model of humble trust.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to respond to God's word with trust and surrender. God's plans may not always be what believers expected, and they may involve uncertainty or cost. Like Mary, believers can say, "Let it be done to me according to your word," trusting that God is good and that His purposes are best.

It also encourages believers to see themselves as servants of the Lord. Their lives belong to God, and their purpose is to do His will. This perspective shapes how they make decisions, handle challenges, and use their time and abilities.

Finally, Mary's response shows that faith does not require complete understanding. She did not know everything that lay ahead, but she trusted the God who spoke. Believers facing uncertainty can trust God's character even when they do not see the full picture.

### Luke 1:46–48 — "My Soul Magnifies the Lord"

verse: Luke 1:46-48

#### Simple Explanation

These verses begin Mary's song of praise, known as the Magnificat. Mary had traveled to visit her relative Elizabeth, who was pregnant with John. When Mary arrived and greeted her, Elizabeth's baby leaped in her womb, and Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, blessed Mary and the child she carried, calling Mary [[v43|the mother of my Lord]]. In response, Mary burst into praise.

[[v46|My soul magnifies the Lord]]. To "magnify" means to make great or to declare greatness. Mary's whole being praised God. [[v47|My spirit has rejoiced in God my Savior]]. Mary called God "my Savior," recognizing her own need for salvation and celebrating God's saving work.

She gave the reason for her praise: [[v48|for he has looked at the humble state of his servant]]. "Humble state" describes Mary's low social position. She was young, female, poor, and from an obscure village. Yet God had looked on her with favor. [[v48|For behold, from now on, all generations will call me blessed]]. Mary recognized that her role would be remembered throughout history, not because of her own greatness, but because of what God had done.

Mary's song draws heavily on the Old Testament, especially the song of Hannah in 1 Samuel 2:1–10. Hannah, who had been childless, praised God after He gave her a son, Samuel. Mary's song echoes Hannah's themes: God's holiness, His care for the lowly, and His reversal of human power. Mary's song also contains phrases from many psalms, showing how deeply she knew the Scriptures.

In the rest of the song (vv. 49–55), Mary praises God's mighty deeds, His mercy, and His reversal of fortunes: scattering the proud, bringing down rulers, lifting up the humble, filling the hungry, and sending the rich away empty. She ends by celebrating God's faithfulness to His promises to Abraham and his descendants.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This song reveals the character of God. He is mighty, holy, merciful, and faithful. He notices the lowly and lifts them up. He opposes the proud and brings down the powerful who abuse their power. He keeps His promises across generations. These themes run throughout Luke's Gospel, where Jesus brings good news to the poor and welcomes the outcast.

Mary's song also shows that true worship begins with recognizing God's greatness and one's own need. Mary did not boast about being chosen. She magnified God and called Him her Savior. Her humility made her a fitting vessel for God's work.

Finally, the song is both personal and corporate. Mary praises God for what He has done for her, and then she praises Him for what He is doing for all His people. Personal experiences of God's grace connect believers to God's larger story of salvation.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to make praise a central part of their lives. Like Mary, they can respond to God's goodness with worship that involves their whole being. Praising God for His character and His work reminds believers of His greatness and lifts their hearts above their circumstances.

They also encourage believers who feel insignificant. God noticed Mary in her humble state. He sees those who are overlooked by the world, and He can use them for His purposes. No one is too small or unimportant for God's attention.

Finally, Mary's song challenges believers to share God's concern for the lowly and His opposition to pride and injustice. Believers can humble themselves before God, care for the hungry and poor, and refuse to trust in wealth or power. Mary's song describes God's kingdom values, and believers are called to live by them.

### Luke 1:78–79 — The Dawn From on High

verse: Luke 1:78-79

#### Simple Explanation

These verses conclude Zechariah's song of praise, called the Benedictus. Zechariah had been unable to speak for about nine months because he doubted the angel's message. When his son was born and he wrote, "His name is John," his speech returned, and he was filled with the Holy Spirit and prophesied.

In the first part of his song, Zechariah praised God for keeping His promises to David and Abraham by raising up a Savior. Then he addressed his infant son, saying that John would be a prophet who would prepare the way for the Lord and give knowledge of salvation through the forgiveness of sins. These final verses explain why all this was happening.

[[v78|because of the tender mercy of our God]]. Salvation comes not because people deserve it but because of God's compassionate heart. The phrase "tender mercy" literally refers to the inner parts, the deepest feelings of compassion.

[[v78|by which the dawn from on high will visit us]]. "Dawn" (or "sunrise") is an image of the coming Messiah. Malachi 4:2 promised that [[Malachi 4:2|shall the sun of righteousness arise with healing in its wings]]. Isaiah 9:2 promised that people walking in darkness would see a great light. Zechariah was saying that God was about to send the Messiah, like the sun rising after a long, dark night.

[[v79|to shine on those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death]]. This echoes Isaiah 9:2. "Darkness" represents ignorance, sin, and despair. "The shadow of death" describes the fear and hopelessness of people facing death without God. The Messiah would bring light into this darkness.

[[v79|to guide our feet into the way of peace]]. The final image is of travelers who, now that the sun has risen, can see the path and walk safely. "Peace" in the Hebrew sense (shalom) means wholeness, well-being, and right relationship with God and others.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal that salvation flows from God's tender mercy. God did not send His Son grudgingly or reluctantly; He sent Him out of deep compassion for people trapped in darkness. This is the heart of the gospel: [[John 3:16|For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son]].

The image of sunrise shows what Jesus brings to the world. Before the dawn, people sit in darkness, unable to see their way, afraid of death. Jesus comes like the rising sun, bringing light, warmth, and life. Later Jesus would say, [[John 8:12|I am the light of the world]]. Those who follow Him do not walk in darkness.

Finally, these verses show that salvation leads to peace. The goal is not merely escaping darkness but walking in the way of peace: peace with God through forgiveness, peace within through trust, and peace with others through love.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses offer hope to anyone sitting in darkness, whether the darkness of sin, grief, depression, fear, or confusion. Jesus came as the dawn from on high to shine on such people. Believers can bring their darkness to Him, trusting that His light can reach even the deepest shadows.

They also encourage believers to rest in God's tender mercy. Salvation is not earned; it is a gift from a compassionate God. When believers feel unworthy, they can remember that God's mercy, not their merit, is the source of their salvation.

Finally, these verses call believers to walk in the way of peace. Having received God's light, they can follow Jesus' guidance in their decisions and relationships, seeking peace with God and with others. They can also be bearers of light, sharing the hope of the dawn with those who are still in darkness.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 1 shows that God keeps His promises, even after long periods of silence and waiting. Zechariah and Elizabeth had prayed for a child for years, and Israel had waited centuries for the Messiah. God answered both in His time. Believers who are waiting on God for answered prayer, for change, or for His promises to be fulfilled can trust that He remembers them and that His timing is perfect.

The chapter contrasts two responses to God's word. Zechariah, a respected priest, doubted and was silenced. Mary, a young woman from an obscure village, believed and surrendered herself to God's will. Believers can choose Mary's response: "Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to your word." This kind of faith trusts God even when His plans are surprising and costly.

The songs of Mary and Zechariah teach believers how to praise God. They magnify His holiness, His mercy, His power, and His faithfulness. They celebrate His care for the lowly and His opposition to pride. Believers can make praise a regular part of their lives and allow these songs to shape their values, caring for the humble and hungry and refusing to trust in wealth or power.

Finally, Luke's careful introduction reminds believers that their faith rests on historical events and reliable testimony. They can study Scripture with confidence, grow in certainty about what they have been taught, and pass on the good news faithfully to others. The dawn from on high has visited the world in Jesus, and believers are called to walk in His light and share it with those in darkness.

## Chapter Prayer

Faithful God, thank You for keeping Your promises across generations and for remembering those who wait on You. Thank You for the careful testimony of eyewitnesses and for the Scriptures that give me certainty about what I believe. When I wait for answers to prayer, help me to trust Your perfect timing.

Give me faith like Mary's, so that I respond to Your word with humble trust and say, "Let it be done to me according to Your word." Forgive me for the times I have doubted like Zechariah. Fill my heart with praise like Mary's song, so that my soul magnifies You and rejoices in You, my Savior. Thank You for looking on the lowly and lifting them up. Thank You for Your tender mercy, by which the dawn from on high has visited us in Jesus. Shine on me in my darkness, and guide my feet into the way of peace. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
