# Mark 4
theme: The Kingdom That Grows in Secret and the Lord Who Stills the Storm
main: Teaching from a boat, Jesus uses parables to show how God's kingdom grows: the word is received differently by different hearts, what is hidden will be revealed, the kingdom grows mysteriously by God's power, and it begins small but becomes great. That evening, when a violent storm threatens to sink the disciples' boat, Jesus calms the wind and waves with a word, and the disciples are left asking who He really is.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 4 is one of only two long teaching sections in Mark's Gospel (the other is Mark 13). Mark generally emphasizes what Jesus did more than what He said, but here he gathers several of Jesus' parables about the kingdom of God. The setting follows the tension of chapter 3: the religious leaders were plotting against Jesus, scribes from Jerusalem had accused Him of working by Satan's power, and His own family thought He was out of His mind. Yet crowds kept coming. Why were people responding so differently to Jesus? And if God's kingdom had truly arrived, why did it seem so small and so opposed? The parables in this chapter address those questions.

### The Parable of the Sower (4:1–9)

Jesus began teaching by the Sea of Galilee. The crowd was so large that He got into a boat and sat down in it on the water, while the people stood on the shore. Coves along the lake form natural amphitheaters where a speaker's voice carries well.

He began with a command: [[v3|Listen!]] Then He told of a farmer scattering seed. Some fell on the hard path, where birds ate it. Some fell on rocky ground with shallow soil, sprang up quickly, and withered in the sun because it had no root. Some fell among thorns, which choked it so it produced no grain. Some fell on good soil and produced a harvest of thirty, sixty, or a hundred times what was sown. Jesus ended, [[v9|Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear]]. The command to "listen" at the beginning and "hear" at the end frames the parable: it is about how people hear God's word.

### Why Parables? (4:10–12)

When Jesus was alone, His followers and the Twelve asked about the parables. He told them, [[v11|To you is given the mystery of God’s Kingdom, but to those who are outside, all things are done in parables]]. In the Bible, a "mystery" is a truth once hidden but now revealed by God. The disciples had been given insight into God's kingdom because they had come close to Jesus. Those "outside" heard only stories.

Jesus then quoted Isaiah 6:9–10, where God told Isaiah that his preaching would harden a people who refused to listen. These words are difficult. They do not mean that Jesus wanted people to remain in sin. Rather, parables both reveal and conceal. For those who come to Jesus wanting to understand, parables open up the truth. For those who have already hardened their hearts, parables remain puzzling, and their rejection is confirmed. The parables test the heart.

### The Sower Explained (4:13–20)

Jesus asked, [[v13|Don’t you understand this parable? How will you understand all of the parables?]] The sower parable is a key to the rest, because it is about how people receive God's word.

He explained that the farmer sows the word. The path represents people from whom Satan immediately takes away the word. The rocky ground represents people who receive the word with joy but have no root, so they fall away when trouble or persecution comes. The thorny ground represents those whose response is choked by [[v19|the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches, and the lusts of other things]]. Mark's list adds "desires for other things," a broad category that includes anything that competes with God for a person's heart. The good soil represents those who hear the word, accept it, and bear fruit. (This parable is studied in depth at Matthew 13:23.)

### The Lamp and the Measure (4:21–25)

Jesus continued with short sayings. A lamp is not brought into a room to be put under a basket or bed, but on a stand. Though the kingdom's truth is now partly hidden in parables, it is meant to be revealed. Nothing is hidden except to be brought into the open.

He then said, [[v24|Take heed what you hear]], and gave a principle about how listening works. This saying is studied below.

### The Seed Growing Secretly (4:26–29)

Mark alone records this parable. A man scatters seed on the ground. Night and day, whether he sleeps or gets up, the seed sprouts and grows, though he does not know how. The soil produces grain by itself: first the stalk, then the head, then the full kernel. When the grain is ripe, he puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come. This parable is studied below.

### The Mustard Seed (4:30–34)

Jesus asked how to describe God's kingdom. It is like a mustard seed, the smallest seed farmers commonly planted in Palestine. Yet when it grows, it becomes the largest of garden plants, with branches big enough for birds to nest in its shade. The kingdom's beginnings in Jesus' ministry looked small and insignificant, but it would grow into something great, offering shelter to many. The image of birds nesting in branches echoes Old Testament pictures of a great kingdom that shelters the nations (Ezekiel 17:23; Daniel 4:12).

Mark notes that Jesus spoke to the people with many parables like these, [[v33|as they were able to hear it]]. He adapted His teaching to what His hearers could receive. In private, He explained everything to His disciples.

### Calming the Storm (4:35–41)

That evening, Jesus said, [[v35|Let’s go over to the other side]]. They left the crowd and took Jesus along in the boat [[v36|even as he was]], suggesting He was tired after a long day of teaching. Other boats were with them. A violent windstorm arose, and the waves broke over the boat so that it was nearly swamped. The Sea of Galilee lies about seven hundred feet below sea level, surrounded by hills, and cool air rushing down through the valleys can suddenly stir up fierce storms.

Jesus was in the stern, [[v38|asleep on the cushion]]. Only Mark mentions the cushion, a vivid detail likely remembered by an eyewitness. The disciples woke Him and said, [[v38|Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?]] This question is studied below.

Jesus got up, rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, [[v39|Peace! Be still!]] The words literally mean "Be silent! Be muzzled!", the same kind of command He gave to demons (Mark 1:25). The wind stopped, and there was a great calm. Then He asked them, [[v40|Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?]]

The disciples were filled with a different kind of fear, awe at what they had witnessed. They asked one another, [[v41|Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?]] In the Old Testament, it is God who rules the raging sea and calms the storm (Psalm 89:9; 107:28–29). The disciples were beginning to realize that Jesus possessed the authority of God Himself. Their question echoes through Mark's Gospel until it is answered at the cross and the empty tomb.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 4:24 — Take Heed What You Hear

verse: Mark 4:24

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to His disciples as part of a series of short sayings following the parable of the sower. He has just explained that the farmer's seed is God's word, and that different kinds of hearers respond in different ways. He has also said that a lamp is meant to be put on a stand, not hidden, meaning that the truth of God's kingdom is meant to be revealed. Now He gives a warning and a promise about listening.

[[v24|Take heed what you hear]]. The phrase means "pay attention to what you are hearing" or "be careful how you listen." In Luke's version, Jesus says, [[Luke 8:18|Be careful therefore how you hear]]. Both emphases matter: what we listen to and how we listen.

Then Jesus uses a common saying from the marketplace: [[v24|With whatever measure you measure, it will be measured to you]]. In buying and selling grain, merchants used measuring containers. A generous merchant would fill the measure full; a stingy one would fill it loosely. Jesus applies this to listening. The attention and openness people give to God's word determine how much understanding they receive. Those who listen carefully and respond receive more.

Jesus adds, [[v24|and more will be given to you who hear]]. God is generous. Those who respond to His word receive even more than they give. The next verse explains: [[v25|For whoever has, to him more will be given; and he who doesn’t have, even that which he has will be taken away from him]]. Those who receive and use God's truth grow in understanding. Those who neglect it lose even what they once had.

In context, this saying explains the difference between the disciples and the outsiders. The disciples had drawn near to Jesus and wanted to understand, so they received more explanation. Those who only heard stories and did not seek further understanding remained outside.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that spiritual understanding grows through attentive, responsive listening. God's word is not meant to be heard passively. It calls for a response. When people listen with openness and act on what they hear, God gives them greater understanding. When people ignore what they hear, their hearts gradually become harder and their understanding fades.

This principle appears throughout Scripture. James writes that those who look into God's law and do what it says will be blessed, while those who hear without doing deceive themselves (James 1:22–25). Proverbs repeatedly urges the hearer to incline the ear and apply the heart to wisdom.

The verse also reveals God's generosity. He does not give exactly what people give Him; He gives more. Those who respond to His word, even in small ways, find that He multiplies their understanding and fruitfulness.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to be careful listeners. In a world full of voices, believers can pay attention to what they allow into their minds and hearts. They can prioritize listening to God's word through Scripture reading, preaching, and reflection, and they can be discerning about other influences that compete for their attention.

It also calls believers to listen with the intention of responding. Reading the Bible or hearing a sermon can become routine. Believers can approach God's word asking, "What is God saying to me, and how should I respond?" Acting on even small insights leads to greater understanding over time.

Finally, this verse warns against neglect. Faith that is not nourished and applied can weaken. Believers who have drifted from regular engagement with God's word can return, trusting that God is generous and will give more to those who seek Him.

### Mark 4:26–29 — The Seed That Grows by Itself

verse: Mark 4:26-29

#### Simple Explanation

This parable is found only in Mark's Gospel. It follows the parable of the sower and the sayings about the lamp and the measure. Like the sower, it uses the image of seed, but its emphasis is different.

A man [[v26|should cast seed on the earth]]. After planting, he goes on with his normal life: [[v27|and should sleep and rise night and day]]. Meanwhile, the seed sprouts and grows, [[v27|though he doesn’t know how]]. Ancient farmers did not understand the biology of plant growth. They knew that seeds grew, but the process was a mystery to them.

[[v28|For the earth bears fruit by itself]]. The Greek word translated "by itself" is "automate," from which we get "automatic." The growth happens without the farmer's direct effort. Jesus describes the stages: first the blade (the green shoot), then the ear (the head of grain), then the full grain in the ear. Growth happens gradually, step by step.

Finally, [[v29|But when the fruit is ripe, immediately he puts in the sickle, because the harvest has come]]. The farmer's role returns at the end: he harvests. The language of the sickle and harvest echoes Joel 3:13, where the harvest is an image of God's final judgment and the climax of His purposes.

Jesus' disciples may have been discouraged. The kingdom of God did not seem to be advancing quickly. Opposition was growing, and many people were not responding. This parable assured them that God's kingdom was growing, even when they could not see it or explain it.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This parable teaches that the growth of God's kingdom depends on God's power, not human effort. The farmer plants, but he cannot make the seed grow. Paul expresses the same truth: [[1 Corinthians 3:6|I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase]]. Believers are responsible to sow the word faithfully, but the results belong to God.

It also teaches that God's work is often hidden and gradual. Much growth happens underground, unseen. The kingdom does not always advance in dramatic, visible ways. Sometimes it grows quietly in hearts, families, and communities over long periods of time.

Finally, the parable points to a certain harvest. Growth is not endless; it moves toward a goal. God will bring His kingdom to completion, and there will be a final harvest at the end of the age. This gives believers both patience in the present and hope for the future.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This parable relieves believers of the pressure to produce spiritual results by their own power. Parents raising children, believers sharing their faith, teachers, pastors, and anyone who serves can plant seeds faithfully and then trust God with the growth. They can rest, as the farmer slept, knowing that God is at work even when they cannot see it.

It also encourages patience with spiritual growth, both in others and in oneself. Growth happens in stages: first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain. Believers can be patient with new Christians, with struggling friends, and with their own slow progress, trusting that God is working gradually toward maturity.

Finally, this parable encourages faithfulness in sowing. The farmer did not know how the seed grew, but he did plant it. Believers can keep sharing God's word, praying, serving, and living faithfully, confident that God will bring the harvest in His time.

### Mark 4:38 — "Teacher, Don't You Care?"

verse: Mark 4:38

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes in the middle of the storm on the Sea of Galilee. At the end of a long day of teaching, Jesus had told His disciples to cross to the other side of the lake. They set out in the evening, with other boats following. Suddenly a violent windstorm arose. The waves broke over the boat, and it was filling with water. Several of the disciples were experienced fishermen who knew this lake, and they were terrified.

Meanwhile, [[v38|He himself was in the stern, asleep on the cushion]]. The stern, at the back of the boat, was where the helmsman sat, and a cushion was sometimes kept there. Jesus was so exhausted, and so at peace, that He slept through the storm.

The disciples woke Him and said, [[v38|Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?]] Mark records the question in its rawest form. Matthew's version is a prayer, [[Matthew 8:25|Save us, Lord!]], and Luke's is a cry of alarm. Mark preserves the disciples' accusation: Jesus' sleep seemed to them like indifference. How could He rest while they were about to drown?

Jesus did not answer their question with words at first. He got up, rebuked the wind, and commanded the sea to be still, and there was a great calm. Then He asked them, [[v40|Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?]] His action answered their question. He did care, and He was in control all along.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse expresses a question that many believers ask in times of suffering: "God, don't You care?" When God seems silent or inactive while trouble rages, it can feel as though He is indifferent. The psalms are full of similar cries: [[Psalm 44:23|Wake up! Why do you sleep, Lord?]] Scripture does not hide these honest questions.

Jesus' response shows that His apparent inactivity was not indifference. He was present in the boat, sharing the same danger as His disciples. His rest reflected perfect trust in His Father, not lack of concern. When He acted, He showed that He had complete authority over the storm.

The story also teaches that fear and faith are closely connected. Jesus asked why they had no faith. Their fear came from forgetting who was in the boat with them. Faith does not mean storms will never come, but it means trusting that the One who is with us is greater than any storm.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse gives believers permission to bring honest questions to God. When life feels overwhelming and God seems silent, they can cry out to Him, even with the words, "Don't You care?" God is not offended by honest prayer. He invites His children to bring their fears to Him.

It also reminds believers that God's silence is not His absence. Jesus was in the boat the whole time. In seasons when God seems quiet, believers can remember His promises, His past faithfulness, and His presence with them, trusting that He cares even when they cannot feel it.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to grow in faith through storms. The disciples learned more about who Jesus was through this frightening experience. Believers can look back on hard seasons and see how God was present and powerful, building confidence for future storms.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 4 helps believers understand how God's kingdom works and how they should respond to it. The parable of the sower shows that the condition of the heart affects how people receive God's word. Believers can examine whether their hearts are hard, shallow, crowded with worries and desires, or open and fruitful. They can ask God to make them good soil, and they can deal honestly with whatever is choking their spiritual life.

The sayings about the lamp and the measure call believers to listen carefully and respond. God's truth is meant to be revealed, and those who receive it with attention and obedience are given more. Regular engagement with Scripture, combined with a willingness to act on what one hears, leads to growing understanding.

The parables of the growing seed and the mustard seed give hope and patience. God's kingdom grows by His power, often slowly and invisibly, but it will grow into something great and will reach its harvest. Believers can plant seeds faithfully in their families, workplaces, and communities, trusting God for the results and refusing to be discouraged by small beginnings or slow progress.

Finally, the storm on the lake shows that following Jesus does not mean avoiding storms. The disciples were in danger precisely because they obeyed His instruction to cross the lake. Yet Jesus was with them, and He had authority over the wind and waves. Believers can bring their fears and even their questions to Him, trusting that He cares and that He is greater than any storm they face. Each experience of His faithfulness can deepen their answer to the disciples' question: "Who then is this?"

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for sowing the word of Your kingdom in my heart. Make me good soil that receives Your word, holds onto it, and bears fruit. Clear away the worries, the deceitfulness of riches, and the desires for other things that choke my faith. Help me to listen carefully and to respond to what I hear.

Thank You that Your kingdom grows by Your power, even when I cannot see how. Give me patience with my own slow growth and with others, and help me to keep planting seeds faithfully, trusting You for the harvest. When storms rise and I wonder whether You care, remind me that You are with me in the boat and that the wind and sea obey You. Turn my fear into faith and my questions into worship. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
