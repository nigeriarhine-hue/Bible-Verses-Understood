# Mark 15
theme: The King Who Would Not Save Himself
main: Handed over to Pilate, Jesus stands silent while a crowd chooses the rebel Barabbas, and Pilate, wanting to please the crowd, sends Jesus to be flogged and crucified. Soldiers mock Him as king, a passerby named Simon carries His cross, and leaders taunt Him to save Himself. After crying out the words of Psalm 22, Jesus dies; the temple curtain tears and a Roman centurion confesses Him as the Son of God. Joseph of Arimathea boldly asks for His body and buries Him while faithful women watch.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 15 follows the night of Jesus' arrest and trial before the Jewish council, during which Peter denied Him three times (Mark 14). Early in the morning, the council finalized its decision. The Jewish leaders could condemn Jesus under their law, but Roman authority reserved the power of execution, so they needed Pilate's verdict. They brought Jesus to him with a charge that would matter to Rome: claiming to be "King of the Jews," which sounded like rebellion against Caesar.

Mark tells the story of the crucifixion with restraint and power. He does not dwell on physical details, which his readers knew all too well. Instead, he highlights the irony that runs through the chapter: Jesus is mocked as a king, yet He truly is the King; He is taunted to save Himself, but He stays on the cross precisely to save others; and the one who finally recognizes Him is not a disciple or a religious leader but the Roman officer in charge of His execution. (Matthew's parallel account is studied at Matthew 27, including the cry of abandonment, the torn curtain, and the centurion's confession.)

### Jesus Before Pilate (15:1–15)

Very early in the morning, the chief priests, elders, scribes, and the whole council reached their decision. They bound Jesus, led Him away, and handed Him over to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor of Judea from A.D. 26 to 36. Pilate usually lived in Caesarea on the coast but came to Jerusalem during festivals to maintain order.

Pilate asked, [[v2|Are you the King of the Jews?]] Jesus answered, [[v2|So you say]], affirming the title but suggesting that Pilate did not understand what kind of king He was. The chief priests accused Him of many things. Pilate asked whether He would answer, but Jesus made no further reply, and Pilate was amazed. His silence fulfilled Isaiah 53:7, which describes the Servant as silent before His accusers like a lamb led to slaughter.

At the festival, Pilate customarily released a prisoner chosen by the crowd. A man named Barabbas was in prison with rebels who had committed murder during an uprising. His name means "son of the father," an irony that early Christians noticed: the guilty "son of the father" was released, while the innocent Son of the Father was condemned.

The crowd came and asked Pilate to follow his custom. Pilate asked whether they wanted him to release "the King of the Jews," because he knew the chief priests had handed Jesus over out of envy. But the chief priests stirred up the crowd to ask for Barabbas. When Pilate asked what to do with Jesus, they shouted, [[v13|Crucify him!]] Pilate asked what evil He had done, but they shouted louder. Pilate, [[v15|wishing to please the multitude]], released Barabbas, had Jesus flogged, and handed Him over to be crucified. This verse is studied below.

Roman flogging was brutal. The victim was stripped and beaten with a whip of leather straps embedded with pieces of bone or metal. It often tore flesh to the bone and sometimes killed the prisoner before crucifixion.

### The Soldiers' Mockery (15:16–20)

The soldiers led Jesus into the courtyard of the palace, the Praetorium, and called together the whole cohort, a unit that could include several hundred men. They dressed Him in purple, the color of royalty, twisted together a crown of thorns, and put it on Him. They saluted Him, "Hail, King of the Jews!" struck His head with a reed, spat on Him, and knelt before Him in mock homage. Then they put His own clothes back on Him and led Him out to crucify Him.

The soldiers meant all of this as a cruel joke. But Mark's readers know that their words were true. Jesus is the King, and one day every knee will bow before Him in genuine worship (Philippians 2:10).

### Simon of Cyrene and the Crucifixion (15:21–32)

On the way, the soldiers forced a man passing by, Simon of Cyrene, to carry Jesus' cross. Cyrene was a city in North Africa with a significant Jewish population. Simon may have been a pilgrim in Jerusalem for Passover, [[v21|coming from the country]]. Roman soldiers had the right to compel civilians to carry loads. Jesus, weakened by the flogging, was apparently unable to carry the crossbeam the whole way.

Mark adds that Simon was [[v21|the father of Alexander and Rufus]]. This detail suggests that Mark's readers knew Simon's sons, who may have become members of the early church. Paul greets a Rufus in Rome (Romans 16:13), possibly the same person. If so, Simon's unwilling encounter with Jesus' cross may have led to his family's faith. Simon became a picture of discipleship: literally taking up the cross and following Jesus (Mark 8:34).

They brought Jesus to Golgotha, meaning "Place of a Skull," likely a rocky hill outside the city walls. They offered Him wine mixed with myrrh, which may have been intended to dull the pain, but He did not take it. He chose to face His suffering with full awareness.

They crucified Him and divided His clothes by casting lots, fulfilling Psalm 22:18. Mark notes, [[v25|It was the third hour when they crucified him]], about nine in the morning. The written charge above Him read, [[v26|THE KING OF THE JEWS]]. Two robbers (probably rebels, like Barabbas's companions) were crucified with Him, one on His right and one on His left. (Verse 28, quoting Isaiah 53:12, "He was counted with transgressors," is not found in many early manuscripts of Mark, though Luke 22:37 records Jesus applying this verse to Himself.)

People passing by insulted Him, shaking their heads, and taunting Him with His own words about destroying and rebuilding the temple: [[v30|save yourself, and come down from the cross!]] The chief priests and scribes also mocked Him among themselves: [[v31|He saved others. He can’t save himself]]. This verse is studied below. They added that if He came down from the cross, they would see and believe. Even the men crucified with Him insulted Him.

### The Death of Jesus (15:33–41)

At noon, darkness covered the whole land until three in the afternoon. At three o'clock, Jesus cried out with a loud voice, [[v34|Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?]], which means, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" These are the opening words of Psalm 22, spoken in Aramaic. (This cry is studied in depth at Matthew 27:46.) Some bystanders thought He was calling Elijah. One ran, filled a sponge with sour wine, put it on a reed, and offered it to Him, saying they should wait to see if Elijah would come to take Him down.

Then Jesus let out a loud cry and breathed His last. At that moment, the curtain of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom. Mark uses the same Greek verb for tearing that he used at Jesus' baptism, when Jesus saw the heavens parting (Mark 1:10). At the beginning and end of Jesus' ministry, a barrier between God and humanity is ripped apart. (The torn curtain is studied at Matthew 27:51.)

The centurion standing in front of Jesus saw how He died and said, [[v39|Truly this man was the Son of God!]] Mark's Gospel began by announcing "Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (1:1). The Father declared Him His Son at His baptism and transfiguration. Demons recognized Him as God's Son. But the first human being in Mark's Gospel to confess Him as the Son of God is a Gentile soldier watching Him die. Mark's message is clear: Jesus is most fully revealed as the Son of God on the cross.

Some women were watching from a distance, including Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James the younger and Joses, and Salome. These women had followed Jesus in Galilee and cared for His needs, and many other women had come up with Him to Jerusalem. The male disciples had fled, but the women remained. They would also witness His burial and become the first witnesses of His resurrection.

### The Burial (15:42–47)

It was the day of Preparation, the day before the Sabbath. The Sabbath began at sunset on Friday, and Jewish law required that bodies not remain on crosses overnight (Deuteronomy 21:22–23), especially on the Sabbath. Joseph of Arimathea, a prominent member of the council who was waiting for the kingdom of God, went boldly to Pilate and asked for Jesus' body. This is studied below.

Pilate was surprised that Jesus was already dead, since crucified men often lingered for days. He summoned the centurion to confirm it. When the centurion confirmed that Jesus was dead, Pilate gave the body to Joseph. This official confirmation is important: Jesus truly died. Joseph bought linen cloth, took the body down, wrapped it, and placed it in a tomb cut out of rock. He rolled a stone against the entrance. Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses saw where He was laid.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 15:15 — Wishing to Please the Crowd

verse: Mark 15:15

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records the moment Pilate condemned Jesus. Pilate had questioned Jesus and was amazed at His silence. He recognized that the chief priests had handed Jesus over [[v10|for envy]]. He asked the crowd whether they wanted him to release "the King of the Jews," apparently hoping they would choose Jesus. But the chief priests stirred up the crowd to demand Barabbas, a rebel involved in a murderous uprising. When Pilate asked what crime Jesus had committed, the crowd only shouted louder, "Crucify him!"

Mark then gives the reason for Pilate's decision: [[v15|Pilate, wishing to please the multitude]]. Pilate was not convinced Jesus was guilty. He knew the charges came from envy. But he wanted to satisfy the crowd. Roman governors were responsible for keeping peace in their provinces, and a riot during Passover, with Jerusalem crowded with pilgrims, could have threatened his position. Historical sources tell us that Pilate had already offended the Jewish population several times and had been reported to the emperor. He likely feared further trouble.

So Pilate [[v15|released Barabbas to them]], a man guilty of violent rebellion. Then he had Jesus flogged and [[v15|handed over Jesus, when he had flogged him, to be crucified]]. The innocent man was condemned, and the guilty man was released.

The phrase "handed over" appears repeatedly in Mark's passion narrative. Judas handed Jesus over to the chief priests (14:10), the chief priests handed Him over to Pilate (15:1), and Pilate handed Him over to be crucified (15:15). Each person passed responsibility to the next. Yet Jesus had predicted this very sequence (10:33), showing that it was also part of God's plan.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the danger of fearing people more than God. Pilate had the authority and the knowledge to release Jesus, but he chose to please the crowd. His decision reveals how the desire for approval, safety, or position can lead people to do what they know is wrong. Proverbs 29:25 warns, [[Proverbs 29:25|The fear of man proves to be a snare, but whoever puts his trust in the LORD is kept safe]].

The exchange of Barabbas for Jesus also carries a deep meaning. Barabbas, guilty of rebellion and murder, went free because Jesus took his place. Christians have long seen in this a picture of the gospel: Jesus, the innocent one, was condemned so that the guilty could go free. Peter later reminded the people of Jerusalem that they had [[Acts 3:14|denied the Holy and Righteous One and asked for a murderer to be granted to you]], yet he called them to repentance and forgiveness.

Finally, the verse shows that human responsibility and God's plan work together. Pilate, the chief priests, and the crowd were responsible for their choices, yet God was working through these events to accomplish salvation (Acts 2:23).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to examine whether they are ruled by the desire to please others. Many people make decisions based on what will keep others happy, avoid conflict, or protect their reputation, even when they know it is wrong. Believers can ask God for the courage to do what is right, trusting Him with the consequences.

It also challenges those in positions of authority. Leaders in families, workplaces, churches, and communities often face pressure to make popular decisions rather than just ones. Pilate's example shows the cost of choosing popularity over justice. Believers in leadership can seek to protect the innocent and act with integrity.

Finally, this verse invites believers to see themselves in Barabbas. Everyone is guilty before God, and Jesus took the place of the guilty. This should lead to gratitude and humility, and to a willingness to extend grace to others, since all have been freed by the same Savior.

### Mark 15:31 — [[Mark 15:31|He Saved Others; He Can't Save Himself]]

verse: Mark 15:31

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records the mockery of the chief priests and scribes as Jesus hung on the cross. Throughout the crucifixion, Jesus was insulted from every side. People passing by shook their heads and taunted Him to save Himself and come down from the cross. The religious leaders joined in, speaking [[v31|among themselves]], mocking Him to one another.

They said, [[v31|He saved others. He can’t save himself]]. The word "saved" can mean rescued, healed, or delivered. The leaders admitted, perhaps sarcastically, that Jesus had saved others. Throughout Mark's Gospel, Jesus had healed the sick, cast out demons, and raised the dead. The word "save" was often used for these healings (Mark 5:34; 10:52). Now, the leaders said, He could not save Himself.

They continued, [[v32|Let the Christ, the King of Israel, now come down from the cross, that we may see and believe him]]. They used His titles mockingly. They claimed that if He performed one more miracle, coming down from the cross, they would believe. But they had already seen many miracles and had refused to believe.

The tragic irony is that their mockery spoke a profound truth. Jesus could have saved Himself. He said in Gethsemane that the Father could send legions of angels (Matthew 26:53). But if He saved Himself, He could not save others. He stayed on the cross precisely because He was saving others. His refusal to come down was not weakness but love.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of the gospel. Jesus came [[Mark 10:45|to give his life as a ransom for many]]. Saving others required Him to give up His own life. If He had come down from the cross, there would have been no sacrifice for sin, no ransom, and no salvation.

It also shows the difference between human and divine power. The leaders thought that power meant self-preservation and spectacular displays. Jesus revealed that God's power is shown in self-giving love. Paul writes that the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to those being saved, it is the power of God (1 Corinthians 1:18).

Finally, the demand "come down, and we will believe" echoes Satan's temptation in the wilderness and Peter's rebuke at Caesarea Philippi. The temptation to avoid the cross followed Jesus to the very end. Each time, He chose the Father's will.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to deep gratitude. Jesus could have saved Himself, but He chose to stay on the cross to save them. Every believer's salvation depended on His refusal to come down. Remembering this can transform how believers view their own worth and God's love.

It also calls believers to follow Jesus' example of self-giving love. In small and large ways, loving others often requires giving up self-protection, comfort, or advantage. Parents sacrifice for children, friends give time for one another, and believers serve those in need. Jesus' example shows that true love is willing to lose in order to help others.

Finally, this verse challenges the demand for proof on our own terms. The leaders said they would believe if Jesus came down from the cross. Many people today say they would believe if God did something spectacular for them. But faith rests on what God has already done, especially in the cross and resurrection of Jesus.

### Mark 15:43 — Joseph's Bold Request

verse: Mark 15:43

#### Simple Explanation

This verse introduces Joseph of Arimathea, who appears in all four Gospels only at Jesus' burial. Jesus had died on the cross around three in the afternoon. It was Friday, the day of Preparation before the Sabbath. The Sabbath would begin at sunset, and Jewish law required that executed bodies be buried before nightfall (Deuteronomy 21:22–23). Without intervention, Jesus' body might have been left on the cross or thrown into a common grave, as often happened with crucified criminals.

Mark describes Joseph in three ways. First, he was from [[v43|Arimathaea]], a town in Judea. Second, he was [[v43|a prominent council member]], a respected member of the Sanhedrin, the council that had just condemned Jesus. Luke tells us that he had not agreed with their decision (Luke 23:51). Third, he [[v43|also himself was looking for God’s Kingdom]], meaning he was waiting expectantly for God to establish His reign. John tells us he was a secret disciple of Jesus because he feared the Jewish leaders (John 19:38).

Then Mark says, [[v43|He boldly went in to Pilate, and asked for Jesus’ body]]. The word "boldly" (literally "having dared") shows that this was a risky act. Approaching the Roman governor to request the body of a man executed for treason could raise suspicion that Joseph was a sympathizer. Identifying with Jesus could also cost him his standing among the council members who had just condemned Him. Touching a corpse would make him ritually unclean during Passover. Yet Joseph acted.

Pilate, surprised that Jesus had died so quickly, confirmed His death with the centurion and then gave the body to Joseph. Joseph bought linen, took the body down, wrapped it, and laid it in a tomb cut from rock.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus' death moved a secret disciple to public courage. While Jesus was alive, Joseph had kept his faith hidden. But after the crucifixion, when the disciples had fled and it seemed that Jesus' mission had failed, Joseph stepped forward. The cross, which scattered many followers, drew him into the open.

It also shows God's care for His Son even in death. Isaiah 53:9 says the Servant would be [[Isaiah 53:9|with a rich man in his death]]. Through Joseph, Jesus received an honorable burial in a new tomb rather than a criminal's grave. The details of the burial also confirm that Jesus truly died, which is essential for the reality of His resurrection.

Finally, Joseph is described as someone "looking for God's kingdom." His action of love came from hope, even when that hope seemed shattered. He did not know that in three days Jesus would rise, but he honored Him anyway.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers who have kept their faith hidden to step forward. Many people believe in Jesus but are afraid to identify with Him publicly because of what family, friends, or colleagues might think. Joseph's example shows that it is never too late to take a stand for Christ.

It also shows that faithfulness sometimes means acting when others have given up. When the disciples had scattered, Joseph and the women remained. Believers can show faithfulness in difficult times, serving and honoring Christ even when circumstances seem hopeless.

Finally, Joseph's example invites believers to use their resources and position for Christ. Joseph used his influence to approach Pilate and his wealth to provide a tomb. Believers can ask how their own resources, connections, and abilities can be used to honor Jesus and serve others.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 15 confronts readers with the cross of Jesus and invites them to consider their response. The chapter shows many responses: the leaders' envy, Pilate's cowardice, the crowd's manipulation, the soldiers' mockery, the bystanders' scorn, the centurion's confession, the women's faithful presence, and Joseph's bold love. Each reader can ask where they stand.

Pilate's decision warns against letting the desire to please others override what is right. Believers face many pressures to go along with the crowd. The fear of people can lead to injustice and compromise. Believers can choose to fear God rather than people, trusting Him with the consequences of doing right.

The exchange of Barabbas and the mocking cry, "He saved others; he cannot save himself," reveal the heart of the gospel. Jesus took the place of the guilty and refused to save Himself so that He could save others. Believers can respond with gratitude, humility, and a willingness to love others in self-giving ways.

The centurion's confession shows that the cross is where Jesus is most clearly revealed as the Son of God. Believers can return again and again to the cross to understand God's love and Jesus' identity. Finally, the women and Joseph show faithfulness when others had fled. Simon of Cyrene, who carried the cross, may have become a believer with his family. Believers can follow these examples: staying near Jesus in hard times, taking up their cross, and stepping out publicly in love for Him.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You stood silent before Your accusers, were rejected in favor of a guilty man, and were mocked as a king. You could have saved Yourself, but You stayed on the cross to save me. I thank You with all my heart. Like Barabbas, I am guilty, and You took my place.

Keep me from Pilate's fear of the crowd, and give me courage to do what is right even when it is unpopular. Help me to take up my cross and follow You, as Simon carried Yours. Like the centurion, I confess that You are truly the Son of God. Give me the faithfulness of the women who stayed near You and the boldness of Joseph, who stepped forward to honor You. Help me to live in grateful love for the King who would not save Himself. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
