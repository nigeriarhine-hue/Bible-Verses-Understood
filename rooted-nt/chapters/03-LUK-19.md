# Luke 19
theme: Seeking the Lost and Weeping Over the City
main: In Jericho, Jesus invites Himself to the home of Zacchaeus, a rich chief tax collector, whose encounter with Jesus leads him to give generously and repay those he cheated; Jesus declares that He came to seek and save the lost. Through the parable of the minas, He teaches faithful service while His followers wait for His kingdom. As He enters Jerusalem to the praise of His disciples, He weeps over the city that did not recognize the time of God's visitation, and He cleanses the temple.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 19 brings Jesus' long journey to Jerusalem, which began in Luke 9:51, to its destination. Jesus passes through Jericho, about fifteen miles northeast of Jerusalem, and then climbs the steep road to the city, arriving at the Mount of Olives. From there He descends into Jerusalem for the final week of His earthly life.

The chapter contains one of the most beloved stories in Luke, the conversion of Zacchaeus, and ends with Jesus' lament over Jerusalem and the cleansing of the temple. Throughout the chapter, Jesus appears as the seeking Savior, the coming King, and the grieving Lord.

### Zacchaeus (19:1–10)

Jesus entered Jericho and was passing through. Jericho was a wealthy city on an important trade route, with palm groves and balsam plantations, and it was a center for collecting taxes and customs. A man named Zacchaeus lived there. He was [[v2|a chief tax collector, and he was rich]]. As a chief tax collector, he supervised other tax collectors and likely profited from their work as well as his own. Tax collectors bid for the right to collect taxes for the Roman authorities and made their money by charging more than was required. Zacchaeus had become rich this way, and his neighbors would have despised him as a traitor and a thief. His name, ironically, comes from a Hebrew word meaning "pure" or "righteous."

Zacchaeus wanted to see who Jesus was, but he was short and could not see over the crowd. So he ran ahead and climbed a sycamore-fig tree along the route. Sycamore-figs had low, spreading branches that were easy to climb. For a wealthy, important man, running and climbing a tree was undignified, showing how eager he was.

When Jesus reached the spot, He looked up and said, [[v5|Zacchaeus, hurry and come down, for today I must stay at your house]]. Jesus called him by name, though they had apparently never met. He invited Himself to Zacchaeus's home, saying He "must" stay there, the same word Luke uses for divine necessity in Jesus' mission. Zacchaeus hurried down and welcomed Him joyfully.

But the crowd grumbled, [[v7|He has gone in to lodge with a man who is a sinner]]. As in Luke 15:2, people were upset that Jesus associated with sinners.

Then Zacchaeus stood up and made a remarkable declaration, studied below. Jesus responded, [[v9|Today, salvation has come to this house, because he also is a son of Abraham]]. Despite his sins, Zacchaeus was a descendant of Abraham, part of God's covenant people, and he had now shown the faith of Abraham. Then Jesus summarized His whole mission in one sentence, also studied below: [[v10|For the Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost]].

Notice the contrast with the rich ruler in Luke 18:18–23. That ruler was respected and law-abiding but went away sad because he would not give up his wealth. Zacchaeus was despised and dishonest, yet he gave much of his wealth away with joy. What seemed impossible for the rich, entering God's kingdom (Luke 18:24–27), became possible by God's grace.

### The Parable of the Minas (19:11–27)

While the people were listening, Jesus told a parable, [[v11|because he was near Jerusalem, and they supposed that God’s Kingdom would be revealed immediately]]. Many expected that when Jesus reached Jerusalem, He would immediately establish His kingdom in power. Jesus told a parable to correct this expectation.

A nobleman went to a distant country to be appointed king and then return. This would have reminded listeners of Archelaus, a son of Herod the Great, who traveled to Rome in 4 B.C. to have his kingship confirmed by Caesar. A delegation of Jews followed him to Rome to oppose his appointment. When Archelaus returned, he took revenge on his opponents.

Before leaving, the nobleman gave ten servants one mina each and told them, [[v13|Conduct business until I come]]. A mina was worth about a hundred days' wages, a significant but not enormous sum, much less than the talents in Matthew's similar parable (Matthew 25:14–30). Meanwhile, his citizens hated him and sent a delegation after him saying, [[v14|We don’t want this man to reign over us]].

When the nobleman returned as king, he called the servants to account. The first had earned ten more minas, and the king said, [[v17|Well done, you good servant! Because you were found faithful with very little, you shall have authority over ten cities]]. The second had earned five and was given five cities. A third had kept his mina wrapped in a cloth because he feared the king as a harsh man. The king condemned him from his own words: if he believed the king was harsh, he should at least have put the money in the bank to earn interest. The king took his mina and gave it to the one with ten. Finally, the king ordered that his enemies who did not want him to reign be brought and killed before him.

The parable taught several lessons. First, the kingdom would not appear immediately; there would be a period of waiting while the King was away. Second, during that time, His servants must faithfully use what He has entrusted to them. Third, the King would return and hold everyone accountable. Fourth, those who rejected His rule would face judgment. The parable anticipated both Jesus' ascension and return, and the rejection He was about to face in Jerusalem. (A similar parable is studied at Matthew 25:21.)

### The Triumphal Entry (19:28–40)

After telling the parable, Jesus went on ahead, going up to Jerusalem. Near Bethphage and Bethany on the Mount of Olives, He sent two disciples to bring a colt that had never been ridden, telling them to say, [[v31|The Lord needs it]]. Everything happened as He said. They put their cloaks on the colt and set Jesus on it, fulfilling Zechariah 9:9, and people spread their cloaks on the road.

As He came to the descent of the Mount of Olives, from which the whole city of Jerusalem and the temple come into view, [[v37|the whole multitude of the disciples began to rejoice and praise God with a loud voice for all the mighty works which they had seen]]. They shouted, [[v38|Blessed is the King who comes in the name of the Lord! Peace in heaven, and glory in the highest!]] Luke's wording echoes the angels' song at Jesus' birth (Luke 2:14). Luke emphasizes that Jesus is "the King," connecting with the parable just told.

Some Pharisees in the crowd told Jesus to rebuke His disciples. They may have feared that such royal language would provoke Roman retaliation, or they may have objected to the claim itself. Jesus answered, [[v40|I tell you that if these were silent, the stones would cry out]]. Creation itself would proclaim the King if His people were silent.

### Jesus Weeps Over Jerusalem (19:41–44)

As He came near and saw the city, Jesus wept over it. This scene, found only in Luke, is studied below. Jesus predicted that enemies would build an embankment around the city, surround it, crush it and its children, and not leave one stone on another, [[v44|because you didn’t know the time of your visitation]]. This was fulfilled in A.D. 70, when Roman armies besieged and destroyed Jerusalem.

### Cleansing the Temple (19:45–48)

Jesus entered the temple and began driving out those who were selling, saying, [[v46|It is written, ‘My house is a house of prayer,’ but you have made it a ‘den of robbers’!]] (This event is studied at Matthew 21:13 and Mark 11:13–14.) He taught in the temple every day. The chief priests, scribes, and leaders of the people were trying to kill Him, but they could not find a way, [[v48|for all the people hung on to every word that he said]].

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 19:8 — Zacchaeus's Changed Heart

verse: Luke 19:8

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records Zacchaeus's response after Jesus invited Himself to his home. Zacchaeus was a chief tax collector in Jericho, a wealthy man who had gained much of his wealth by overcharging people. His neighbors despised him. When Jesus came to town, Zacchaeus climbed a tree to see Him. To everyone's surprise, Jesus stopped, called him by name, and said He must stay at his house. Zacchaeus welcomed Him joyfully, while the crowd grumbled that Jesus was going to be the guest of a sinner.

Then [[v8|Zacchaeus stood and said to the Lord]]. Standing suggests a formal, public declaration. He addressed Jesus as "Lord," showing respect and submission.

[[v8|Behold, Lord, half of my goods I give to the poor]]. This was extraordinary generosity. Jewish teachers of that time often recommended giving no more than a fifth of one's possessions to charity to avoid becoming poor oneself. Zacchaeus gave half.

[[v8|If I have wrongfully exacted anything of anyone, I restore four times as much]]. "If" here does not suggest doubt; Zacchaeus knew he had cheated people. The phrase means "whomever I have defrauded." The law of Moses required someone who had stolen and confessed to repay the amount plus one-fifth (Leviticus 6:1–5; Numbers 5:6–7). Fourfold restitution was required only in certain cases of theft, such as stealing a sheep (Exodus 22:1). Zacchaeus went far beyond what the law required.

Notice that Jesus did not demand any of this. Zacchaeus responded freely to Jesus' grace. Jesus' acceptance came first; Zacchaeus's transformation followed. The man who had been known for taking now became known for giving.

Jesus declared, [[v9|Today, salvation has come to this house]]. Zacchaeus's generosity did not earn salvation; it was the evidence that salvation had arrived.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows what genuine repentance looks like. It is not merely feeling sorry or saying the right words. It involves concrete changes: generosity to the poor and restitution to those one has wronged. John the Baptist had told tax collectors to collect no more than required (Luke 3:12–13); Zacchaeus went much further.

It also shows the transforming power of grace. Zacchaeus was not changed by condemnation or lectures. He was changed by Jesus' acceptance. Jesus called him by name and chose to stay in his home, despite the crowd's disapproval. This undeserved welcome melted Zacchaeus's heart. Paul writes that God's kindness leads people to repentance (Romans 2:4).

Finally, the verse shows that salvation affects how people handle money. Many of Jesus' teachings in Luke deal with wealth. The rich fool hoarded; the rich man ignored Lazarus; the rich ruler could not let go. Zacchaeus, transformed by Jesus, used his wealth to bless others and make things right.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to let their faith change how they use money. Generosity to the poor and fairness in financial dealings are signs of a heart transformed by Christ. Believers can ask how their encounter with Jesus should shape their giving and spending.

It also calls believers to make restitution where possible. If they have wronged others, whether by cheating, stealing, lying, or harming them, repentance may include making things right: apologizing, repaying, or repairing damage. This shows that repentance is real.

Finally, this verse shows believers how to help others change. Zacchaeus was transformed not by judgment but by grace. Believers can show acceptance and love to people whom others despise, trusting that God's grace can change hearts.

### Luke 19:10 — To Seek and Save the Lost

verse: Luke 19:10

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is Jesus' explanation of why He went to the house of Zacchaeus. The crowd had grumbled that Jesus was staying with a sinner. Zacchaeus had responded to Jesus' grace by giving half his possessions to the poor and promising to repay fourfold anyone he had cheated. Jesus declared that salvation had come to his house because he was a son of Abraham.

Then Jesus explained His mission: [[v10|For the Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost]].

[[v10|the Son of Man]] is Jesus' favorite title for Himself, drawn from Daniel 7:13–14, where one like a son of man receives an everlasting kingdom. The glorious Son of Man came on a mission of rescue.

[[v10|came]]. This word implies that Jesus came from somewhere, sent with a purpose. His coming was intentional.

[[v10|to seek]]. Jesus actively searches for lost people. He did not wait for Zacchaeus to come to Him; He stopped, looked up, called him by name, and invited Himself to his home. This echoes the parables of Luke 15, where the shepherd searches for the lost sheep and the woman searches for the lost coin.

[[v10|and to save]]. Jesus not only finds the lost; He rescues them. Salvation includes forgiveness, restoration to God, and transformation, as seen in Zacchaeus's life.

[[v10|that which was lost]]. "Lost" describes people who are separated from God, wandering, and unable to find their way back on their own. In Ezekiel 34, God criticized Israel's leaders for not seeking the lost sheep and promised, [[Ezekiel 34:16|I will seek that which was lost]]. Jesus fulfills this promise.

This verse summarizes the whole of Luke's Gospel. From the beginning, Jesus came for the poor, the outcast, sinners, and the lost. He welcomed tax collectors, touched lepers, defended a sinful woman, and told stories of a seeking shepherd and a waiting father.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of Jesus' mission. He did not come primarily to condemn, to establish a political kingdom, or to reward the righteous. He came to seek and save the lost. This is the good news: God takes the initiative to rescue people who cannot rescue themselves.

It also reveals that everyone is in some sense lost apart from Christ. Zacchaeus was obviously lost, but the respectable rich ruler was also lost, unable to let go of his wealth. The grumbling crowd, who looked down on Zacchaeus, needed to see that they too needed a Savior.

Finally, the verse defines the mission of Jesus' followers. If Jesus came to seek and save the lost, His people are called to join Him in that mission, reaching out to those far from God with the same love and initiative He showed.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse offers hope to anyone who feels lost. Jesus is seeking them. They do not need to find their own way back to God; Jesus comes looking for them, calls them by name, and invites them into relationship.

It also challenges believers to share Jesus' mission. They can look for the "Zacchaeuses" in their communities, people who are despised, overlooked, or far from God, and reach out to them with kindness and the good news. This may mean going where respectable people would not go, as Jesus did.

Finally, this verse warns against the attitude of the grumbling crowd. Believers can resist the temptation to judge those Jesus seeks, and instead rejoice when the lost are found.

### Luke 19:41–42 — Jesus Weeps Over Jerusalem

verse: Luke 19:41-42

#### Simple Explanation

This scene, found only in Luke, takes place during Jesus' entry into Jerusalem. Jesus was riding a colt down the Mount of Olives, and a crowd of disciples was joyfully praising God, shouting, "Blessed is the King who comes in the name of the Lord!" It was a moment of celebration.

Then [[v41|When he came near, he saw the city and wept over it]]. From the Mount of Olives, the whole city of Jerusalem spreads out below, with the temple prominent in view. In the middle of the celebration, Jesus wept. The word used means loud, audible weeping, not just quiet tears. It is a different and stronger word than the one used when Jesus wept at Lazarus's tomb (John 11:35).

Jesus said, [[v42|If you, even you, had known today the things which belong to your peace!]] The name "Jerusalem" is often understood to mean "city of peace." Yet the city did not recognize the way to true peace, which was standing before it in Jesus. The crowds had just shouted "peace in heaven," but Jerusalem as a whole, especially its leaders, would reject the King who brought peace with God.

[[v42|But now, they are hidden from your eyes]]. Because they had refused to recognize Jesus, the truth was now hidden from them. Repeated rejection had led to spiritual blindness.

In the following verses, Jesus predicted the city's destruction: enemies would build embankments, surround the city, crush its people, and leave not one stone on another, [[v44|because you didn’t know the time of your visitation]]. "Visitation" refers to God coming to His people, which happened in Jesus. Zechariah had sung that God had visited His people (Luke 1:68). But Jerusalem did not recognize the visit. This prophecy was fulfilled in A.D. 70, when Roman armies besieged and destroyed Jerusalem.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of Jesus toward those who reject Him. He was about to be betrayed, condemned, and crucified in this city. Yet He did not respond with anger or satisfaction at their coming judgment. He wept. God's judgment is real, but He takes no pleasure in it. Through Ezekiel, God said, [[Ezekiel 33:11|I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked]].

It also shows the seriousness of missing God's visitation. Jerusalem had the Scriptures, the temple, and the prophets, and then the Messiah Himself came. But they did not recognize Him. Privilege brings responsibility, and rejecting God's gracious visit has consequences.

Finally, Jesus' tears show that He fully shared human emotion. He was not a detached figure. He felt deep grief over the city He loved, the city that would kill Him. His love for people continued even as they rejected Him.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to recognize the times when God visits them. God works in people's lives through His Word, His Spirit, circumstances, and other people. Believers can be attentive to God's work and respond rather than letting opportunities pass.

They also call believers to share Jesus' heart for people who reject Him. It is easy to become angry or dismissive toward those who oppose the gospel. Jesus wept over them. Believers can pray with compassion for their communities, cities, and nations, grieving over sin and longing for people to find peace with God.

Finally, these verses remind believers that peace comes through Jesus. Many people seek peace through politics, wealth, achievement, or distraction. True peace, with God and within, is found in recognizing and receiving Jesus. Believers can point others to Him as the source of the peace they seek.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 19 brings together the seeking love of Jesus and the seriousness of how people respond to Him. Zacchaeus shows that no one is too lost or too despised for Jesus to seek. Jesus called him by name, entered his home, and transformed his life. Believers can take comfort that Jesus seeks them, and they can join His mission of seeking the lost, reaching out to people others avoid.

Zacchaeus's response also shows what real repentance looks like: generosity to the poor and restitution to those one has wronged. Believers can allow their encounter with Jesus to change how they handle money and how they make things right with others.

The parable of the minas reminds believers that they live in a time of waiting between Jesus' ascension and His return. During this time, they are called to faithfully use what He has entrusted to them. They will give an account when the King returns. Faithfulness in small things leads to greater responsibility in His kingdom.

The triumphal entry shows that Jesus is the King who comes in the name of the Lord, deserving praise even if the stones must cry out. And His tears over Jerusalem show His grief over those who reject Him and miss the time of God's visitation. Believers can welcome Jesus as King, recognize God's work in their lives, and share His compassion for people who do not yet know the way of peace.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for seeking me as You sought Zacchaeus, calling me by name and coming into my life. Thank You that You came to seek and to save the lost. Let Your grace change my heart as it changed his, so that I give generously and make things right with those I have wronged.

Help me to be faithful with everything You have entrusted to me while I wait for Your return. I welcome You as the King who comes in the name of the Lord. Give me Your heart for those who do not yet know You, a heart that weeps over their lostness rather than condemning them. Help me to recognize when You are working in my life, and keep me from missing the time of Your visitation. Lead me and many others into the things that make for peace. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
