# Romans 15
theme: Welcome One Another as Christ Welcomed You, and Abound in Hope
main: Paul calls the strong to bear with the weaknesses of the weak and to please their neighbors for their good, following the example of Christ, who did not please Himself. The Scriptures were written to give endurance, encouragement, and hope, and Paul prays that the church will live in harmony and glorify God with one voice. He urges Jews and Gentiles to welcome one another as Christ welcomed them, showing from Scripture that Christ came to confirm God's promises to Israel and to bring the Gentiles to praise God. Paul prays that the God of hope will fill them with joy and peace. He then explains his ministry as a priestly service among the Gentiles, his plans to visit Rome on his way to Spain after delivering a gift to Jerusalem, and asks for their prayers.

## Chapter Background and Story

Romans 15 completes Paul's discussion of the "strong" and the "weak" begun in chapter 14, and then moves into the conclusion of the letter. Paul shares his plans, explains the purpose of his ministry, and asks for prayer. The chapter shows the heart of a pastor and missionary: deeply concerned for unity in the church, passionate about reaching those who have never heard of Christ, and humbly dependent on the prayers of others.

### Bearing with the Weak (15:1–6)

Paul's call to the strong, grounded in the example of Christ, is studied below. His teaching on the purpose of Scripture is also studied below.

Paul then prays: [[v5|Now the God of perseverance and of encouragement grant you to be of the same mind with one another according to Christ Jesus]], [[v6|that with one accord you may with one mouth glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ]]. Unity is not merely for the church's comfort; it is for God's glory. When believers who differ in background and conviction praise God together, they display His power to unite people in Christ.

### Christ, Servant of Jews and Gentiles (15:7–13)

Paul's command to welcome one another is studied below. He explains that [[v8|Christ has been made a servant of the circumcision for the truth of God, that he might confirm the promises given to the fathers]], [[v9|and that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy]]. Jesus came as a Jew and ministered mainly to Jews, fulfilling God's promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. And through Him, the Gentiles are brought to praise God for His mercy.

Paul supports this with four Old Testament quotations, drawn from all parts of the Hebrew Scriptures: the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings. [[v9|Therefore I will give praise to you among the Gentiles and sing to your name]] (Psalm 18:49; 2 Samuel 22:50). [[v10|Rejoice, you Gentiles, with his people]] (Deuteronomy 32:43). [[v11|Praise the Lord, all you Gentiles! Let all the peoples praise him]] (Psalm 117:1). [[v12|There will be the root of Jesse, he who arises to rule over the Gentiles; in him the Gentiles will hope]] (Isaiah 11:10). Jesse was the father of David; the [[Isaiah 11:10|root of Jesse]] is the Messiah from David's line. The whole Old Testament anticipates the day when Gentiles would join Israel in praising God.

Paul's prayer for hope is studied below.

### Paul's Ministry to the Gentiles (15:14–21)

Paul expresses confidence in the Roman believers: [[v14|I myself am also persuaded about you, my brothers, that you yourselves are full of goodness, filled with all knowledge, able also to admonish others]]. He has written boldly as a reminder, because of the grace God gave him [[v16|that I should be a servant of Christ Jesus to the Gentiles, serving as a priest of the Good News of God, that the offering up of the Gentiles might be made acceptable, sanctified by the Holy Spirit]]. Paul uses priestly language. Just as a priest offered sacrifices to God, Paul's ministry was like presenting the Gentiles themselves as an offering to God, made holy by the Holy Spirit.

Paul boasts only in what Christ has done through him: [[v18|For I will not dare to speak of any things except those which Christ worked through me for the obedience of the Gentiles, by word and deed]], [[v19|in the power of signs and wonders, in the power of God’s Spirit; so that from Jerusalem and around as far as to Illyricum, I have fully preached the Good News of Christ]]. Illyricum was a Roman province on the eastern coast of the Adriatic Sea, roughly modern-day Albania and the former Yugoslavia. Paul had planted churches in a vast arc stretching about 1,400 miles.

Paul's guiding principle: [[v20|yes, making it my aim to preach the Good News, not where Christ was already named, that I might not build on another’s foundation]]. Paul was a pioneer. He wanted to take the gospel to places where people had never heard of Christ. He quotes Isaiah 52:15: [[v21|They will see, to whom no news of him came. They who haven’t heard will understand]].

### Paul's Travel Plans (15:22–33)

Paul had often been prevented from visiting Rome because he was busy preaching in new areas. But now that his work in the eastern Mediterranean was complete, he planned to visit Rome on his way to Spain. [[v24|whenever I travel to Spain, I will come to you. For I hope to see you on my journey, and to be helped on my way there by you]]. Spain was at the western edge of the known world. Paul hoped the Roman church would become a base of support for his mission there.

But first, Paul was going to Jerusalem with the collection from the churches of Macedonia and Achaia for the poor believers there. [[v27|Yes, it has been their good pleasure, and they are their debtors. For if the Gentiles have been made partakers of their spiritual things, they owe it to them also to serve them in material things]]. The gift expressed the unity of Jewish and Gentile believers.

Paul asked for prayer: [[v30|Now I beg you, brothers, by our Lord Jesus Christ and by the love of the Spirit, that you strive together with me in your prayers to God for me]]. The word "strive" suggests an athlete straining or a soldier fighting. Prayer is hard work. Paul asked them to pray that he would be rescued from unbelievers in Judea, that his gift would be accepted by the Jerusalem believers, and that he would come to Rome with joy.

God answered these prayers, though not in the way Paul expected. He was arrested in Jerusalem, but his life was preserved (Acts 21–23). He did come to Rome, but as a prisoner (Acts 28). Whether he ever reached Spain is uncertain; some early Christian writers suggest that he did, after being released from his first Roman imprisonment. Paul's plans were held in God's hands, as all believers' plans are.

The chapter ends with a blessing: [[v33|Now the God of peace be with you all. Amen]].

## Important Verses Explained

### Romans 15:1–3 — Christ Did Not Please Himself

verse: Romans 15:1-3

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been addressing disagreements in the Roman church between the "strong," who felt free to eat anything and treat all days alike, and the "weak," whose consciences led them to avoid certain foods and observe special days. In chapter 14, he urged both groups to stop judging and despising each other. Now he speaks especially to the strong.

[[v1|Now we who are strong ought to bear the weaknesses of the weak, and not to please ourselves]]. Paul includes himself among the strong. He agreed that believers are free in these matters. But strength brings responsibility. The strong should "bear" the weaknesses of the weak, carrying their burdens patiently, like someone helping a person who cannot walk alone. They should not insist on their own preferences or rights.

[[v2|Let each one of us please his neighbor for that which is good, to be building him up]]. Instead of pleasing themselves, believers should seek to please their neighbors. This does not mean flattering people or doing whatever others want. It means seeking their "good," their true benefit, and "building them up" in faith.

[[v3|For even Christ didn’t please himself]]. Paul gives the ultimate example: Christ. Jesus did not live to please Himself. He came to serve, to give His life for others. He set aside His own rights and comfort for the sake of others.

[[v3|But, as it is written, “The reproaches of those who reproached you fell on me.”]] Paul quotes Psalm 69:9. In this psalm, a righteous sufferer says that the insults directed at God fell on him. Paul applies it to Christ, who bore the insults and hostility of those who rejected God. Christ took on suffering for the sake of others. If He did not please Himself, His followers should not insist on pleasing themselves.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that Christian strength is expressed in service. In the world, the strong often use their strength to dominate others. In Christ's kingdom, the strong use their strength to support the weak. Paul writes, [[Galatians 6:2|Bear one another’s burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ]].

They also show that Christ is the pattern for Christian behavior. Paul repeatedly points to Christ's example of self-giving love. He writes to the Philippians, [[Philippians 2:4|each of you not just looking to his own things, but each of you also to the things of others]], and then describes how Christ humbled Himself.

Finally, these verses show that love seeks the good of others, not merely their approval. Pleasing one's neighbor "for that which is good" means caring about what truly helps them, which sometimes means gently challenging them, but always with love.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers who are mature in faith to be patient with those who are still growing. Strong believers can set aside their own preferences to support others, rather than insisting on their freedom.

They also encourage believers to ask, "How can I build up others?" rather than "What do I want?" In relationships at home, at church, and at work, believers can look for ways to strengthen and encourage others.

Finally, these verses call believers to follow Christ's example. When believers are tempted to please themselves, they can remember that Christ did not please Himself but gave His life for others.

### Romans 15:4 — Written for Our Learning

verse: Romans 15:4

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had just quoted Psalm 69:9 to show that Christ did not please Himself. Now he explains why he quotes the Old Testament so often.

[[v4|For whatever things were written before were written for our learning]]. The Scriptures written "before," the Old Testament, were written to teach believers. They are not merely ancient history or documents for another people. They were written with later readers in mind, including Christians.

[[v4|that through perseverance and through encouragement of the Scriptures we might have hope]]. The Scriptures produce two things in believers: "perseverance," the ability to endure difficulty, and "encouragement," comfort and strength. Through these, believers have "hope."

How do the Scriptures do this? They show how God has been faithful to His people throughout history. They tell of people like Abraham, Joseph, Moses, Ruth, David, and the prophets who trusted God through hardship. They record God's promises and their fulfillment. When believers read these stories and promises, they are encouraged to endure and to hope.

This verse shows that the Old Testament remains valuable for Christians. While believers are not under the Law of Moses as a way of salvation, the Old Testament is still God's word, given for their instruction. Paul writes elsewhere that the experiences of Israel [[1 Corinthians 10:11|were written for our admonition]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the purpose of Scripture: to give hope. God's word is not merely information; it is a source of strength and encouragement for life. Paul writes to Timothy that all Scripture is inspired by God and is useful for teaching, correction, and training in righteousness (2 Timothy 3:16–17).

It also shows the unity of the Bible. The Old and New Testaments belong together. The Old Testament points to Christ, and the New Testament shows its fulfillment. Christians read the whole Bible as one story of God's faithfulness.

Finally, the verse shows that hope is connected to God's word. When believers feel hopeless, one of the best things they can do is turn to Scripture. God speaks through His word to renew hope.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to read the Old Testament. Some believers focus only on the New Testament, but the Old Testament was also written for their learning. Its stories, poetry, prophecies, and wisdom can teach and encourage them.

It also encourages believers to turn to Scripture in hard times. When believers face trials, they can read the stories of those who trusted God before them and the promises God has made. Scripture can give them endurance and hope.

Finally, the verse invites believers to read Scripture with expectation. God's word is meant to produce hope. Believers can come to the Bible expecting God to speak, encourage, and strengthen them.

### Romans 15:7 — Accept One Another as Christ Accepted You

verse: Romans 15:7

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had been urging the strong and the weak in the Roman church to live in harmony. He had prayed that they would glorify God with one voice. Now he gives a clear command that summarizes everything he has said since 14:1.

[[v7|Therefore accept one another]]. "Accept" means to receive, to welcome, to bring into fellowship. This is the same word Paul used in 14:1: "accept one who is weak in faith." Now he applies it to everyone: "one another." Both strong and weak, Jewish and Gentile believers, are to welcome each other.

[[v7|even as Christ also accepted you]]. The standard and motive for welcome is Christ's welcome. How did Christ receive them? Freely, by grace, while they were still sinners (5:8). He did not wait for them to become perfect or to agree on every question. He welcomed them as they were and began to transform them. Believers are to welcome one another in the same way.

[[v7|to the glory of God]]. The purpose of this mutual welcome is God's glory. When believers welcome one another across differences, they display God's grace and bring Him glory.

In the following verses, Paul explains how Christ welcomed both Jews and Gentiles. He came as a servant to the Jews, to confirm God's promises to their ancestors, and He brought the Gentiles to glorify God for His mercy. Christ's welcome reaches across every division.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Christ's welcome is the foundation of Christian community. Believers belong together because Christ has received each of them. They did not earn their place; they were welcomed by grace. This truth makes it impossible for any believer to look down on another.

It also shows that the church is meant to display God's glory. When people from different backgrounds, cultures, and convictions welcome one another in love, the world sees the power of the gospel. Jesus prayed that His followers would be one [[John 17:21|that the world may believe that you sent me]].

Finally, the verse shows that welcome is active. It is not enough to tolerate others; believers are to welcome them, embracing them as family. This involves warmth, hospitality, and genuine love.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to welcome people who are different from them. Churches often divide along lines of culture, age, class, politics, or preferences. Believers can intentionally welcome those who are different, as Christ welcomed them.

It also reminds believers of their own welcome. When believers are tempted to judge or exclude others, they can remember how Christ received them: freely, graciously, and while they were still sinners.

Finally, the verse invites believers to see their welcome of others as an act of worship. When they receive one another in love, they bring glory to God. Hospitality, kindness, and inclusion are ways of honoring Him.

### Romans 15:13 — The God of Hope

verse: Romans 15:13

#### Simple Explanation

Paul had just quoted four Old Testament passages showing that God always intended for the Gentiles to praise Him alongside Israel. The last quotation, from Isaiah, ended with the words, "in him the Gentiles will hope." Now Paul offers a prayer for his readers.

[[v13|Now may the God of hope]]. Paul describes God as "the God of hope." God is the source of hope. He gives hope, and He is the object of hope. Believers' hope rests in Him.

[[v13|fill you with all joy and peace in believing]]. Paul prays that God would fill them with "all joy and peace." These are gifts of God, not merely feelings people create for themselves. And they come "in believing," through faith. As believers trust God, He fills them with joy and peace.

[[v13|that you may abound in hope]]. The result is that believers would "abound," overflow, in hope. Not just a little hope, but hope that spills over.

[[v13|in the power of the Holy Spirit]]. This is the work of the Holy Spirit. Hope is not produced by positive thinking or favorable circumstances. It is the Spirit's work in believers' hearts.

This beautiful prayer forms a fitting conclusion to the main teaching section of Romans. After discussing sin, justification, new life, God's plan for Israel, and practical living, Paul prays that believers would be filled with joy, peace, and overflowing hope.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that hope is central to the Christian life. Paul has spoken of hope throughout Romans: Abraham's hope against hope (4:18), the hope of glory (5:2), hope that does not disappoint (5:5), and the hope of creation's renewal (8:20–25). Christian hope is confident expectation based on God's promises.

It also shows that joy, peace, and hope come through faith. Believers do not produce these qualities by effort alone. As they trust God, He fills them. Faith is the channel through which God's blessings flow.

Finally, the verse shows the work of the Trinity. The "God of hope" (the Father) fills believers with joy and peace as they believe (in Christ), so that they abound in hope "in the power of the Holy Spirit." Father, Son, and Spirit work together to bring hope.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse is a prayer believers can pray for themselves and others. When believers feel discouraged, anxious, or hopeless, they can ask the God of hope to fill them with joy and peace.

It also encourages believers to trust God more deeply. Joy and peace come "in believing." When believers focus on God's promises and trust Him, they experience His joy and peace even in difficult circumstances.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to depend on the Holy Spirit. Hope is not something believers can manufacture. They can ask the Spirit to fill them with hope and to make that hope overflow to others.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Romans 15 calls the strong to bear with the weak and to seek the good of their neighbors rather than pleasing themselves. Christ is the ultimate example: He did not please Himself but bore the insults meant for God. Believers who are mature in faith can use their strength to support and build up others, setting aside their own preferences when love requires it.

The chapter also reminds believers that the Scriptures were written for their instruction, to give them endurance, encouragement, and hope. Believers can read the whole Bible, including the Old Testament, expecting God to strengthen them through it.

Romans 15 calls believers to welcome one another as Christ has welcomed them. Christ came as a servant to Israel and brought the Gentiles to praise God for His mercy. Believers can welcome people of every background into their fellowship, and in doing so, bring glory to God. Paul's prayer that the God of hope would fill believers with joy and peace reminds them that hope comes from God, through faith, by the power of the Spirit.

Finally, the chapter shows Paul's heart for mission and his dependence on prayer. He longed to preach Christ where He had not been named, and he asked the Roman believers to strive together with him in prayer. Believers can share Paul's passion for those who have never heard the gospel, support missionary work, and join in earnest prayer for those who carry the good news.

## Chapter Prayer

God of hope, fill me with all joy and peace as I trust in You, so that I may overflow with hope by the power of the Holy Spirit. Lord Jesus, You did not please Yourself but bore the reproaches meant for God. Help me to follow Your example, bearing with the weaknesses of others and seeking their good. Thank You for the Scriptures, written for my learning; give me endurance and encouragement through Your word. Thank You for welcoming me by Your grace. Help me to welcome others as You have welcomed me, for the glory of God. Give me Paul's passion for those who have never heard of You, and teach me to pray earnestly for those who carry Your gospel to the ends of the earth. God of peace, be with me and with all Your people. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
