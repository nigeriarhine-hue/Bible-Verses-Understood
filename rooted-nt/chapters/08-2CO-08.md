# 2 Corinthians 8
theme: The Grace of Giving
main: Paul encourages the Corinthians to complete the collection they had begun for the poor believers in Jerusalem. He holds up the example of the Macedonian churches, who gave joyfully and beyond their means out of deep poverty because they first gave themselves to the Lord. The ultimate pattern for giving is Jesus Christ, who though rich became poor so that others might become rich. Paul explains that giving should be willing and proportionate, aimed at mutual care, and handled with careful integrity.

## Chapter Background and Story

Chapters 8 and 9 turn to a practical matter that was close to Paul's heart: the collection for the believers in Jerusalem. The church there was largely poor. Famine had struck Judea in the 40s (Acts 11:28–30), and many Jewish believers may have lost family support or employment because of their faith in Jesus. At the Jerusalem council, the leaders had asked Paul to [[Galatians 2:10|remember the poor]], which he said he was eager to do.

Over several years, Paul gathered gifts from the Gentile churches he had founded, in Galatia, Macedonia, and Achaia, the province that included Corinth (1 Corinthians 16:1–4; Romans 15:25–27). For Paul, this collection was more than charity. It was a powerful sign of unity between Jewish and Gentile believers. The Gentiles had received spiritual blessings through the Jewish people; now they could share material blessings in return (Romans 15:27).

The Corinthians had been among the first to show interest in the collection a year earlier. But during the conflict with Paul, their giving appears to have stalled. Now that the relationship has been restored (chapter 7), Paul gently encourages them to finish what they started. These two chapters contain the New Testament's most extended teaching on giving, and Paul handles the subject with remarkable tact. He never uses the ordinary word for money. Instead he speaks of "grace," "fellowship," "service," and "blessing."

### The Example of Macedonia (8:1–6)

[[v1|Moreover, brothers, we make known to you the grace of God which has been given in the assemblies of Macedonia]]. The churches of Macedonia included Philippi, Thessalonica, and Berea. Paul calls their generosity "the grace of God." Their giving was not their own achievement; it was evidence of God's grace at work in them.

[[v2|how in a severe ordeal of affliction, the abundance of their joy and their deep poverty abounded to the riches of their generosity]]. The Macedonian believers were suffering persecution (1 Thessalonians 1:6; 2:14) and were very poor. Yet joy and poverty combined to overflow in generosity. [[v3|For according to their power, I testify, yes and beyond their power, they gave of their own accord]], [[v4|begging us with much entreaty to receive this grace and the fellowship in the service to the saints]]. Paul did not have to persuade them. They pleaded for the privilege of giving. The secret of their generosity, given in verse 5, is studied below.

Encouraged by this example, Paul urged Titus, who had already begun the work in Corinth, to help them complete it (v6).

### Abounding in This Grace (8:7–15)

[[v7|But as you abound in everything—in faith, utterance, knowledge, all earnestness, and in your love to us—see that you also abound in this grace]]. The Corinthians prized spiritual gifts, especially speech and knowledge (1 Corinthians 1:5). Paul gently suggests that generosity belongs on that list too.

[[v8|I speak not by way of commandment, but as proving through the earnestness of others the sincerity also of your love]]. Paul does not order them to give. He wants their gift to be a genuine expression of love. The supreme example of giving, Christ Himself, is studied below.

Paul offers advice. The Corinthians had begun with eagerness a year earlier; now they should finish. The principle of giving according to what one has is studied below.

[[v13|For this is not that others may be eased and you distressed]], [[v14|but for equality. Your abundance at this present time supplies their lack, that their abundance also may become a supply for your lack, that there may be equality]]. Paul is not asking the Corinthians to impoverish themselves so that others can live in ease. He is describing mutual care within the family of God. At present, the Corinthians have more and Jerusalem has less. Another time, it may be the reverse. The goal is that no one in God's family should go without what they need.

He illustrates this with the story of the manna in the wilderness: [[v15|As it is written, “He who gathered much had nothing left over, and he who gathered little had no lack.”]] In Exodus 16:18, when the Israelites gathered manna, each family ended up with what it needed. God's provision was shared so that no one was in want. Paul sees the church's generosity as continuing that pattern.

### Integrity in Handling Money (8:16–24)

Paul then describes the team he is sending to Corinth to receive the gift. Titus himself was eager to go ([[v16|But thanks be to God, who puts the same earnest care for you into the heart of Titus]]). With him went [[v18|the brother whose praise in the Good News is known throughout all the assemblies]], a respected believer chosen by the churches. Paul does not name him, and scholars have suggested various candidates, such as Luke or Barnabas, but no one knows for certain. A third brother, tested and proven earnest, also went (v22). Paul's reasons for this careful arrangement are studied below.

[[v23|As for Titus, he is my partner and fellow worker for you. As for our brothers, they are the apostles of the assemblies, the glory of Christ]]. "Apostles" here means messengers or representatives sent by the churches, not apostles in the sense of the Twelve. Paul closes by asking the Corinthians to show these men the proof of their love (v24).

## Important Verses Explained

### 2 Corinthians 8:5 — First They Gave Themselves to the Lord

verse: 2 Corinthians 8:5

#### Simple Explanation

Paul has just described the astonishing generosity of the Macedonian churches. They were poor and suffering, yet they gave beyond their ability and even begged for the opportunity to help. Now Paul explains what made it possible.

"This was not as we had expected." Paul had expected something modest from such poor churches, perhaps even hoped to spare them. Instead, they surpassed every expectation.

The reason is that "first they gave their own selves to the Lord." Before they gave any money, they gave themselves. Their generosity flowed from surrender. Once a person has given their whole life to God, giving possessions becomes a natural expression of that deeper commitment.

They also gave themselves "to us through the will of God." They committed themselves to Paul and his mission, trusting that this was God's will for them. Their partnership with Paul was an outworking of their devotion to the Lord.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the true source of Christian generosity. Giving is not first a financial matter but a spiritual one. Where the heart belongs to God, the wallet follows. Jesus said, [[Matthew 6:21|for where your treasure is, there your heart will be also]]. The Macedonians show the other side of that truth: when the heart is given to God, treasure is released for His purposes.

Paul makes the same point in Romans 12:1, where he urges believers to present their bodies as a living sacrifice. The offering God wants most is not a portion of what people have but the whole of who they are. Gifts of money, time, and talent are meaningful because they express that deeper offering.

It is also striking that their generosity flowed from joy (v2). Self-giving to the Lord produced not grim duty but overflowing gladness. Those who know they belong to God find freedom in letting go of what they own.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Conversations about giving often begin with percentages, budgets, or needs. Those are important, but this verse suggests starting at a deeper place. The first question is not "How much should I give?" but "Have I given myself to the Lord?" When that question is settled, decisions about money become a matter of worship rather than reluctant obligation.

This verse also guards against giving as a substitute for surrender. It is possible to give money while holding back one's heart, or to use generosity to feel spiritually secure. The Macedonians reversed that order. Their lives belonged to God first, and their gifts followed.

### 2 Corinthians 8:9 — Though He Was Rich, He Became Poor

verse: 2 Corinthians 8:9

#### Simple Explanation

In the middle of a practical discussion about a financial collection, Paul suddenly points to the heart of the gospel. He does not command the Corinthians to give. Instead he reminds them of what Christ has given.

"Though he was rich." Before He came to earth, the Son of God shared the glory, honor, and riches of heaven with the Father (John 17:5). "Yet for your sakes he became poor." He took on human nature, was born in humble circumstances, lived without a home of His own (Luke 9:58), and finally gave up His life on the cross.

The purpose of His poverty was "that you through his poverty might become rich." The riches Paul has in mind are spiritual: forgiveness, reconciliation with God, adoption as God's children, the gift of the Spirit, and eternal life. Christ's self-emptying became the source of every blessing believers enjoy.

Paul's logic is simple. The Corinthians have received unimaginable riches through Christ's self-giving. How could they hold back from giving to fellow believers in need?

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is a compact summary of the incarnation and the cross. Paul expresses the same truth more fully in Philippians 2:5–8, where Christ, who existed in the form of God, emptied Himself, took the form of a servant, and humbled Himself to death on a cross. The eternal Son chose to step down from glory into human weakness and suffering for the sake of others.

It is important to read "become rich" in its context. Paul is not promising that believers will become financially wealthy by following Christ. Paul himself lived in poverty (2 Corinthians 6:10; 11:27). The riches in view are the riches of grace. Some modern teaching has used this verse to argue that Christ's death guarantees material prosperity, but that reading overlooks Paul's own life and the point of the passage, which is to encourage generosity toward the poor, not the pursuit of wealth.

The verse also shows that Christian giving is shaped by Christ's example. He did not give out of surplus but gave Himself. His people imitate Him, in their measure, when they give sacrificially for the good of others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

The most powerful motivation for generosity is not guilt or pressure but gratitude. When believers remember how much Christ gave up for them, their own giving becomes a joyful response. A heart that dwells on the grace of the Lord Jesus becomes more open-handed.

This verse also challenges the common assumption that the goal of life is accumulation. Christ moved from riches to poverty for the sake of others. That does not mean every believer is called to poverty, but it does mean that wealth is held for the sake of others, and that downward steps taken in love, such as sharing, simplifying, or serving the poor, follow the pattern of Christ.

### 2 Corinthians 8:12 — Acceptable According to What You Have

verse: 2 Corinthians 8:12

#### Simple Explanation

Paul has just urged the Corinthians to finish the giving they began a year earlier. Now he explains the standard by which God views their gift.

"If the readiness is there," meaning if there is a willing heart, then the gift "is acceptable according to what you have, not according to what you don’t have." God does not measure giving by the size of the amount but by the willingness behind it in relation to what a person actually has.

This principle frees people from comparison. A wealthy person and a poor person cannot give the same amount, and God does not expect them to. Each gives in proportion to their means. Paul made the same point in 1 Corinthians 16:2, asking each one to [[1 Corinthians 16:2|save as he may prosper]].

The principle also guards against unhealthy pressure. Paul does not want anyone to go into debt or neglect their own family's needs in order to give. As verse 13 says, the goal is not that others should be relieved while the givers are burdened.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Jesus illustrated this principle when He watched a poor widow put two small coins into the temple treasury. He said she had given more than all the others, because they gave out of their abundance, but she gave out of her poverty (Mark 12:41–44). God sees the heart and the cost, not just the amount.

This verse also reflects the character of God, who is not a harsh taskmaster demanding what people do not have. He gives first and receives gladly whatever is offered in love. The willingness of the heart matters to Him more than the size of the gift. When God asked for offerings for the tabernacle, He said to receive them [[Exodus 25:2|from everyone whose heart makes him willing]]. The theme runs throughout Scripture.

At the same time, Paul does not use this principle as an excuse for half-hearted giving. Willingness must lead to action; that is why he urges the Corinthians to "complete the doing" (v11). The ideal is a willing heart that follows through.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

People with limited income sometimes feel their gifts do not matter. This verse affirms that small gifts given willingly are fully acceptable to God. A modest offering from someone with little is precious in His sight.

For those with greater means, the same principle calls for proportionate generosity. Giving "according to what you have" means that as resources increase, generosity can grow too. And for everyone, the verse encourages finishing what was promised. Good intentions to give, serve, or help are worth following through on.

### 2 Corinthians 8:20–21 — Honorable in the Sight of God and People

verse: 2 Corinthians 8:20-21

#### Simple Explanation

Paul explains why he arranged for several trusted men, including one chosen by the churches themselves, to accompany the collection. He wanted to avoid any suspicion that he might misuse the money.

"We are avoiding this, that any man should blame us concerning this abundance which is administered by us." The collection was a large sum. Paul's opponents had already questioned his motives, perhaps suggesting he was using the collection for his own benefit (2 Corinthians 12:16–18). By involving others, Paul made the process transparent.

Verse 21 states the principle: "Having regard for honorable things, not only in the sight of the Lord, but also in the sight of men." It is not enough to be honest before God privately. Those who handle money for others must also act in ways that are visibly honest and accountable.

This verse echoes Proverbs 3:4, which speaks of finding favor and good understanding in the sight of God and man.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Paul's approach shows that integrity is not only about avoiding wrongdoing but also about avoiding the appearance of wrongdoing where possible. The reputation of the gospel was at stake. If Christian leaders were seen as greedy or careless with money, people would be turned away from Christ.

Paul's practice also shows humility. He did not insist that people simply trust him because he was an apostle. He welcomed accountability. Leaders who resist oversight, especially in financial matters, place themselves and their communities at risk. Leaders who invite oversight protect both themselves and the people they serve.

Throughout Scripture, God cares deeply about honest dealing. Leviticus 19:35–36 calls for just weights and measures. Jesus warned against devouring widows' houses (Mark 12:40). Paul's careful handling of the collection reflects God's own justice and faithfulness.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Churches, ministries, and charities today can follow Paul's model by having more than one person handle funds, keeping clear records, being transparent about how money is used, and welcoming outside review. These practices are not signs of distrust but expressions of care for the community and for the reputation of Christ.

The same principle applies to individuals. Believers who manage money for others, whether as treasurers, executors, business partners, or employees, should act in ways that are honorable "in the sight of men" as well as before God. Clear records, honest communication, and openness to accountability help build trust and honor God.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Second Corinthians 8 presents giving not as a burden but as a grace, something God works in His people. The Macedonian believers had every reason to hold back. They were poor and persecuted. Yet they gave joyfully and beyond their means because they had first given themselves to the Lord. Their example shows that generosity flows from surrender and joy, not from surplus.

The heart of the chapter is Christ Himself. He was rich and became poor so that others could become rich in God's grace. Every act of Christian giving is a small reflection of His great self-giving. When believers remember the grace of the Lord Jesus, giving becomes an act of gratitude rather than pressure.

Paul also gives practical wisdom. Giving should be willing, not coerced. It should be proportionate to what a person has, so no one need feel ashamed of a small gift or burdened beyond their means. Its goal is mutual care within the family of God, so that those who have more help those who have less, knowing the situation may someday be reversed. And good intentions should be carried through to completion.

Finally, the chapter calls for integrity in handling money. Paul took care to be transparent and accountable, protecting the reputation of the gospel. Churches and individuals can follow his example by handling resources honestly and openly. In all these ways, the way believers use money becomes a witness to the generous grace of God.

## Chapter Prayer

Generous God, thank You for the grace of my Lord Jesus Christ, who though He was rich became poor for my sake, so that through His poverty I might become rich in Your love. Before I offer You anything else, I give myself to You again. Free my heart from clinging to what I have, and fill me with the joy that overflows in generosity. Help me to give willingly and according to what You have given me, without comparison and without pressure. Open my eyes to the needs of brothers and sisters near and far, and let my abundance meet their lack. Help me to follow through on the good I intend to do. Make me honest and transparent in everything I handle, so that my life honors You in the sight of others. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
