# Luke 6
theme: The Sermon on the Plain: Kingdom Blessings and Radical Mercy
main: Jesus faces criticism over the Sabbath, heals a man's withered hand, and spends a whole night in prayer before choosing twelve apostles. Coming down to a level place among a great crowd, He pronounces blessings on the poor, hungry, grieving, and persecuted and woes on the rich and comfortable. He calls His followers to love enemies, show mercy as the Father is merciful, give generously, avoid hypocritical judgment, and build their lives by doing what He says.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 6 contains two Sabbath controversies, the choosing of the Twelve, and Jesus' great sermon often called the Sermon on the Plain, because Luke says Jesus delivered it on [[v17|a level place]]. This sermon has much in common with Matthew's Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7). Jesus likely taught similar themes on many occasions, and the Gospel writers selected and arranged His teaching for their own purposes. Luke's version is shorter and more direct, and it includes woes alongside the blessings. (Several parallel teachings are studied in depth in Matthew's commentary: the Sabbath at Matthew 12:8, the Beatitudes at Matthew 5:3, love for enemies at Matthew 5:44, the Golden Rule at Matthew 7:12, judging at Matthew 7:1, and the wise builder at Matthew 7:24.)

### Lord of the Sabbath (6:1–11)

On a Sabbath, Jesus' disciples were walking through grain fields, picking heads of grain, rubbing them in their hands to remove the husks, and eating. Some Pharisees objected that they were breaking the Sabbath by harvesting and threshing. Jesus reminded them that David and his men ate the consecrated bread from God's house when they were hungry (1 Samuel 21:1–6). Then He declared, [[v5|The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath]].

On another Sabbath, Jesus was teaching in a synagogue where there was a man whose [[v6|right hand was withered]]. Luke, the physician, specifies that it was the right hand, the hand most people would need for work. The scribes and Pharisees watched closely to see whether Jesus would heal on the Sabbath, so they could accuse Him. Jesus knew their thoughts. He told the man to stand up in front of everyone. Then He asked, [[v9|Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?]] He looked around at them all and told the man to stretch out his hand. It was completely restored.

The religious leaders [[v11|were filled with rage]] and began discussing what they might do to Jesus. (Mark 3:5 records Jesus' anger and grief at their hardened hearts.)

### Choosing the Twelve After a Night of Prayer (6:12–16)

Luke then records something found only in his Gospel: [[v12|In these days, he went out to the mountain to pray, and he continued all night in prayer to God]]. This is studied below. When morning came, Jesus called His disciples and chose twelve of them, whom He named apostles, meaning "those who are sent."

Luke's list includes Simon Peter, Andrew, James, John, Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew, Thomas, James son of Alphaeus, Simon the Zealot, Judas son of James (called Thaddaeus in Matthew and Mark), and Judas Iscariot, [[v16|who also became a traitor]]. The phrase "became a traitor" suggests that Judas was not a traitor when chosen, but became one later.

### The Crowd on the Plain (6:17–19)

Jesus came down with the Twelve and stood on a level place. A large crowd of His disciples was there, along with a great number of people from all over Judea, from Jerusalem, and from the coastal region of Tyre and Sidon, a Gentile area. They came to hear Him and to be healed. Those troubled by unclean spirits were healed, and the whole crowd tried to touch Him, [[v19|for power came out of him and healed them all]]. The scene shows Jesus surrounded by needy people from many backgrounds, including Gentiles, as He begins His teaching.

### Blessings and Woes (6:20–26)

Looking at His disciples, Jesus said, [[v20|Blessed are you who are poor, for God’s Kingdom is yours]]. He continued with blessings on those who are hungry now, who weep now, and who are hated, excluded, insulted, and rejected because of the Son of Man. These blessings are studied below.

Then, unlike Matthew's version, Luke records four woes that mirror the four blessings: [[v24|But woe to you who are rich! For you have received your consolation]]. Woe to those who are well fed now, for they will be hungry. Woe to those who laugh now, for they will mourn and weep. And woe when everyone speaks well of you, for that is how their ancestors treated the false prophets.

A "woe" is an expression of grief and warning, used by the Old Testament prophets to announce coming judgment (Isaiah 5:8–23). Jesus was not saying that wealth, food, or laughter are evil in themselves. He was warning those who find all their comfort in these things now and have no need for God. Their "consolation," their comfort, is already received in full. By contrast, the poor, hungry, and grieving who look to God will receive His kingdom, His filling, and His joy.

### Love Your Enemies (6:27–36)

Jesus then turned to [[v27|you who hear]], those who are listening and willing to respond. He commanded, [[v27|love your enemies, do good to those who hate you]], and to bless those who curse them and pray for those who mistreat them. He gave examples: turn the other cheek to someone who strikes you, do not withhold your shirt from someone who takes your coat, give to everyone who asks, and do not demand back what is taken. (As discussed at Matthew 5:44, these commands address personal retaliation and generosity; they do not require anyone to remain in danger or forbid seeking protection.)

Jesus stated the Golden Rule: [[v31|As you would like people to do to you, do exactly so to them]]. He explained that loving those who love you, doing good to those who do good to you, and lending to those who will repay you is no different from what sinners do. Instead, His followers should love their enemies, do good, and lend without expecting anything back. Then their reward will be great, and they will be [[v35|children of the Most High; for he is kind toward the unthankful and evil]]. He concluded with a call to imitate God, studied below: [[v36|Therefore be merciful, even as your Father is also merciful]].

### Do Not Judge, and Give Generously (6:37–42)

Jesus continued: [[v37|Don’t judge, and you won’t be judged. Don’t condemn, and you won’t be condemned. Set free, and you will be set free]]. "Set free" can also mean "forgive" or "release." Then He gave a promise about generosity, studied below.

He told a parable: can a blind person guide a blind person? Both will fall into a pit. A disciple is not above the teacher, but everyone who is fully trained will become like the teacher. This means that disciples will resemble their teachers. Following a blind guide leads to blindness; following Jesus leads to becoming like Him.

He then warned against hypocritical judging, using the image of someone with a beam in his own eye trying to remove a speck from his brother's eye. First, remove the beam from your own eye, and then you will see clearly to help your brother.

### Good Fruit and a Firm Foundation (6:43–49)

A good tree does not produce bad fruit, and a bad tree does not produce good fruit. Each tree is known by its fruit. People do not gather figs from thorns or grapes from briers. In the same way, a good person brings good things out of the good stored up in the heart, and an evil person brings evil things out of evil stored up in the heart, [[v45|for out of the abundance of the heart, his mouth speaks]].

Jesus asked, [[v46|Why do you call me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ and don’t do the things which I say?]] Calling Jesus "Lord" while ignoring His teaching is a contradiction. He ended with a parable: everyone who comes to Him, hears His words, and does them is like a man who dug deep and laid a foundation on rock. When a flood came, the house stood firm. But the one who hears and does not obey is like a man who built a house on the ground without a foundation. When the flood came, it collapsed, and its destruction was great. Luke's version emphasizes digging deep, a detail that suggests effort and commitment in building a life on Jesus' words.

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 6:12 — A Night of Prayer Before Choosing the Twelve

verse: Luke 6:12

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes what Jesus did before one of the most important decisions of His ministry: choosing the twelve apostles. It comes right after conflict with the religious leaders. Jesus had healed a man with a withered hand on the Sabbath, and the scribes and Pharisees were [[v11|filled with rage]], discussing what they might do to Him. Opposition was growing.

Luke writes, [[v12|In these days, he went out to the mountain to pray, and he continued all night in prayer to God]]. Mountains in Scripture are often places of encounter with God. Moses received the law on Mount Sinai; Elijah met God on Mount Horeb. Jesus went to a mountain to be alone with His Father.

He prayed "all night." This was not a quick prayer but an extended time of communion with God, lasting until morning. Only Luke records this detail. The phrase "prayer to God" emphasizes the focus of His prayer: He was seeking His Father.

The next verse explains what happened after the night of prayer: [[v13|When it was day, he called his disciples, and from them he chose twelve, whom he also named apostles]]. Jesus chose the Twelve after a night of prayer. These men would be His closest companions, would carry His message after His resurrection, and would lay the foundation of the church. The choice was so important that Jesus sought His Father's guidance in extended prayer.

Interestingly, among those chosen after this night of prayer was Judas Iscariot, who would betray Jesus. Jesus' prayerful choice did not mean that everything would go smoothly. God's purposes would be accomplished even through betrayal.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus made important decisions in dependence on His Father. Though He was the Son of God, He did not act independently. He sought the Father's will in prayer. In John's Gospel, Jesus says, [[John 5:19|the Son can do nothing of himself, but what he sees the Father doing]]. Prayer was how Jesus aligned His actions with the Father's will.

It also shows the seriousness of leadership in God's kingdom. The apostles would be foundational to the church (Ephesians 2:20). Jesus did not choose them casually. The early church followed this example, praying before selecting leaders and sending missionaries (Acts 1:24–26; 13:2–3; 14:23).

Finally, the verse shows that prayer often comes before and during times of opposition. As hostility grew, Jesus withdrew to pray. Prayer gave Him strength, clarity, and peace in the face of conflict.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to pray before making important decisions. Choices about relationships, careers, moves, ministries, and leadership deserve careful prayer. While not every decision requires an all-night prayer vigil, believers can follow Jesus' example by seeking God's guidance seriously and patiently.

It also encourages churches to pray when choosing leaders. Selecting pastors, elders, teachers, or ministry leaders should be done prayerfully, seeking God's will rather than relying only on human judgment or popularity.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that prayer is especially important in times of conflict and pressure. When facing opposition, criticism, or difficult circumstances, they can follow Jesus' example by withdrawing to pray, seeking God's strength and wisdom.

### Luke 6:20–21 — Blessed Are You Who Are Poor

verse: Luke 6:20-21

#### Simple Explanation

These verses begin the Sermon on the Plain. Jesus had just come down from the mountain where He spent the night in prayer and chose the Twelve. He stood on a level place surrounded by a large crowd of disciples and people from all over Judea, Jerusalem, and the Gentile coast of Tyre and Sidon. Many had come to be healed. Luke says Jesus [[v20|lifted up his eyes to his disciples]], indicating that He was speaking especially to those who had chosen to follow Him.

He said, [[v20|Blessed are you who are poor, for God’s Kingdom is yours]]. Notice that Luke records Jesus speaking directly: "you who are poor," rather than Matthew's "the poor in spirit." Luke's wording emphasizes the actual poverty of many of Jesus' followers. Many of the people listening were materially poor: fishermen, laborers, the sick, and the marginalized. Jesus told them that God's kingdom belongs to them.

[[v21|Blessed are you who hunger now, for you will be filled]]. Many in the crowd knew real hunger. Jesus promised that God would satisfy them. [[v21|Blessed are you who weep now, for you will laugh]]. Those who grieve now will know joy in God's kingdom.

The word "now" is important. Jesus contrasts the present situation with the future reversal God will bring. Those who are poor, hungry, and weeping now will be blessed in God's kingdom. Then, in the woes that follow, Jesus warns those who are rich, full, and laughing now that their situation will be reversed.

These blessings echo Mary's song in Luke 1, where she praised God for filling the hungry with good things and sending the rich away empty (Luke 1:53).

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses reveal God's special concern for the poor, hungry, and grieving. Throughout Scripture, God shows Himself as the defender of the weak and oppressed. Jesus' mission statement in Luke 4:18 was to preach good news to the poor. These blessings fulfill that mission.

They also teach that God's kingdom reverses the world's values. The world sees the poor, hungry, and grieving as unfortunate; Jesus calls them blessed, not because poverty is good in itself, but because God is with them and His kingdom belongs to them. Those who are poor often recognize their need for God more readily than those who are comfortable.

Christians have discussed how Luke's "you who are poor" relates to Matthew's "poor in spirit." The two are complementary. Luke emphasizes God's care for the materially poor; Matthew emphasizes the spiritual humility that recognizes dependence on God. Both are true. God cares about physical poverty, and He also welcomes all who come to Him with empty hands.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses bring hope to those who are poor, hungry, or grieving. God sees them, cares for them, and promises that their situation is not the end of the story. His kingdom belongs to them, and He will satisfy and comfort them.

They also challenge believers to share God's concern for the poor. If God blesses the poor and hungry, His people should care for them in practical ways: sharing food, resources, and compassion; advocating for justice; and treating the poor with dignity.

Finally, these verses, together with the woes, invite believers to examine where they find their comfort. Wealth, comfort, and popularity can lead people to feel they have no need for God. Believers can hold these things loosely and find their true security in God's kingdom.

### Luke 6:36 — Be Merciful as Your Father Is Merciful

verse: Luke 6:36

#### Simple Explanation

This verse summarizes Jesus' teaching about love for enemies. He has just told His followers to love their enemies, do good to those who hate them, bless those who curse them, and pray for those who mistreat them. He has told them to give to everyone who asks, to treat others as they want to be treated, and to lend without expecting anything back. He explained that ordinary people love those who love them, but His followers should go further, loving even the ungrateful and wicked. If they do, they will be [[v35|children of the Most High; for he is kind toward the unthankful and evil]].

Then Jesus gives this summary command: [[v36|Therefore be merciful, even as your Father is also merciful]].

"Merciful" means showing compassion, kindness, and forgiveness, especially toward those who do not deserve it. Mercy goes beyond fairness; it gives what is needed rather than what is earned.

The standard is God Himself: [[v36|even as your Father is also merciful]]. God is described throughout Scripture as merciful. When He revealed Himself to Moses, He proclaimed, [[Exodus 34:6|The LORD! The LORD, a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth]]. God shows kindness even to those who reject Him, sending sun and rain on the just and the unjust. His children are called to reflect His character.

Matthew's parallel says, [[Matthew 5:48|you shall be perfect, just as your Father in heaven is perfect]]. Luke's version focuses specifically on mercy, showing that the perfection Jesus calls for is especially the perfection of merciful love.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that mercy is at the heart of God's character and the Christian life. Believers have received God's mercy: forgiveness for their sins, adoption into His family, and grace they did not deserve. Having received mercy, they are called to show mercy to others. Jesus taught, [[Matthew 5:7|Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy]].

The verse also shows that Christian ethics flow from God's character. Believers do not love enemies merely because it is a rule. They do it because they are children of a merciful Father, and children resemble their parents. The goal of the Christian life is to become like God in His love and mercy.

Finally, the verse sets a high standard that no one can meet perfectly. God's mercy is infinite; human mercy is limited. This drives believers back to God's grace, depending on Him to make them more merciful through His Spirit.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to show mercy in everyday relationships. Mercy might mean forgiving someone who has wronged them, being patient with someone who is difficult, helping someone who cannot repay, or giving someone a second chance. It means responding to others not according to what they deserve, but according to the mercy God has shown.

It also encourages believers to remember God's mercy toward them. When it is hard to show mercy to others, believers can reflect on how much God has forgiven them. Gratitude for God's mercy softens the heart and makes it easier to extend mercy to others.

Finally, mercy requires wisdom. Showing mercy does not mean ignoring wrongdoing, enabling harmful behavior, or remaining in dangerous situations. God's mercy is joined with His justice and truth. Believers can show mercy while still setting healthy boundaries and seeking justice where needed.

### Luke 6:38 — Give, and It Will Be Given to You

verse: Luke 6:38

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes in the section of Jesus' sermon about mercy and judgment. He has just said, [[v37|Don’t judge, and you won’t be judged. Don’t condemn, and you won’t be condemned. Set free, and you will be set free]]. Now He adds a positive command and promise: [[v38|Give, and it will be given to you]].

Jesus uses a vivid picture from the grain market: [[v38|good measure, pressed down, shaken together, and running over, will be given to you]]. In the ancient world, grain was sold by volume, using a measuring container. A generous merchant would fill the measure, press the grain down, shake it to settle it, and then keep filling until it overflowed. The buyer would hold the grain in a fold of his robe, which was used like a large pocket. The image is of extravagant generosity.

Then Jesus states the principle: [[v38|For with the same measure you measure it will be measured back to you]]. The way people treat others will come back to them. Those who are generous, merciful, and forgiving will receive generosity, mercy, and forgiveness.

In context, "giving" refers broadly to generosity: forgiving, not condemning, showing mercy, and sharing resources. Jesus has just spoken of lending without expecting repayment and giving to anyone who asks. The verse applies to all these forms of generosity.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals God's generous character. He is not stingy. When His people give, He responds with overflowing generosity. Paul writes, [[2 Corinthians 9:6|he who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly. He who sows bountifully will also reap bountifully]].

It is important to understand this verse correctly. Jesus is not teaching a formula for getting rich by giving money. Some teachers have used this verse to promise financial wealth to those who give to their ministries. That distorts Jesus' meaning. The context is about mercy, forgiveness, and generosity toward others, not about investment strategies. The reward God gives may include material provision, but it also includes spiritual blessings, joy, relationships, and eternal reward.

The verse also connects with Jesus' teaching that the measure people use for others will be used for them. Those who judge harshly will be judged; those who show mercy will receive mercy. God responds to people in ways that reflect how they treat others.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to be generous in every area of life. They can be generous with forgiveness, giving others the benefit of the doubt. They can be generous with time, attention, and encouragement. They can be generous with resources, sharing with those in need.

It also frees believers from anxiety about generosity. Many people hesitate to give because they fear they will not have enough. Jesus promises that God will provide abundantly for those who give. Believers can trust Him and give freely.

Finally, believers can guard against misusing this verse. Generosity should flow from love for God and others, not from a desire to get something back. The joy of giving and the knowledge that God sees and rewards are motivations enough. Believers can also be cautious about teachers who use this verse to pressure people into giving for personal gain.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 6 presents a picture of life in God's kingdom that challenges the world's values at every point. Jesus begins by praying all night before choosing His apostles, showing believers the importance of seeking God before important decisions and in times of opposition.

The blessings and woes call believers to see life from God's perspective. God cares deeply for the poor, hungry, grieving, and persecuted, and He warns those who find their comfort in wealth and popularity. Believers can care for those in need, hold their possessions loosely, and find their security in God rather than in comfort or approval.

The command to love enemies and be merciful as the Father is merciful lies at the heart of the chapter. Believers are called to respond to hatred with love, to curses with blessings, and to mistreatment with prayer. This is impossible by human strength alone, but it becomes possible as believers reflect on God's mercy toward them and depend on His Spirit. Mercy should be joined with wisdom, setting boundaries where necessary while refusing bitterness and revenge.

Jesus' teaching about judging and giving calls for humility and generosity. Believers should examine themselves before criticizing others, forgive freely, and give generously, trusting God's overflowing generosity in return. Finally, the parable of the two builders reminds believers that hearing Jesus' words is not enough. Calling Him "Lord" means doing what He says. Those who dig deep and build on His teaching will stand firm when life's floods come.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for showing me how to pray before making important decisions. Teach me to seek my Father's will in prayer, especially when I face difficult choices or opposition. Thank You for blessing the poor, the hungry, and those who weep. Help me to share Your concern for them, and keep me from finding my comfort in wealth, comfort, or the approval of others.

Give me grace to love my enemies, to do good to those who hate me, and to pray for those who mistreat me. Make me merciful as my Father is merciful. Keep me from judging and condemning others, and help me to forgive freely. Make me generous in every way. Above all, do not let me call You "Lord" without doing what You say. Help me to dig deep and build my life on Your words, so that I stand firm when the storms come. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
