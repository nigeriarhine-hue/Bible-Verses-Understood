# Acts 2
theme: Pentecost: The Spirit Poured Out and the Church Born
main: On the day of Pentecost, the Holy Spirit comes on the gathered believers with the sound of a mighty wind and tongues like fire, and they declare God's mighty works in the languages of pilgrims from many nations. Peter explains that this fulfills Joel's prophecy and proclaims that the crucified Jesus has been raised and exalted as Lord and Christ. Cut to the heart, about three thousand people repent and are baptized, and the new community devotes itself to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread, and prayer, sharing generously and praising God with glad hearts.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 2 describes one of the most important events in the history of God's people: the coming of the Holy Spirit and the birth of the church. Jesus had told His disciples to wait in Jerusalem for "the promise of the Father" (1:4). Ten days after His ascension, the promise was fulfilled.

The timing was significant. Pentecost, also called the Feast of Weeks, came fifty days after Passover (the name Pentecost comes from the Greek word for "fiftieth"). It was a harvest festival when the first fruits of the wheat harvest were offered to God (Leviticus 23:15–21). In later Jewish tradition, it was also associated with the giving of the Law at Mount Sinai. Jesus had died at Passover as the Passover Lamb; now, at Pentecost, the Spirit came to gather the first fruits of a great harvest of people, and to write God's law on human hearts, as the new covenant promised (Jeremiah 31:33; Ezekiel 36:26–27).

### The Coming of the Spirit (2:1–13)

The believers were [[v1|all with one accord in one place]]. Suddenly a sound like a violent wind filled the house. In both Hebrew and Greek, the same word can mean "wind," "breath," and "spirit." The wind recalls God's breath giving life to Adam (Genesis 2:7) and to the dry bones in Ezekiel's vision (Ezekiel 37:9–14). Then [[v3|Tongues like fire appeared and were distributed to them, and one sat on each of them]]. Fire often signals God's presence in Scripture, as at the burning bush and on Mount Sinai (Exodus 3:2; 19:18). John the Baptist had promised that Jesus would baptize with the Holy Spirit and fire (Luke 3:16). The Spirit came on each person individually, not only on the leaders. This is studied below.

Jerusalem was crowded with Jewish pilgrims from across the known world, as well as [[v10|proselytes]], Gentiles who had converted to Judaism. When they heard the sound, a crowd gathered. They were astonished, because [[v6|everyone heard them speaking in his own language]]. Luke lists the nations from the east (Parthians, Medes, Elamites, Mesopotamia), from Asia Minor, from North Africa, from Rome, and from Crete and Arabia. These Galileans, who were often looked down on as uneducated, were declaring [[v11|the mighty works of God]] in languages they had never learned.

Many interpreters see Pentecost as a reversal of the Tower of Babel (Genesis 11:1–9). At Babel, God confused human languages and scattered the nations because of their pride. At Pentecost, God overcame the barrier of language so that people from many nations could hear the gospel. Babel scattered; Pentecost gathered.

Some were amazed and asked, [[v12|What does this mean?]] Others mocked: [[v13|They are filled with new wine]].

### Peter's Sermon (2:14–36)

Peter stood up with the eleven and addressed the crowd. The man who had denied Jesus in fear before a servant girl now spoke boldly before thousands. He began by dismissing the accusation of drunkenness: it was only [[v15|the third hour of the day]], about nine in the morning.

Instead, he said, [[v16|this is what has been spoken through the prophet Joel]]. Peter quoted Joel 2:28–32, studied below. He then turned to his central message: Jesus of Nazareth.

[[v22|Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved by God to you by mighty works and wonders and signs which God did by him among you, even as you yourselves know]]. Peter appealed to what his audience had seen. Jesus' ministry had been public.

Then he described Jesus' death: [[v23|him, being delivered up by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God, you have taken by the hand of lawless men, crucified and killed]]. Peter held together God's sovereign plan and human responsibility. Jesus' death was part of God's plan, yet those who crucified Him were guilty. ("Lawless men" probably refers to the Roman authorities, who did not know God's law.)

[[v24|whom God raised up, having freed him from the agony of death, because it was not possible that he should be held by it]]. Death could not keep its hold on the Author of life.

Peter supported this with Psalm 16:8–11, in which David says, [[v27|you will not leave my soul in Hades, neither will you allow your Holy One to see decay]]. Peter pointed out that David had died and was buried, and [[v29|his tomb is with us to this day]]. David's body had decayed. So David must have been speaking as a prophet about his descendant, the Messiah, whom God had promised to place on his throne (2 Samuel 7:12–13). [[v32|This Jesus God raised up, to which we all are witnesses]].

Peter then connected the resurrection with what the crowd was seeing: [[v33|Being therefore exalted by the right hand of God, and having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, he has poured out this which you now see and hear]]. The risen and ascended Jesus was the one pouring out the Spirit.

Peter quoted Psalm 110:1: [[v34|The Lord said to my Lord, “Sit by my right hand]] until I make your enemies a footstool for your feet. Jesus Himself had used this psalm to show that the Messiah is greater than David (Luke 20:41–44). Peter's conclusion is studied below.

### The Response (2:37–41)

The listeners [[v37|were cut to the heart]] and asked, [[v37|Brothers, what shall we do?]] Peter's answer is studied below. [[v41|Then those who gladly received his word were baptized. There were added that day about three thousand souls]]. Jesus had promised that His disciples would do "greater works" after He went to the Father (John 14:12). On a single day, three thousand people came to faith.

### The Life of the Early Church (2:42–47)

Luke describes the life of the new community, studied below. He adds that [[v43|Fear came on every soul, and many wonders and signs were done through the apostles]]. The believers shared their possessions: [[v44|All who believed were together, and had all things in common]]. They [[v45|sold their possessions and goods, and distributed them to all, according as anyone had need]]. This sharing was voluntary, not commanded (Acts 5:4 shows that property remained the owner's to use). It was a spontaneous expression of love, so that no one among them was in need (Acts 4:34), fulfilling God's desire for His people in Deuteronomy 15:4.

They met daily in the temple courts and in homes, eating together [[v46|with gladness and singleness of heart]], [[v47|praising God and having favor with all the people. The Lord added to the assembly day by day those who were being saved]]. Notice who added people to the church: the Lord. Growth was His work.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 2:4 — Filled with the Holy Spirit

verse: Acts 2:4

#### Simple Explanation

The believers were gathered together on the day of Pentecost, about one hundred twenty of them, praying as they had been doing since Jesus ascended (1:14–15). Suddenly the sound of a mighty wind filled the house, and something like flames of fire appeared and rested on each of them.

[[v4|They were all filled with the Holy Spirit]]. Notice the word "all." The Spirit did not come only on the apostles or on the leaders, but on every believer present, men and women, young and old. This was something new. In the Old Testament, the Spirit came upon certain individuals, such as prophets, judges, and kings, for particular tasks. Moses had once said, [[Numbers 11:29|I wish that all the LORD’s people were prophets, that the LORD would put his Spirit on them!]] At Pentecost, that wish was fulfilled.

[[v4|and began to speak with other languages, as the Spirit gave them the ability to speak]]. The believers spoke in languages they had not learned. The crowd of pilgrims from many nations heard them speaking in their own native languages. The Spirit gave them this ability; it was not something they produced on their own.

What did they speak about? The crowd said, [[v11|we hear them speaking in our languages the mighty works of God!]] The gift of languages was used to praise God and declare His works. It was the beginning of the church's witness to the nations.

This filling with the Spirit fulfilled Jesus' promise: [[Acts 1:8|you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you]]. The Spirit came to empower them for witness.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Pentecost marks the beginning of a new era in God's dealings with His people. The Spirit now dwells in all believers, uniting them to Christ and to one another. Paul later writes, [[1 Corinthians 12:13|For in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body]]. Pentecost was the birthday of the church as a Spirit-filled community.

Christians hold different views on how Pentecost relates to believers today. Some traditions, especially Pentecostal and charismatic churches, emphasize that believers can and should experience a distinct filling or baptism of the Spirit, often accompanied by speaking in tongues, as a continuing gift. Other traditions see Pentecost as a unique, unrepeatable event that inaugurated the church, with all believers receiving the Spirit at conversion, and they understand the miraculous signs of Acts as especially tied to the apostolic era. Many Christians hold positions between these views. All agree that every believer has the Holy Spirit (Romans 8:9) and that believers are called to be continually filled with the Spirit (Ephesians 5:18).

Finally, the gift of languages shows God's heart for all nations. The gospel would not be limited to one language or culture. From the very first day, the church was multilingual and multinational, pointing to the vision of Revelation: a great multitude [[Revelation 7:9|out of every nation and of all tribes, peoples, and languages]] worshiping God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse reminds believers that the Christian life is meant to be lived in the power of the Spirit. The disciples did not begin their mission by their own strength. They waited for the Spirit, and He empowered them. Believers today can depend on the Spirit's help in their witness, their struggles, and their service.

It also challenges believers to value every member of the church. The Spirit was poured out on all believers, not only on the leaders. Every believer has been given the Spirit and has a part to play in God's work.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to celebrate the diversity of the church. God's people come from every nation and language. Churches can reflect this by welcoming people from different backgrounds, supporting the translation of Scripture, and sharing the gospel across cultural lines.

### Acts 2:17–18 — I Will Pour Out My Spirit on All Flesh

verse: Acts 2:17-18

#### Simple Explanation

Some in the crowd had mocked the believers, saying they were drunk. Peter stood up and explained that this was not drunkenness but the fulfillment of a prophecy given through the prophet Joel centuries earlier (Joel 2:28–32).

[[v17|It will be in the last days, says God, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh]]. Peter's quotation begins with "in the last days," words that are not in the original Hebrew of Joel but which Peter uses to interpret the moment. The "last days" are the final era of God's plan, which began with Jesus' coming and will be completed at His return. Believers have been living in the "last days" since Pentecost.

"All flesh" means all kinds of people. The Spirit would not be limited by age, gender, or social status. Joel's prophecy continues: [[v17|Your sons and your daughters will prophesy. Your young men will see visions. Your old men will dream dreams]]. Both sons and daughters, both young and old, would receive the Spirit.

[[v18|Yes, and on my servants and on my handmaidens in those days, I will pour out my Spirit, and they will prophesy]]. Even servants and slaves, both men and women, would receive the Spirit. In the ancient world, social status determined much of life. God's Spirit would be poured out on everyone who belongs to Him, regardless of status.

"Prophesy" in this context means speaking God's message under the Spirit's inspiration. The believers at Pentecost were declaring the mighty works of God, a form of prophecy.

The quotation ends with the promise: [[v21|whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved]]. Peter goes on to show that "the Lord" on whom people must call is Jesus.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This prophecy reveals the generous, inclusive nature of the Spirit's outpouring. Under the old covenant, the Spirit's work was mostly limited to certain leaders. Under the new covenant, every believer, man or woman, young or old, rich or poor, receives the Spirit. Paul writes that in Christ [[Galatians 3:28|There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus]].

It also shows that Pentecost marks the arrival of the last days. The cosmic signs Joel describes, the sun darkened and the moon turned to blood, have been understood in different ways. Some connect them to the darkness at Jesus' crucifixion (Luke 23:44–45); others see them as pointing to events before Christ's return; others read them as vivid prophetic language for God's dramatic intervention in history. All agree that Pentecost begins the age that leads to "the great and glorious day of the Lord."

Finally, the passage shows God's initiative. He says, "I will pour out." The Spirit is God's gift, not a human achievement.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses remind believers that the Spirit is given to all God's people. No believer is too young, too old, too poor, or too insignificant to be used by God. Churches are called to recognize and encourage the gifts of all their members, including women, young people, the elderly, and those with little social status.

They also encourage believers to live with a sense of urgency. The last days have begun. Believers live between Pentecost and Christ's return, a time of opportunity for the gospel to go to all nations.

Finally, these verses invite everyone to call on the name of the Lord. The promise is for "whoever." No one is excluded from the offer of salvation. Believers can share this hope with confidence.

### Acts 2:36–38 — Repent and Be Baptized

verse: Acts 2:36-38

#### Simple Explanation

Peter had just finished explaining that Jesus, whom the people had crucified, had been raised from the dead and exalted to God's right hand, and that He was the one pouring out the Spirit. Then he reached his conclusion: [[v36|Let all the house of Israel therefore know certainly that God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified]].

"Lord" was the title used for God in the Greek translation of the Old Testament. "Christ" means Messiah, the anointed King. The One they had rejected and crucified was the Lord of all and the promised Messiah. The words "this Jesus whom you crucified" brought the message home personally.

[[v37|Now when they heard this, they were cut to the heart]]. The Holy Spirit convicted them of their sin, just as Jesus had promised (John 16:8). They asked, [[v37|Brothers, what shall we do?]]

Peter answered, [[v38|Repent and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit]]. "Repent" means to change one's mind and turn around, turning from sin and from rejecting Jesus to trusting and following Him. "Be baptized" was the outward sign of this inner change, publicly identifying with Jesus. Baptism "in the name of Jesus Christ" meant baptism by His authority and into relationship with Him.

The promises were "the forgiveness of sins" and "the gift of the Holy Spirit." The very people who had called for Jesus' crucifixion could be forgiven and receive the Spirit.

Peter added, [[v39|For the promise is to you and to your children, and to all who are far off, even as many as the Lord our God will call to himself]]. The promise extended across generations and to people far away, which would later include the Gentiles.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses summarize the gospel response: repentance and faith, expressed in baptism, leading to forgiveness and the gift of the Spirit. Elsewhere in Acts, forgiveness is linked with faith (10:43) and with repentance (3:19), showing that these belong together.

Christians understand the relationship between baptism and forgiveness in different ways. Some traditions, including Roman Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, and Churches of Christ, see baptism as a means through which God gives forgiveness and new life. Many evangelical and Baptist traditions understand baptism as an outward sign and public confession of the forgiveness already received through faith. Others hold positions in between. All agree that forgiveness comes through Christ and is received by faith, and that baptism is commanded by Jesus and important for every believer.

The passage also shows the astonishing grace of God. These listeners had been part of the city that rejected and crucified Jesus only seven weeks earlier. Yet the offer of forgiveness was extended to them. No sin is too great for God's grace when people repent and turn to Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses show the right response to the gospel. Hearing about Jesus is not enough; it calls for a response. Those who recognize their sin and Jesus' lordship are called to repent, turn to Him in faith, and be baptized as a public sign of belonging to Him.

They also bring hope to those who feel their sin is too great. Peter's listeners had rejected the Messiah. Yet God offered them forgiveness and His Spirit. Anyone who turns to Christ can be forgiven, no matter what they have done.

Finally, these verses show the importance of baptism in the Christian life. Jesus commanded it (Matthew 28:19), and the early church practiced it immediately. Believers who have not been baptized are encouraged to consider Jesus' command and to take this step of obedience.

### Acts 2:42 — Devoted to Teaching, Fellowship, Bread, and Prayer

verse: Acts 2:42

#### Simple Explanation

After Peter's sermon, about three thousand people were baptized and joined the believers in a single day. Suddenly there was a large community of new believers who needed to grow in their faith. Luke tells us how they lived.

[[v42|They continued steadfastly]]. The word means to persist, to be devoted, to keep at something with determination. These new believers did not simply attend occasional meetings; they were committed.

Luke names four things they were devoted to.

[[v42|in the apostles’ teaching]]. The apostles had been with Jesus and had been taught by Him, including during the forty days after His resurrection. They passed on what Jesus taught and explained how the Scriptures pointed to Him. The new believers were eager to learn. Today, the apostles' teaching is preserved in the New Testament.

[[v42|and fellowship]]. The Greek word koinonia means sharing, partnership, or communion. It includes spending time together, caring for one another, and sharing life and possessions, as the following verses describe.

[[v42|in the breaking of bread]]. This probably refers both to shared meals and to the Lord's Supper, which in the early church was often celebrated as part of a meal. The believers remembered Jesus' death as He had commanded (Luke 22:19).

[[v42|and prayer]]. In Greek, it is literally "the prayers," which may include both the set times of prayer at the temple and their own times of prayer together. The church that had been born in prayer (1:14) continued in prayer.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse describes the essential marks of a healthy church. A church grounded in the apostles' teaching stays faithful to the truth. A church marked by fellowship expresses love and unity. A church that breaks bread together remembers Christ's death and lives in communion with Him. A church devoted to prayer depends on God.

These four practices are not merely activities; they are means by which believers are joined to Christ and to one another. Through the apostles' teaching, believers hear Christ's word. Through fellowship, they experience His love in His people. Through the breaking of bread, they remember His sacrifice. Through prayer, they speak with the Father in Jesus' name.

The verse also shows that the Spirit's coming led to a new kind of community. The Spirit did not produce isolated, individual believers but a family devoted to God and to one another. The result was gladness, generosity, and growth (2:46–47).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse offers a pattern for every believer and every church. Believers can examine whether they are devoted to these four practices: learning God's word, sharing life with other believers, regularly remembering Christ at His table, and praying together.

It also challenges a consumer approach to church. The early believers did not merely attend services; they devoted themselves to a shared life. Believers today can look for ways to be more deeply involved in their church community, through small groups, shared meals, service, and prayer.

Finally, the verse encourages churches to focus on what matters most. Programs and styles vary across cultures and generations, but these four practices remain central. Churches that return to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread, and prayer will find themselves on the same path as the first believers.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 2 shows what happens when God pours out His Spirit. Ordinary believers were filled with power, spoke of God's mighty works, and became bold witnesses. Believers today have the same Spirit living within them. They can depend on Him for courage, wisdom, and love, and they can trust that God still works powerfully through ordinary people who are yielded to Him.

The chapter also centers on the message of Jesus. Peter's sermon was about Jesus' life, death, resurrection, and exaltation as Lord and Christ. The Spirit's coming led directly to the proclamation of Jesus. Believers today are called to keep Jesus at the center of their message, sharing the good news that He died for sins, rose again, and offers forgiveness and the Holy Spirit to all who turn to Him.

Acts 2 shows the right response to the gospel: repentance, faith, and baptism, leading to forgiveness and the gift of the Spirit. The offer of grace extended even to those who had rejected Jesus. No one is beyond the reach of God's forgiveness. Anyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved.

Finally, the chapter paints a picture of a Spirit-filled church: devoted to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread, and prayer, sharing generously, meeting with gladness, and praising God. Churches today can be renewed by returning to these essentials, and believers can find joy in living as part of such a community.

## Chapter Prayer

Holy Spirit, thank You for coming at Pentecost and for living in all who belong to Jesus. Fill me afresh with Your power, so that I may speak of the mighty works of God and bear witness to Jesus with courage and love. Lord Jesus, thank You that You were raised from the dead and exalted as Lord and Christ, and that You pour out the Spirit on Your people. I repent of my sin and turn to You again. Thank You for the forgiveness You offer and the gift of Your Spirit. Make me devoted to Your word, to fellowship with Your people, to remembering Your death at Your table, and to prayer. Fill Your church with gladness, generosity, and unity, and add to us daily those who are being saved. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
