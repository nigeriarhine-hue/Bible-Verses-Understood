# Mark 9
theme: Glory Revealed, Faith Stretched, and the Way of the Servant
main: Three disciples see Jesus transfigured in glory and hear the Father say, "Listen to him." At the foot of the mountain, a desperate father whose son is tormented cries out, "I believe. Help my unbelief!" and Jesus frees the boy. As Jesus again predicts His death, the disciples argue about greatness, try to stop an outsider who heals in Jesus' name, and are warned to take sin seriously and to live at peace with one another.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 9 continues the second half of Mark's Gospel, which began at Caesarea Philippi when Peter confessed that Jesus is the Christ and Jesus revealed that the Christ must suffer, die, and rise (Mark 8:27–38). Jesus had called His followers to take up the cross. Now the disciples receive a glimpse of the glory that lies beyond suffering, and then they return to a world of struggle, unbelief, and their own pride. Throughout the chapter, the disciples continue to misunderstand, but Jesus patiently teaches them what it means to follow a suffering Messiah.

### The Transfiguration (9:1–13)

Jesus said that some standing there would not die before they saw [[v1|God’s Kingdom come with power]]. Six days later, the transfiguration gave three of them such a glimpse. (Matthew 16:28 and its discussion note other ways this saying has been understood.)

Jesus took Peter, James, and John up a high mountain, and He was transfigured before them. His clothes became dazzling white, [[v3|such as no launderer on earth can whiten them]], a vivid detail only Mark includes, likely from Peter's memory. Elijah and Moses appeared, talking with Jesus. Moses represented the Law, and Elijah represented the Prophets; both pointed to Jesus.

Peter said, "Rabbi, it is good for us to be here," and suggested building three shelters. Mark explains, [[v6|For he didn’t know what to say, for they were very afraid]]. A cloud overshadowed them, and a voice came from the cloud: [[v7|This is my beloved Son. Listen to him]]. Suddenly they saw only Jesus. (This scene is studied in depth at Matthew 17:5.)

On the way down, Jesus told them not to tell anyone until the Son of Man had risen from the dead. They kept the matter to themselves, [[v10|questioning what the “rising from the dead” meant]]. Jews who believed in resurrection expected it at the end of history for all the righteous, not for one person in the middle of history. The disciples could not yet grasp what Jesus meant.

They asked why the scribes said Elijah must come first (based on Malachi 4:5). Jesus agreed that Elijah comes first to restore all things, but He asked how it could also be written that the Son of Man must suffer much and be rejected. Then He said that Elijah had already come, and people did to him whatever they wanted. He was referring to John the Baptist, who had been executed by Herod (Mark 6:14–29). John's suffering foreshadowed Jesus' own.

### The Boy With an Unclean Spirit (9:14–29)

When they returned to the other disciples, they found a large crowd and some scribes arguing with them. When the crowd saw Jesus, they were amazed and ran to greet Him. Jesus asked what they were arguing about. A man in the crowd explained that he had brought his son, who had a spirit that made him mute. The spirit would seize him, throw him to the ground, make him foam at the mouth, grind his teeth, and become rigid. The father had asked the disciples to drive it out, but they could not.

Jesus responded, [[v19|Unbelieving generation, how long shall I be with you? How long shall I bear with you? Bring him to me]]. His frustration was directed at the general lack of faith, including the disciples'. When they brought the boy, the spirit immediately threw him into a convulsion. He fell to the ground and rolled around, foaming at the mouth.

Mark alone records the conversation that followed. Jesus asked the father how long this had been happening. [[v21|From childhood]], the father said. The spirit had often thrown him into fire or water to kill him. (As in Matthew's account, the Gospel attributes these seizures to a demon in this particular case; this should not be generalized to mean that epilepsy is always demonic.) Then the father pleaded, [[v22|But if you can do anything, have compassion on us and help us]].

Jesus repeated the father's words back to him: [[v23|If you can believe, all things are possible to him who believes]]. The question was not whether Jesus could help, but whether the father would trust Him. The father's response is studied below.

Seeing a crowd running together, Jesus rebuked the spirit: [[v25|You mute and deaf spirit, I command you, come out of him, and never enter him again!]] The spirit shrieked, convulsed the boy violently, and came out. The boy lay so still that many said he was dead. But Jesus took him by the hand and lifted him up, and he stood.

Later, in private, the disciples asked why they could not drive it out. Jesus answered, [[v29|This kind can come out by nothing but by prayer and fasting]]. (The words "and fasting" are not found in some of the oldest manuscripts.) The disciples had previously cast out demons (Mark 6:13), but perhaps they had begun to rely on their own authority rather than on God. Dependence on God expressed in prayer was essential.

### Second Prediction of the Passion (9:30–32)

Jesus and His disciples passed through Galilee, and He did not want anyone to know, because He was teaching His disciples. He told them, [[v31|The Son of Man is being handed over to the hands of men, and they will kill him; and when he is killed, on the third day he will rise again]]. This was His second prediction of His death. But [[v32|they didn’t understand the saying, and were afraid to ask him]]. Perhaps they remembered how Jesus rebuked Peter the last time, or perhaps they did not want to face what He was saying.

### Who Is the Greatest? (9:33–37)

When they reached Capernaum and were in the house, Jesus asked what they had been arguing about on the road. They were silent, because they had been arguing about who was the greatest. The contrast is stark: Jesus was speaking about His suffering and death, while they were competing for status.

Jesus sat down, the posture of a teacher, called the Twelve, and said, [[v35|If any man wants to be first, he shall be last of all, and servant of all]]. Then He took a little child, placed the child among them, and took the child in His arms. In that culture, children had little status. Jesus said that whoever welcomes such a child in His name welcomes Him, and whoever welcomes Him welcomes the Father who sent Him. Greatness in God's kingdom is measured by humble service to those with no status. (Matthew's parallel is studied at Matthew 18:3–4.)

### The Outsider Who Cast Out Demons (9:38–41)

John then told Jesus that they had seen someone driving out demons in Jesus' name and tried to stop him [[v38|because he doesn’t follow us]]. Notice that John said "follow us," not "follow you." The disciples were concerned about their group's status, and perhaps they were embarrassed that an outsider succeeded where they had just failed (vv. 18, 28).

Jesus said not to stop him, because no one who does a miracle in His name can quickly speak evil of Him. He added, [[v40|For whoever is not against us is on our side]]. This is studied below. Jesus then said that anyone who gives His followers even a cup of water in His name because they belong to Christ will not lose their reward.

### Warnings About Causing Sin (9:42–50)

Jesus then warned that whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in Him to stumble would be better off thrown into the sea with a millstone around his neck. "Little ones" includes children and humble, vulnerable believers. A millstone was a heavy stone turned by a donkey to grind grain.

He then used vivid exaggeration: if your hand, foot, or eye causes you to sin, cut it off or tear it out. It is better to enter life maimed than to go into Gehenna. Gehenna was the Valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, once associated with child sacrifice (Jeremiah 7:31) and later used as a symbol of final judgment. Jesus quoted Isaiah 66:24 about the worm that does not die and the fire that is not quenched. (Verses 44 and 46, which repeat this quotation, are absent from many early manuscripts.) Jesus was not commanding literal self-mutilation but showing how seriously sin must be dealt with. Whatever leads us into sin must be removed decisively.

The chapter ends with sayings about salt, studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 9:24 — [[Mark 9:24|I Believe. Help My Unbelief!]]

verse: Mark 9:24

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records the cry of a desperate father. His son had been tormented by a spirit since childhood. It made the boy unable to speak, threw him to the ground in violent convulsions, and often threw him into fire or water to kill him. The father had brought his son to Jesus' disciples while Jesus was on the mountain, but they could not help. By the time Jesus arrived, the father had experienced years of suffering and one more disappointment.

When the boy was brought to Jesus, the spirit threw him into another convulsion. The father pleaded, "But if you can do anything, have compassion on us and help us." His words reveal both hope and doubt. He hoped Jesus might help, but he was not sure.

Jesus responded, [[v23|If you can believe, all things are possible to him who believes]]. Jesus turned the father's "if you can" back to him. The question was not about Jesus' ability but about the father's trust.

Then comes this verse: [[v24|Immediately the father of the child cried out with tears, “I believe. Help my unbelief!”]] The word "immediately" shows the urgency of his response. "With tears" (found in many manuscripts) shows his emotion. His words are deeply honest. He declares, "I believe," affirming that he does trust Jesus. But in the same breath, he admits that his faith is mixed with doubt: "Help my unbelief." He does not pretend to have perfect faith. He asks Jesus to strengthen the faith he has.

Jesus did not reject him because his faith was imperfect. He immediately commanded the spirit to leave the boy, and the boy was freed.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse is one of the most honest prayers in the Bible. It shows that faith and doubt can exist together in the same heart. The father believed enough to bring his son to Jesus and to ask for help, but he also struggled with doubt after years of suffering and the disciples' failure. Jesus accepted his mixed faith and responded with power.

It also shows that faith itself can be a gift that we ask God to strengthen. The father asked Jesus not only to heal his son but to help his unbelief. Believers can ask God to increase their faith, just as the disciples later asked, [[Luke 17:5|Increase our faith]]. God is not waiting for people to produce perfect faith before He acts; He helps them believe.

Finally, the verse shows that the power lies in Jesus, not in the strength of the believer's faith. Earlier Jesus taught that even faith as small as a mustard seed can move mountains (Matthew 17:20). The father's faith was weak, but it was directed toward the right person.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers who struggle with doubt. Many people feel guilty because their faith is not stronger, especially in prolonged suffering or after repeated disappointments. The father's prayer shows that it is acceptable to come to Jesus with mixed faith. Believers can be honest with God about their doubts and ask Him to strengthen their trust.

It also provides a simple, powerful prayer for difficult times: "I believe. Help my unbelief." When believers face situations that test their faith, such as illness, loss, or unanswered prayers, they can use these words, affirming their trust while asking God to help them where they are weak.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to keep bringing their needs to Jesus even after disappointments. The father had been let down by the disciples, but he did not give up. Believers who have been discouraged by unanswered prayers or by the failures of others can still come to Jesus Himself, trusting His compassion and power.

### Mark 9:40 — [[Mark 9:40|Whoever Is Not Against Us Is on Our Side]]

verse: Mark 9:40

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words in response to a report from John, one of the Twelve. John told Jesus, [[v38|Teacher, we saw someone who doesn’t follow us casting out demons in your name; and we forbade him, because he doesn’t follow us]].

The context is important. Just before this, the disciples had failed to cast out a demon from a boy (vv. 14–29), and they had been arguing about which of them was the greatest (vv. 33–34). Now they had encountered someone outside their group who was successfully casting out demons using Jesus' name. They tried to stop him. Notice that John said "he doesn't follow us," not "he doesn't follow you." The disciples' concern seems to have been about their group's authority and status.

Jesus answered, [[v39|Don’t forbid him, for there is no one who will do a mighty work in my name and be able quickly to speak evil of me]]. Someone who was genuinely doing good in Jesus' name was not going to turn quickly against Him. Then Jesus gave the principle in this verse: [[v40|For whoever is not against us is on our side]].

Jesus was teaching His disciples not to be exclusive or territorial. The man was working on behalf of Jesus' kingdom, even though he was not part of their particular group. They should welcome such allies rather than oppose them.

Jesus followed this with a promise: even someone who gives His followers a cup of water in His name because they belong to Christ will not lose their reward (v. 41). Small acts of support for His people are valued by God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse warns against a narrow, possessive spirit among believers. The disciples assumed that God's work had to happen through their group. Jesus corrected them, showing that God's kingdom is larger than any one group of followers. This applies to the church today, where believers from different traditions, denominations, and ministries serve Christ in different ways.

In Matthew 12:30, Jesus said, [[Matthew 12:30|He who is not with me is against me]]. These sayings are not contradictory. In Matthew 12, Jesus was speaking about people's personal response to Him: there is no neutral ground regarding Jesus Himself. In Mark 9, He is speaking about how His disciples should treat others who are working in His name: they should not oppose those who are doing His work simply because they belong to a different group.

The verse also highlights the danger of pride and rivalry among believers. The disciples had just been arguing about who was the greatest, and now they were trying to stop someone else's ministry. Pride often shows itself in a desire to control and to be recognized as the only legitimate ones.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to be generous toward other Christians who serve Jesus differently. Churches and ministries can easily become competitive, suspicious, or dismissive of others. Believers can rejoice when the gospel is preached, the poor are served, and people are helped in Jesus' name, even if it is done by people outside their own church or tradition.

This does not mean ignoring false teaching. Scripture calls believers to test teaching and to reject what contradicts the gospel (1 John 4:1). But disagreements on secondary matters, or differences in methods and styles, should not lead believers to oppose others who are genuinely serving Christ.

Finally, this verse invites believers to examine their motives. Are they more concerned with Christ's work being done or with their own group getting credit? Humility allows believers to celebrate God's work wherever it happens.

### Mark 9:50 — Have Salt in Yourselves and Be at Peace

verse: Mark 9:50

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes a series of sayings in which Jesus warned His disciples about the seriousness of sin. He had told them that causing a little one to stumble deserves severe judgment, and that they should remove anything that causes them to sin, even if it is as valuable as a hand, foot, or eye. In verse 49, He said, [[v49|For everyone will be salted with fire]], a difficult saying that may refer to the testing and purifying trials that believers will face, or to sacrifices in the Old Testament, which were offered with salt (Leviticus 2:13).

Now Jesus speaks about salt in a more positive way: [[v50|Salt is good]]. In the ancient world, salt was valuable for flavoring and preserving food. But Jesus asks what happens if salt loses its saltiness. Salt in that region was often mixed with impurities, and if the actual salt dissolved away, what remained was useless. Jesus asks, [[v50|with what will you season it?]]

Then He gives two commands: [[v50|Have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another]]. "Have salt in yourselves" means to maintain the distinctive qualities of discipleship, such as purity, faithfulness, and devotion to God, that make believers like valuable salt. Salt was also associated with covenants in the Old Testament ("a covenant of salt," Numbers 18:19), representing faithfulness and loyalty.

"Be at peace with one another" brings the teaching back to where it started. The disciples had been arguing about who was the greatest (v. 34) and had tried to stop someone outside their group (v. 38). Jesus ends this section of teaching by calling them to peace. The qualities that make them "salt" should lead to harmony, not rivalry.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse connects personal holiness with peaceful relationships. Disciples who are truly "salty," devoted to God and faithful to His ways, will live at peace with others. Pride, rivalry, and competition destroy this peace. Paul later urges believers to [[Romans 12:18|as much as it is up to you, be at peace with all men]].

The image of salt losing its saltiness warns against losing the distinctive character of discipleship. Believers can drift into the attitudes and values of the world, becoming indistinguishable from those around them. When this happens, they lose their ability to influence others for good.

Finally, the call to "have salt in yourselves" may also point to the purifying work of God in believers' lives. The "fire" of trials and the "salt" of God's covenant faithfulness work together to make believers holy and useful to God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to maintain their distinctive Christian character. In a world that pressures people to conform, believers can remain faithful to Christ's teaching in their speech, integrity, generosity, and relationships. This distinctiveness is what makes them valuable to the world, like salt that preserves and flavors.

It also calls believers to pursue peace with one another. Churches, families, and friendships are often damaged by competition, pride, and conflict. Believers can take practical steps toward peace: apologizing when they have wronged others, refusing to gossip, letting go of the need to be right, and seeking reconciliation.

Finally, this verse connects holiness and harmony. Believers can examine whether their pursuit of righteousness has made them proud or harsh, or whether it has made them more humble and peaceful. True holiness produces peace.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 9 moves from the glory of the mountaintop to the struggles of the valley, and both speak to everyday life. The transfiguration reminds believers that Jesus is the glorious Son of God, and the Father's command, "Listen to him," remains the guiding principle for discipleship. Even when life is difficult or confusing, believers can trust that the One they follow is glorious, and that the path of the cross leads to resurrection.

The father of the tormented boy shows that faith can be weak and still be real. Believers who struggle with doubt can come to Jesus honestly, praying, "I believe. Help my unbelief." They can also learn from the disciples' failure that spiritual power depends on prayerful dependence on God rather than on past experience or personal ability.

The disciples' argument about greatness and their attempt to stop the outsider reveal the persistent temptations of pride and rivalry. Jesus answers with a child in His arms and a call to be servant of all. Believers can look for ways to serve those with little status, celebrate God's work through others, and resist the urge to compete for recognition.

Finally, Jesus' warnings about sin call for seriousness. Whatever leads believers or others into sin should be dealt with decisively. Believers should be especially careful not to cause vulnerable people to stumble. And Jesus' closing words bring these teachings together: have salt in yourselves, maintaining a distinctive faithfulness to God, and be at peace with one another.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for revealing Your glory on the mountain and for the Father's command to listen to You. Help me to hear Your voice above all others. When I face situations that seem hopeless, I come to You as the father of the tormented boy did. I believe; help my unbelief. Strengthen my faith, and teach me to depend on You in prayer.

Forgive me for wanting to be great and for competing with others. Make me a servant of all, willing to welcome the least important for Your sake. Help me to rejoice when Your work is done through people outside my own circle. Give me courage to deal seriously with sin in my life, and keep me from causing anyone to stumble. Keep me salty with faithfulness to You, and help me to live at peace with others. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
