# Matthew 25
theme: Ready, Faithful, and Merciful When the King Returns
main: Jesus completes His teaching about His return with three pictures of what readiness means. The parable of the ten virgins calls for prepared, lasting faith; the parable of the talents calls for faithful use of what God has entrusted; and the scene of the sheep and the goats shows that the King will judge people by how they treated the least of His brothers and sisters, because what is done for them is done for Him.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 25 continues the Olivet Discourse that began in chapter 24. Jesus is still on the Mount of Olives with His disciples, a few days before His crucifixion. In chapter 24 He warned about deception, predicted the destruction of the temple, described His glorious return, and stressed that no one knows the day or hour. He ended with a short parable about a faithful servant who is found caring for the household when his master returns. Chapter 25 expands on what it means to be ready through two longer parables and a vivid picture of the final judgment.

Each section adds a different emphasis. The first parable focuses on preparation over time, especially when the bridegroom is delayed. The second focuses on responsibility and active faithfulness. The third focuses on mercy toward those in need. Together they show that readiness for Christ's return is not passive waiting but faithful, active, compassionate living.

### The Parable of the Ten Virgins (25:1–13)

Jesus compared the kingdom of heaven to ten young women, bridesmaids, who took their lamps and went out to meet the bridegroom. In Jewish weddings of that time, the groom would go to the bride's family home, sometimes after negotiations that took hours, and then lead a procession back to his own home for the wedding feast. Friends of the bride would wait to join the procession, carrying lamps or torches, which were needed for a nighttime celebration. The lamps were probably torches with oil-soaked rags or small clay oil lamps that needed regular refilling.

Five of the young women were foolish and took no extra oil. Five were wise and brought flasks of oil. The bridegroom was delayed, and all of them grew drowsy and fell asleep. Notice that all ten slept; sleeping was not the problem. At midnight someone shouted that the bridegroom was coming. All ten got up and trimmed their lamps. The foolish ones realized their lamps were going out and asked the wise ones to share. The wise said there would not be enough for everyone and told them to go buy oil. While they were gone, the bridegroom arrived. Those who were ready went in with him to the wedding feast, and the door was shut.

Later the other bridesmaids came, saying, [[v11|Lord, Lord, open to us]]. He replied, [[v12|Most certainly I tell you, I don’t know you]]. These words echo Jesus' warning in Matthew 7:21–23. Jesus concluded, [[v13|Watch therefore, for you don’t know the day nor the hour in which the Son of Man is coming]]. This parable is studied below.

### The Parable of the Talents (25:14–30)

Jesus told another parable. A man going on a journey entrusted his wealth to his servants. To one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one, [[v15|to each according to his own ability]]. A talent was a large sum of money, equal to about twenty years' wages for a laborer. Even one talent was a great deal. (The English word "talent," meaning a natural ability, actually comes from this parable.)

The servant with five talents immediately put the money to work and gained five more. The one with two gained two more. But the servant with one talent dug a hole and buried his master's money. Burying money was considered a safe way to protect it in that culture, but it produced no gain.

After a long time, the master returned and settled accounts. The first two servants reported their gains, and the master praised each with the same words: [[v21|Well done, good and faithful servant. You have been faithful over a few things, I will set you over many things. Enter into the joy of your lord]].

The third servant came and said, [[v24|Lord, I knew you that you are a hard man]], who reaped where he did not sow. He said he was afraid and so he hid the talent. Now here it was, returned. The master called him [[v26|wicked and slothful]]. If he truly believed his master was demanding, he should at least have put the money in the bank to earn interest. The master took the talent and gave it to the servant with ten. He declared that those who have will receive more, and those who do not have will lose even what they have. The worthless servant was thrown into the outer darkness.

The third servant's problem was not that he had less, but that he did nothing with what he had been given. His view of his master as harsh led to fear, and fear led to inaction. Faithfulness means using what God entrusts to us for His purposes.

### The Sheep and the Goats (25:31–46)

Jesus concluded the discourse with a description of the final judgment. When the Son of Man comes in His glory, with all His angels, He will sit on His glorious throne. All the nations will be gathered before Him, and He will separate them as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats. In Palestine, shepherds often grazed sheep and goats together during the day but separated them at night, since goats needed more warmth. Sheep were generally more valuable and sometimes associated with blessing.

The King, as Jesus now calls the Son of Man, will say to those on His right, [[v34|Come, blessed of my Father, inherit the Kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world]]. Then He explains why: they fed Him when He was hungry, gave Him drink when He was thirsty, welcomed Him as a stranger, clothed Him, cared for Him when sick, and visited Him in prison.

The righteous are surprised. They ask when they ever saw Him in such need. The King answers, [[v40|because you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me]]. This verse is studied below.

Then the King will say to those on His left, [[v41|Depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire which is prepared for the devil and his angels]]. They did not feed, welcome, clothe, or visit Him. They also ask when they saw Him in need and did not help. He answers that whatever they did not do for the least of these, they did not do for Him. The chapter ends with a solemn statement: [[v46|These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life]].

Notice that the eternal fire was [[v41|prepared for the devil and his angels]], not for human beings. The kingdom, by contrast, was prepared for the blessed from the foundation of the world. God's original intention for humanity is blessing, not judgment.

After this, Matthew 26 begins the account of Jesus' arrest and crucifixion. The King who will one day sit on the throne of glory first goes to the cross, where He will Himself be hungry, thirsty, stripped, treated as a stranger, and imprisoned.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 25:13 — Keep Watch, Because You Do Not Know the Hour

verse: Matthew 25:13

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes the parable of the ten virgins. In the story, ten bridesmaids were waiting for a bridegroom to arrive so they could join the wedding procession and celebration. Five were wise and brought extra oil for their lamps. Five were foolish and did not. When the bridegroom was delayed, all of them fell asleep. At midnight the announcement came: the bridegroom was coming. The foolish bridesmaids discovered that their lamps were going out. While they went to buy oil, the bridegroom arrived, the wise bridesmaids went in with him, and the door was shut. When the foolish ones returned, the bridegroom said he did not know them.

Jesus then applied the parable: [[v13|Watch therefore, for you don’t know the day nor the hour in which the Son of Man is coming]]. "Watch" means to stay alert and ready. In the story, both groups slept, so "watching" does not mean never resting. It means being prepared so that, whenever the bridegroom comes, you are ready to go in.

The key difference between the wise and the foolish was preparation for a delay. The foolish bridesmaids were ready if the bridegroom came quickly, but not if he took longer than expected. The wise ones had planned for the long wait.

Jesus' disciples, and Matthew's first readers, may have expected Jesus to return very soon. This parable prepared them for the possibility of a long delay. Faith that is ready only for a quick return may run out when the wait is long.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This parable teaches that spiritual readiness cannot be borrowed. The wise bridesmaids could not share their oil, not because they were selfish, but because each person must be prepared for themselves. Many interpreters understand the oil as representing genuine faith, a living relationship with God, or a life that bears fruit, things that cannot be transferred from one person to another. A person cannot rely on the faith of their parents, spouse, or church.

The parable also warns that there will be a point when it is too late. The door was shut. This reflects the seriousness of Jesus' return. The opportunity to respond to God's invitation is open now, but it will not remain open forever. The bridegroom's words, "I don't know you," echo Jesus' warning in Matthew 7:23 to those who called Him "Lord" but did not truly know Him.

At the same time, the parable is a picture of joy. The goal is a wedding feast, a celebration. Christ's return is not only a time of judgment but the beginning of the great celebration of God's kingdom. Those who are ready will share in His joy.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to prepare for a long journey of faith. It is easy to be enthusiastic at first and then fade over time. Believers can develop habits that sustain faith over many years: regular prayer, Scripture reading, worship, fellowship, and service. These are like oil that keeps the lamp burning even when the wait is long.

It also reminds each person that their relationship with God is personal. Being surrounded by believers, attending church, or growing up in a Christian family is valuable, but it cannot replace personal faith. Each person must come to Christ for themselves.

Finally, this verse encourages believers not to put off what matters most. If someone has been delaying a decision to follow Christ, to reconcile with someone, or to deal with sin, now is the time. The door is open today, and the bridegroom could come at any time.

### Matthew 25:21 — "Well Done, Good and Faithful Servant"

verse: Matthew 25:21

#### Simple Explanation

These words are spoken by the master in the parable of the talents to the servant who had received five talents. Before leaving on a long journey, the master entrusted his wealth to three servants according to their abilities: five talents to one, two to another, and one to the third. A talent was a very large amount of money, about twenty years of wages for a laborer. The master expected the servants to manage his wealth wisely while he was gone.

The first servant immediately went and put the money to work, probably through trade or investment, and doubled it. When the master returned after a long time and asked for an account, the servant reported that he had gained five more talents.

The master responded, [[v21|Well done, good and faithful servant]]. "Good" describes the servant's character; "faithful" describes his reliability in carrying out his responsibility. The master then said, [[v21|You have been faithful over a few things, I will set you over many things]]. Five talents was a huge sum, yet the master calls it "a few things." Compared to what he would now receive, even this great responsibility was small. Faithfulness leads to greater responsibility.

Finally, the master said, [[v21|Enter into the joy of your lord]]. The reward was not only more responsibility but a share in the master's joy, a welcome into close fellowship with him.

The servant with two talents also doubled his amount and received exactly the same words of praise (v. 23). The master did not compare the two servants. Each was praised for faithfulness with what he had been given.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows what God values in His servants: goodness and faithfulness. God does not measure people by the size of their gifts or the scale of their results but by their faithfulness with what He has entrusted to them. The servant with two talents received the same praise as the one with five. Paul writes, [[1 Corinthians 4:2|it is required of stewards that they be found faithful]].

The parable also teaches that everything believers have is entrusted by God. Abilities, opportunities, resources, time, relationships, and the gospel itself are gifts from the Master. Believers are stewards, not owners, and they are accountable for how they use what has been given to them.

Finally, the master's words reveal the joy that awaits faithful servants. Serving God is not a grim duty followed by a cold evaluation. It leads to a welcome into the Master's joy. The reward of faithful service is deeper fellowship with God Himself.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to consider what God has entrusted to them. Everyone has received something: abilities, education, time, money, relationships, experiences, and spiritual gifts. The question is not how much we have compared to others, but whether we are using what we have for God's purposes.

It also encourages believers who feel their gifts are small. The servant with two talents received the same praise as the one with five. God does not expect people to produce what He has not given them. He looks for faithfulness. A person who faithfully cares for one family member, serves in a small role, or uses modest resources generously is just as pleasing to God as someone with greater visibility.

Finally, this verse warns against fear and inaction. The third servant buried his talent because he was afraid. Many believers hold back from serving because they fear failure or think their contribution doesn't matter. Jesus' parable encourages them to step out and use their gifts, trusting that God values faithful effort and that their work for Him is never wasted.

### Matthew 25:40 — Done for the Least, Done for Christ

verse: Matthew 25:40

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the King's explanation to the righteous at the final judgment. Jesus describes the scene: the Son of Man sitting on His glorious throne, all nations gathered before Him, and the people separated like sheep and goats. To those on His right, the King says they are blessed and will inherit the kingdom, because when He was hungry, thirsty, a stranger, naked, sick, and in prison, they cared for Him.

The righteous are surprised. They ask when they ever saw Him in such situations. They do not remember serving the King. The King answers, [[v40|Most certainly I tell you, because you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me]].

"The least of these" refers to people who are small, insignificant, and powerless in the world's eyes. The six needs mentioned, hunger, thirst, being a stranger, lacking clothing, sickness, and imprisonment, describe people in desperate circumstances. In that culture, prisoners often depended on family and friends to bring food, and visiting them could be risky. Strangers had no community to protect them.

Jesus calls these people "my brothers." Christians have interpreted this phrase in two main ways. Some understand "the least of these my brothers" to refer to Jesus' disciples, especially those who are poor, persecuted, or traveling as messengers of the gospel, since Matthew elsewhere uses "brothers" for Jesus' followers (Matthew 12:50; 28:10) and "little ones" for disciples (Matthew 10:42). Others understand it to include all people in need, since Jesus identifies with suffering humanity. Both views agree that Jesus identifies Himself personally with those who suffer and that acts of mercy toward them are acts of service to Him. Scripture elsewhere commands care for all people in need (Galatians 6:10).

The righteous did not serve in order to be seen by the King or to earn a reward. Their acts of compassion flowed naturally from their character, and they were surprised to learn their significance.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals Jesus' deep identification with the poor and suffering. The King of glory, seated on His throne, says that when the hungry are fed and the prisoner is visited, it is done to Him. This echoes the Old Testament, where God declares Himself the defender of the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the stranger (Deuteronomy 10:18; Proverbs 19:17).

It also shows that genuine faith is seen in practical mercy. The parable does not teach that people are saved by doing good works; elsewhere Matthew makes clear that salvation comes through Jesus, who gives His life as a ransom (Matthew 20:28; 26:28). But it does show that those who truly belong to the King will reflect His compassion. Mercy toward the needy is evidence of a heart transformed by God. James writes that faith without works is dead (James 2:14–17).

Finally, the passage gives dignity to ordinary acts of kindness. Feeding someone, giving water, welcoming a stranger, providing clothing, visiting the sick, and going to see a prisoner are not dramatic acts. They are simple, practical, and available to almost everyone. Yet the King treats them as service done to Himself.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to see Christ in those who are in need. When believers encounter someone who is hungry, lonely, sick, a stranger, poorly clothed, or in prison, they are meeting someone with whom Jesus identifies. Serving such people is a way of serving Him.

Practical applications include supporting food banks and shelters, welcoming immigrants and newcomers, visiting the sick and elderly, caring for people in prison and their families, giving clothing and resources, and simply noticing people who are often ignored. Churches can organize ministries that meet these needs, and individuals can look for opportunities in their daily lives.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to serve without seeking recognition. The righteous were surprised by the King's words because they had not been keeping score. Believers can serve with humble, unselfconscious love, trusting that the King sees every act of mercy, even those no one else notices.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 25 gives three pictures of what it means to be ready for Christ's return, and each applies to daily life. The parable of the ten virgins calls for lasting faith. The Christian life is often longer and more ordinary than expected, and enthusiasm alone will not sustain it. Believers can build habits of prayer, worship, Scripture, and fellowship that keep their faith alive over many years. They can also remember that each person must be ready for themselves; faith cannot be borrowed from others.

The parable of the talents calls for faithful use of what God has entrusted. Every believer has received gifts, opportunities, and resources from God. The question is not how much one has compared with others, but whether one is using it faithfully. Fear and comparison can lead to inaction, but God praises those who step out in faith and serve with what they have. The reward is not only greater responsibility but the joy of sharing in the Master's joy.

The scene of the sheep and the goats calls for mercy toward the needy. Jesus identifies Himself with the hungry, thirsty, stranger, naked, sick, and imprisoned. Believers can look for practical ways to care for such people, recognizing that in serving them, they serve Christ. This mercy should flow naturally from hearts transformed by God's grace, not from a desire for recognition.

Together, these three pictures show that waiting for Christ is not passive. It involves preparation, faithfulness, and compassion. The final words of the chapter are sobering: there are two destinies, eternal punishment and eternal life. But the kingdom was prepared for God's people from the foundation of the world, and the King who will judge is the same One who went to the cross to save. Believers can live with hope, readiness, and love as they wait for His return.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the Bridegroom and the King who will come again in glory. Keep my faith alive through long seasons of waiting, and help me to be ready whenever You come. I know that I cannot borrow faith from others, so draw me close to You and fill my life with what only You can give.

Thank You for everything You have entrusted to me. Free me from fear and comparison, and help me to use my time, abilities, and resources faithfully for Your purposes. I long to hear You say, "Well done, good and faithful servant." Open my eyes to see You in the hungry, the thirsty, the stranger, the poorly clothed, the sick, and the prisoner. Give me a heart of mercy that serves them as I would serve You, without seeking praise. Thank You for preparing a kingdom for Your people, and for going to the cross so that I could share in it. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
