# John 9
theme: The Man Born Blind Receives Sight, and the Seeing Become Blind
main: Jesus gives sight to a man who had been blind from birth, rejecting the assumption that his blindness was punishment for sin and revealing it instead as an occasion for God's works to be displayed. As the healed man is questioned, pressured, and finally thrown out of the synagogue, his understanding of Jesus grows from "a man called Jesus" to "a prophet" to worship of the Son of God. Meanwhile the religious leaders, who claim to see, harden into deeper blindness.

## Chapter Background and Story

John 9 is one of the most carefully told stories in the Gospels. It is almost like a drama in several scenes: Jesus and the blind man, the man and his neighbors, the man and the Pharisees, the Pharisees and his parents, the man and the Pharisees again, Jesus and the man, and finally Jesus and the Pharisees. As the scenes unfold, two movements happen at the same time. The man who was blind sees more and more clearly who Jesus is, while those who claim to see become more and more blind.

The chapter puts into action Jesus' claim in John 8:12, "I am the light of the world." He now shows it by giving light to eyes that had never seen.

### Who Sinned? (9:1–7)

As Jesus walked along, He saw a man who had been blind from birth. The disciples asked a common question of their time: [[v2|Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?]] Many people believed that suffering was always the direct result of a specific sin. Some rabbis even speculated that a baby could sin in the womb, or that children suffered for their parents' sins.

Jesus rejected both options. This is studied below. Then He said, [[v4|I must work the works of him who sent me while it is day. The night is coming, when no one can work]]. Jesus' time on earth was limited, and He was committed to doing the Father's work while He could. He added, [[v5|While I am in the world, I am the light of the world]].

Jesus spat on the ground, made mud, and put it on the man's eyes. Then He said, [[v7|Go, wash in the pool of Siloam]]. John explains that Siloam means "Sent." This was the pool from which water was drawn for the ceremonies at the Feast of Booths. The detail also points to Jesus, who throughout John is the One "sent" by the Father. The man went, washed, and [[v7|came back seeing]].

Why mud? Some see an echo of Genesis 2:7, where God formed the first man from the dust of the ground. Jesus, the Creator, now completes a man's creation by forming eyes that work. Others note that saliva was sometimes used in ancient healing practices. What is clear is that Jesus involved the man in his healing; he had to go and wash, trusting the word of One he could not yet see.

### The Neighbors' Questions (9:8–12)

The man's neighbors could hardly believe it. Some said, [[v8|Isn’t this he who sat and begged?]] Others said he only looked like the man. He kept saying, [[v9|I am he]]. When they asked how his eyes were opened, he told the story simply: [[v11|A man called Jesus made mud, anointed my eyes, and said to me, ‘Go to the pool of Siloam and wash.’ So I went away and washed, and I received sight]]. At this stage, Jesus is to him simply "a man called Jesus." When asked where Jesus was, he said he did not know.

### The First Hearing before the Pharisees (9:13–17)

The neighbors brought the man to the Pharisees. John notes, [[v14|It was a Sabbath when Jesus made the mud and opened his eyes]]. Kneading dough, or by extension mud, was among the kinds of work forbidden on the Sabbath under the rules the teachers had developed, and so was healing in cases that were not life-threatening.

The Pharisees were divided. Some said, [[v16|This man is not from God, because he doesn’t keep the Sabbath]]. Others asked, [[v16|How can a man who is a sinner do such signs?]] They asked the man his opinion, and he answered, [[v17|He is a prophet]]. His understanding was growing.

### The Parents Questioned (9:18–23)

The religious leaders did not believe that the man had really been born blind, so they called his parents. The parents confirmed that this was their son and that he had been born blind, but they would not say how he now saw or who had healed him. [[v21|He is of age. Ask him. He will speak for himself]].

John explains their fear: [[v22|the Jews had already agreed that if any man would confess him as Christ, he would be put out of the synagogue]]. Being expelled from the synagogue meant being cut off from community, worship, and social life. It was a serious threat, and the parents chose safety over speaking the truth about what had happened to their own son.

### The Second Hearing (9:24–34)

The leaders called the man back and said, [[v24|Give glory to God. We know that this man is a sinner]]. "Give glory to God" was a formula used to urge someone to tell the truth (Joshua 7:19). They wanted him to agree that Jesus was a sinner. His reply is studied below.

When they asked him again how Jesus had healed him, he showed surprising boldness: [[v27|I told you already, and you didn’t listen. Why do you want to hear it again? You don’t also want to become his disciples, do you?]] The leaders insulted him: [[v28|You are his disciple, but we are disciples of Moses]]. They said they did not know where Jesus came from.

The man, who had spent his life begging, now reasoned with the experts. It was amazing, he said, that they did not know where Jesus came from, since He had opened his eyes. God does not listen to sinners but to those who worship Him and do His will. [[v32|Since the world began it has never been heard of that anyone opened the eyes of someone born blind]]. His conclusion: [[v33|If this man were not from God, he could do nothing]].

The leaders had no answer except contempt: [[v34|You were altogether born in sins, and do you teach us?]] Then they threw him out. Ironically, they used the same assumption the disciples had made at the beginning, that his blindness proved his sinfulness.

### Jesus Finds the Man (9:35–41)

When Jesus heard that the man had been thrown out, He went and found him. This scene is studied below. The man came to faith and worshiped Jesus.

Jesus then said, [[v39|I came into this world for judgment, that those who don’t see may see; and that those who see may become blind]]. Some Pharisees nearby asked, [[v40|Are we also blind?]] Jesus answered, [[v41|If you were blind, you would have no sin; but now you say, ‘We see.’ Therefore your sin remains]]. Those who admit their need can receive sight. Those who insist that they already see, and refuse the light, remain in their sin.

## Important Verses Explained

### John 9:2–3 — That the Works of God Might Be Revealed

verse: John 9:2-3

#### Simple Explanation

As Jesus and His disciples walked through Jerusalem, they saw a man who had been blind since birth. He was probably sitting by the road begging, since blind people had few other ways to survive. The disciples did not ask how they could help him. Instead, they asked a theological question: [[v2|Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?]]

Their question reflected a widespread belief that suffering was always punishment for a particular sin. If someone was born blind, either the parents had sinned, or perhaps the child had somehow sinned before birth. The book of Job had already challenged this way of thinking, but it remained common.

Jesus answered, [[v3|This man didn’t sin, nor did his parents]]. Jesus was not saying that the man and his parents were sinless in general. He meant that the man's blindness was not caused by a specific sin committed by him or his parents. The disciples' assumption was wrong.

Then Jesus gave a new perspective: [[v3|but that the works of God might be revealed in him]]. Instead of looking backward to find someone to blame, Jesus looked forward to what God would do. This man's life, which others saw only as a tragedy or a punishment, would become a place where God's power and mercy would be displayed.

Jesus did not merely discuss the man's situation. He acted, giving him sight.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage corrects a harmful view of suffering. While Scripture teaches that sin brought suffering into the world (Genesis 3), and that some specific sins bring painful consequences, it also teaches that not all suffering is a direct punishment for particular sins. Job suffered though he was righteous. Jesus also rejected the idea that people who died in disasters were worse sinners than others (Luke 13:1–5).

The passage also shows God's ability to bring good out of suffering. This does not mean that God enjoys suffering or that every painful thing is good in itself. It means that God can work even in what is broken, displaying His glory through healing, comfort, endurance, or the hope of final restoration. Paul expresses a similar confidence: [[Romans 8:28|We know that all things work together for good for those who love God]].

Finally, the passage reveals Jesus' compassion and purpose. He came to do the works of the Father, and those works include restoring what is broken. This healing is a sign of the new creation, when God will wipe away every tear and heal every disease.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage warns against assuming that people's suffering is their fault. When someone is sick, disabled, poor, or grieving, it is easy to look for someone to blame. Jesus' response calls His followers to compassion rather than judgment. Instead of asking "Who sinned?", believers can ask how God might be glorified through their love and care for the one who is suffering.

It also gives hope to people who live with long-term suffering, including disabilities they were born with. Their lives are not punishments, and they are not less valuable to God. God can display His works in many ways, through healing, through strength in weakness, through the love of others, and through the faith that perseveres.

Finally, the passage encourages believers to respond to suffering with action. Jesus did not stop at a theological answer; He helped the man. Believers are called to do the works of God while it is day, showing mercy to those in need.

### John 9:25 — Though I Was Blind, Now I See

verse: John 9:25

#### Simple Explanation

The man who had been born blind was standing before religious leaders for the second time. They had already questioned him, questioned his parents, and argued among themselves. Now they tried to pressure him to agree with their conclusion: [[v24|Give glory to God. We know that this man is a sinner]]. Because Jesus had healed on the Sabbath, they were sure He could not be from God.

The man answered, [[v25|I don’t know if he is a sinner]]. He did not claim to have answers to all their theological questions. He was not a trained scholar and did not try to debate their interpretation of the Sabbath laws.

[[v25|One thing I do know: that though I was blind, now I see]]. What he did know was his own experience. He had been blind all his life. Now he could see. No argument could change that fact.

This answer was simple but powerful. The religious leaders had knowledge, authority, and power, but they could not deny what had happened. The man's testimony rested on the undeniable change in his life.

Throughout the chapter, the man shows growing courage. He began by simply describing what happened, then called Jesus a prophet, then challenged the leaders, and finally worshiped Jesus. His faith grew as he faced opposition.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The man's words have become a picture of conversion. Spiritually, all people are born blind, unable to see God's truth and glory. Paul writes that [[2 Corinthians 4:4|the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelieving]]. When Christ opens a person's eyes, they can say, "I was blind, but now I see." The well-known hymn "Amazing Grace" uses these very words.

The verse also shows the power of personal testimony. Theological arguments are important, but a transformed life speaks in a way that is hard to dismiss. The apostles also bore witness to what they had seen and heard (1 John 1:1–3).

Finally, the scene shows that faithfulness to the truth may cost something. The man's honesty led to his being thrown out of the synagogue. His parents had chosen safety. He chose to tell the truth, and in losing his place in the community, he found Jesus.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers who feel unqualified to talk about their faith. They may not be able to answer every theological question or win every argument. But they can share what they know: what Jesus has done in their lives. A simple, honest testimony can be powerful.

It also reminds believers to remember what God has done for them. Gratitude grows when believers recall how Christ opened their eyes, forgave their sins, and changed their lives. These memories strengthen faith in difficult times.

Finally, the man's courage challenges believers to stand for the truth even under pressure. Many people face social, family, or professional pressure to deny or downplay their faith. The man's example shows that it is better to lose human approval than to deny what Christ has done.

### John 9:35–38 — Lord, I Believe

verse: John 9:35-38

#### Simple Explanation

The man who had been healed had just been thrown out of the synagogue. For a first-century Jew, this meant being cut off from community life, worship, and relationships. He had received his sight, only to lose his place among his people. His own parents had refused to stand with him.

Then: [[v35|Jesus heard that they had thrown him out, and finding him]]. Jesus went looking for him. The man had not seen Jesus since he went to wash in the pool; he had been blind when Jesus spoke to him. Now Jesus sought him out.

Jesus asked, [[v35|Do you believe in the Son of God?]] (Some early manuscripts read "Son of Man.") The man answered, [[v36|Who is he, Lord, that I may believe in him?]] He was ready to believe; he only needed to know who this person was.

Jesus said, [[v37|You have both seen him, and it is he who speaks with you]]. The words "you have seen him" are beautiful, since the man had been blind and could now see. The first face he could recognize as the face of his healer was the face of the Son of God.

[[v38|He said, “Lord, I believe!” and he worshiped him]]. The man's faith reached its full expression. He began by calling Jesus "a man," then "a prophet," then "from God," and now he worshiped Him as Lord.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This scene shows the seeking heart of Jesus. Just as a shepherd goes after a lost sheep, Jesus went after the man who had been cast out. In the next chapter, Jesus describes Himself as the Good Shepherd who knows His sheep and calls them by name (John 10:3, 11). Those whom the religious leaders rejected, Jesus welcomed.

The scene also shows that faith grows over time. The man did not understand everything at once. His understanding developed through his experience, his testimony, and the opposition he faced. When Jesus revealed Himself fully, he was ready to believe and worship.

The man's worship is significant. In John's Gospel, worship belongs to God alone. Jesus accepted the man's worship without correction, confirming His divine identity. The man received both physical sight and spiritual sight, seeing Jesus for who He truly is.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage offers comfort to anyone who has been rejected for their faith or has been pushed out by others. Jesus seeks out those who are cast out. When people lose relationships, community, or status because of their faith in Him, Jesus comes to them and draws them closer.

It also encourages people whose faith is still growing. Not everyone understands everything about Jesus at once. Faith often grows step by step. Jesus patiently reveals Himself to those who are willing to believe.

Finally, the passage shows the right response to Jesus: belief and worship. Knowing facts about Jesus is not enough. The man's response, "Lord, I believe," followed by worship, is the response Jesus invites from everyone.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

John 9 teaches believers to look at suffering differently. Instead of searching for someone to blame, Jesus pointed to what God would do. Christians are called to respond to people who suffer, including those with lifelong disabilities, with compassion, dignity, and practical help, trusting that God can display His works even in situations that seem hopeless.

The chapter also celebrates the power of a changed life. The man who was healed could not answer every question, but he knew that he had been blind and now could see. Every believer has a story of what Christ has done. Sharing that story honestly and humbly can be a powerful witness, even when others oppose it.

John 9 shows that faithfulness can be costly. The man's parents chose safety over truth, while the man chose truth and was thrown out. Many believers face pressure to remain silent about Jesus. This chapter reminds them that Jesus seeks out those who suffer for His sake and draws them into deeper fellowship with Himself.

Finally, the chapter contains a sobering warning. Those who insist that they already see clearly, and refuse to admit their need, can remain blind. Spiritual sight begins with humility. Those who acknowledge their blindness can receive sight from Jesus, the light of the world, and grow in faith until they worship Him as Lord.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the light of the world. Thank You for seeing the man born blind when others saw only a beggar or a theological question. Teach me to see suffering people with Your compassion, and keep me from blaming those who are already hurting. Thank You for opening my eyes to see You. Like the man You healed, I can say that once I was blind, but now I see. Give me courage to speak honestly about what You have done in my life, even when others oppose me or do not understand. When I feel rejected or pushed aside, remind me that You seek out those who are cast out. Keep me humble, so that I never claim to see while refusing Your light. Help my faith to grow until my whole life is worship of You, my Lord. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
