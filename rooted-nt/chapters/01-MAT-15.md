# Matthew 15
theme: Clean Hands, Unclean Hearts, and Faith From the Outside
main: Jesus confronts religious leaders who use human traditions to cancel God's commands, teaching that true defilement comes from the heart, not from unwashed hands or food. He then honors the persistent faith of a Gentile woman and feeds a large crowd in Gentile territory, showing that God's mercy reaches beyond Israel's borders to all who come to Him in need.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 15 continues the pattern of the previous chapters: Jesus faces growing opposition from religious leaders while showing compassion to ordinary people, including outsiders. A delegation of Pharisees and scribes now arrives from Jerusalem, the center of religious authority, which shows that official concern about Jesus is increasing. The chapter is arranged so that its first half is about what truly makes a person "clean" or "unclean," and its second half shows Jesus welcoming people whom many Jews considered unclean: a Canaanite woman and Gentile crowds.

### The Tradition of the Elders (15:1–9)

The Pharisees and scribes asked Jesus, [[v2|Why do your disciples disobey the tradition of the elders? For they don’t wash their hands when they eat bread]]. The "tradition of the elders" was a large body of oral teachings that interpreted and applied the law of Moses. The Pharisees believed these traditions had been passed down from Moses and were authoritative. They were later written down in the Mishnah around A.D. 200.

The handwashing in question was not about hygiene but ritual purity. The law of Moses required priests to wash before serving in the tabernacle (Exodus 30:17–21). The Pharisees extended this to ordinary meals for all Israelites, pouring water over the hands in a prescribed way to remove any ritual defilement picked up through contact with unclean things or people.

Jesus responded with a counter-question: [[v3|Why do you also disobey the commandment of God because of your tradition?]] He gave an example. God commanded, [[Exodus 20:12|Honor your father and your mother]], and warned against speaking evil of parents (Exodus 21:17). Honoring parents included supporting them financially in their old age. But some used a vow called [[Mark 7:11|Corban]], declaring money or property "devoted to God." Once declared, those resources could not be used to help parents, though the person might still keep using them. In this way, a religious tradition provided a loophole that cancelled one of the Ten Commandments.

Jesus called them [[v7|hypocrites]] and quoted Isaiah 29:13: [[v8|These people draw near to me with their mouth, and honor me with their lips; but their heart is far from me]]. Their worship was outwardly correct but inwardly empty, and they treated human rules as if they were God's commands.

### What Truly Defiles (15:10–20)

Jesus then called the crowd and gave a principle: [[v11|That which enters into the mouth doesn’t defile the man; but that which proceeds out of the mouth, this defiles the man]]. This was a startling statement. The law of Moses contained detailed food laws (Leviticus 11), and many Jews had suffered and even died rather than eat forbidden food (as recorded in 1 Maccabees 1:62–63). Jesus was not merely criticizing handwashing traditions; He was teaching that the deepest problem of uncleanness is not external but internal.

The disciples told Jesus that the Pharisees were offended. Jesus replied that every plant His Father did not plant would be uprooted, and He called the Pharisees [[v14|blind guides of the blind]]. Those who follow blind guides will fall into a pit with them.

Peter asked Jesus to explain. Jesus pointed out that food passes through the digestive system and out of the body; it does not touch the heart. But what comes out of the mouth comes from the heart: [[v19|evil thoughts, murders, adulteries, sexual sins, thefts, false testimony, and blasphemies]]. These are what make a person unclean before God. In Mark’s account the saying ends with the words [[Mark 7:19|making all foods clean]], which many translations treat as Mark’s own comment on what Jesus’ teaching meant. The early church later recognized that the food laws, which had marked Israel as distinct, no longer applied to followers of Jesus (Acts 10:9–16).

### The Canaanite Woman's Faith (15:21–28)

Jesus then withdrew to the region of Tyre and Sidon, Gentile cities on the Mediterranean coast north of Galilee, in what is now Lebanon. A Canaanite woman from that area came to Him, crying out, [[v22|Have mercy on me, Lord, you son of David! My daughter is severely possessed by a demon!]]

Matthew calls her a "Canaanite," an old term that recalls Israel's ancient enemies in the land, people the Israelites had been told to drive out. Yet she addressed Jesus as "Lord" and "Son of David," recognizing Him as Israel's Messiah, which many in Israel had refused to do.

At first Jesus did not answer her at all. The disciples, annoyed by her persistent crying, urged Him to send her away. Jesus said, [[v24|I wasn’t sent to anyone but the lost sheep of the house of Israel]], echoing His instructions to the Twelve (Matthew 10:6). His earthly mission was focused first on Israel, in keeping with God's plan.

But the woman came, knelt before Him, and said simply, [[v25|Lord, help me]]. Jesus replied, [[v26|It is not appropriate to take the children’s bread and throw it to the dogs]]. The "children" were Israel; "dogs" was a term Jews sometimes used for Gentiles. The Greek word Jesus used refers to small household dogs rather than wild scavengers, which softens the image somewhat, but the statement still sounds harsh.

The woman did not argue or take offense. She accepted the comparison and turned it into an appeal: [[v27|Yes, Lord, but even the dogs eat the crumbs which fall from their masters’ table]]. She did not claim the children's place; she asked only for crumbs, trusting that even a crumb of Jesus' mercy would be enough.

Jesus answered, [[v28|Woman, great is your faith! Be it done to you even as you desire]]. Her daughter was healed at that moment. Many interpreters believe Jesus' initial silence and hard words were a test that drew out and displayed the woman's faith, in contrast to the unbelief of Israel's leaders in the first part of the chapter. Her faith, like the centurion's in Matthew 8, is called great.

### Healing and Feeding Many (15:29–39)

Jesus returned toward the Sea of Galilee, apparently to its eastern side, in the Decapolis region, which had a largely Gentile population (Mark 7:31). He went up a mountain and sat down. Great crowds brought the lame, blind, mute, crippled, and many others and laid them at His feet, and He healed them. The crowds were amazed and [[v31|glorified the God of Israel]], a phrase that suggests many in the crowd were Gentiles praising Israel's God.

Jesus then told His disciples that He had compassion on the crowd, which had been with Him for three days and had nothing to eat. He did not want to send them away hungry. The disciples asked where they could find enough bread in such a remote place, apparently forgetting the earlier feeding of the five thousand. They had seven loaves and a few small fish. Jesus gave thanks, broke the bread, and gave it to the disciples to distribute. Everyone ate and was satisfied, and seven large baskets of leftovers were collected. About four thousand men were fed, besides women and children.

This second feeding is similar to the first (Matthew 14), but its setting among Gentiles is significant. The first feeding, with twelve baskets left over, took place among Jews. The second, with seven baskets, took place in largely Gentile territory. Some see in the number seven a symbol of completeness, suggesting that Jesus' provision extends to the whole world. Jesus then sent the crowds away and went by boat to the region of Magdala.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 15:8 — Lips Near, Hearts Far

verse: Matthew 15:8

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to the Pharisees and scribes who came from Jerusalem to criticize His disciples for not washing their hands ritually before eating. Jesus had just shown how their tradition of "Corban" allowed people to avoid supporting their aging parents by declaring their resources devoted to God. In this way, they kept a human tradition while breaking God's clear command to honor father and mother.

After calling them hypocrites, Jesus quoted Isaiah 29:13. In Isaiah's day, around 700 B.C., the people of Jerusalem continued to offer sacrifices, keep festivals, and say the right words in worship, but their lives showed that they did not truly love or trust God. God said through Isaiah that the people [[v8|draw near to me with their mouth, and honor me with their lips; but their heart is far from me]].

Jesus applied these words to the religious leaders of His own time. They were very careful about outward religious practices. They spoke about God, kept many rules, and appeared devout. But their hearts were distant from God. Their concern for tradition had become more important than God's actual commands and more important than love for people.

The next verse completes the quotation: [[v9|And they worship me in vain, teaching as doctrine rules made by men]]. Their worship was empty because it was not rooted in a heart devoted to God, and because they had elevated human rules to the level of divine commands.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that God looks at the heart. Throughout Scripture, God makes clear that outward religion without inward devotion is unacceptable to Him. When Samuel looked for a king among Jesse's sons, God told him, [[1 Samuel 16:7|man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart]]. The prophets repeatedly criticized Israel for offering sacrifices while practicing injustice (Isaiah 1:10–17; Amos 5:21–24).

The verse also warns about the danger of tradition. Traditions can be helpful; they preserve wisdom and give structure to worship and life. But when traditions are treated as equal to or above God's Word, they can distort faith and even contradict God's commands. Jesus does not reject all tradition, but He insists that God's Word must judge every human practice.

Finally, the verse shows that hypocrisy is not only a problem of the Pharisees. Every religious person faces the temptation to say the right words and perform the right rituals while their heart is elsewhere. Jesus' warning applies to all who worship.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to examine whether their worship comes from the heart. It is possible to sing songs, recite prayers, attend services, and use religious language while the heart is distracted, indifferent, or even resistant to God. Worship becomes real when it involves honest love for God, a desire to obey Him, and a willingness to let Him change us.

It also calls believers to examine their traditions. Every church and family has practices and expectations, some written and some unspoken. These can be good, but they should be tested by Scripture. Believers can ask whether any tradition is preventing them from obeying God's clear commands, such as caring for family, loving neighbors, or showing mercy.

Finally, Jesus' specific example, neglecting parents through a religious loophole, shows that genuine faith includes practical care for family. Honoring parents, especially as they age, is part of honoring God. Religious activity should never become an excuse to neglect the people God has placed in our lives.

### Matthew 15:18–19 — What Comes From the Heart

verse: Matthew 15:18-19

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to His disciples after Peter asks Him to explain His saying about defilement. Earlier, Jesus told the crowd that what goes into the mouth does not defile a person, but what comes out of the mouth does. The disciples were concerned because the Pharisees had been offended. Peter asked for an explanation, and Jesus expressed surprise that they still did not understand.

He explained with a simple illustration. Food enters the mouth, goes into the stomach, and passes out of the body. It never reaches the heart, so it cannot make a person morally unclean. But [[v18|the things which proceed out of the mouth come out of the heart, and they defile the man]].

Then Jesus listed what comes from the heart: [[v19|evil thoughts, murders, adulteries, sexual sins, thefts, false testimony, and blasphemies]]. The list begins with evil thoughts, the inner source from which other sins grow. The rest closely follows the second half of the Ten Commandments: murder (the sixth commandment), adultery and sexual immorality (the seventh), theft (the eighth), false testimony (the ninth), and blasphemy, or slander, which damages others with words. These sins are not caused by external contamination. They come from within.

Jesus concludes in the next verse that these are what defile a person, but eating with unwashed hands does not. The Pharisees were focused on keeping their hands clean while their hearts produced the very things that make people unclean before God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This teaching reveals the true source of sin: the human heart. Jeremiah wrote, [[Jeremiah 17:9|The heart is deceitful above all things and it is exceedingly corrupt]]. Sin is not merely a matter of bad surroundings or bad influences; it arises from within. This means that external rules, however carefully kept, cannot solve the problem of sin. A new heart is needed.

The Old Testament prophets promised that God would one day give His people new hearts. Through Ezekiel, God said, [[Ezekiel 36:26|I will also give you a new heart, and I will put a new spirit within you]]. The New Testament teaches that this promise is fulfilled through Jesus and the Holy Spirit. Only God can cleanse the heart.

This teaching also had significant consequences for the early church. Jesus' words about food helped the first Christians understand that the food laws, which had separated Jews from Gentiles, no longer needed to divide believers. What matters before God is not the food on one's plate but the condition of one's heart.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses call believers to look beneath their outward behavior to the condition of their hearts. It is possible to avoid obvious sins while harboring bitterness, lust, greed, or contempt. Jesus teaches that such thoughts are the roots of sinful actions. Honest self-examination, asking God to reveal what is hidden, is the beginning of real change.

They also warn against blaming outside forces entirely for our sins. While circumstances and influences matter, Jesus places responsibility on the heart. Believers can take ownership of their sins, confess them honestly, and ask God for inner transformation rather than only changing external habits.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to seek God's cleansing. Because the problem is in the heart, the solution must reach the heart. Through prayer, confession, Scripture, and the work of the Holy Spirit, God renews people from within. Believers can pray as David did, [[Psalm 51:10|Create in me a clean heart, O God]], and trust that God answers such prayers.

### Matthew 15:28 — [[Matthew 15:28|Woman, Great Is Your Faith]]

verse: Matthew 15:28

#### Simple Explanation

This verse concludes Jesus' encounter with the Canaanite woman in the region of Tyre and Sidon. She had come to Jesus crying out for mercy because her daughter was severely tormented by a demon. She called Him "Lord" and "Son of David," recognizing Him as Israel's Messiah.

The conversation that followed was difficult. At first Jesus did not answer her. The disciples wanted Him to send her away. Jesus said that He was sent only to the lost sheep of Israel. When she knelt and pleaded, "Lord, help me," He said it was not right to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs. She answered with humility and quick wit: even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from the master's table.

Now Jesus responds with admiration: [[v28|Woman, great is your faith!]] He grants her request, and her daughter is healed at that very moment.

Why does Jesus call her faith great? First, she was persistent. She kept asking despite silence, the disciples' resistance, and apparent refusal. Second, she was humble. She did not demand her rights or claim to be worthy. She accepted her place as an outsider and asked only for crumbs. Third, she trusted Jesus' power and goodness completely. She believed that even a crumb of His mercy would be enough to heal her daughter.

Her faith stands in sharp contrast to the Pharisees in the first part of the chapter. They were Israel's religious leaders, yet their hearts were far from God. She was a Gentile, from a people associated with Israel's ancient enemies, yet she showed great faith.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This story shows that God's mercy is not limited by ethnicity, background, or status. Although Jesus' earthly mission was focused first on Israel, He responded to faith wherever He found it. This anticipates the spread of the gospel to all nations after His resurrection (Matthew 28:19). Paul later teaches that in Christ there is no distinction between Jew and Greek, for [[Romans 10:12|the same Lord is Lord of all, and is rich to all who call on him]].

The story also shows the nature of persistent faith. Jesus' initial silence and words were not a rejection of the woman. They gave her the opportunity to express her faith clearly and publicly. Elsewhere Jesus teaches His followers to keep asking and not lose heart (Luke 18:1–8). Faith sometimes means holding on to God's goodness even when His response seems slow or unclear.

Finally, the woman's humility is a picture of how all people come to God. No one has a right to God's mercy. Everyone comes, in a sense, asking for crumbs from the Master's table. Yet God, in His grace, gives not crumbs but the full bread of life.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to keep praying when answers are delayed. The woman did not give up when Jesus was silent or when His words were hard to hear. Believers who have prayed for a long time for a loved one, for healing, or for change can continue to bring their requests to God, trusting His goodness even when they do not understand His timing.

It also calls for humility in prayer. The woman did not argue that she deserved help. She appealed to Jesus' mercy. Believers can come to God not on the basis of their own worthiness but on the basis of His grace.

Finally, the story challenges believers to recognize faith in unexpected places. The disciples wanted to send the woman away; Jesus praised her. Churches and individuals can be quick to dismiss people who seem like outsiders. Jesus' example calls believers to welcome all who seek Him and to recognize that genuine faith may be found in people they would least expect.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 15 calls believers to examine the relationship between outward religion and inward reality. The Pharisees were careful about ritual washing but neglected God's command to care for their parents. Their lips honored God while their hearts were far from Him. Believers can ask whether any of their religious habits have become a substitute for genuine love for God and others. Worship that pleases God comes from the heart and shows itself in obedience and mercy.

The chapter also teaches that the source of sin is within. External rules and appearances cannot cleanse a heart that produces evil thoughts, sexual sin, dishonesty, and slander. The solution is not merely trying harder to behave better but asking God to change the heart. Confession, repentance, and dependence on the Holy Spirit are essential parts of genuine spiritual growth.

The story of the Canaanite woman provides a powerful example of persistent, humble faith. She kept asking despite obstacles and trusted that even a crumb of Jesus' mercy would be enough. Believers facing unanswered prayers or difficult circumstances can learn from her persistence and humility. They can also learn from Jesus' response to her: He welcomed someone others wanted to send away.

Finally, the healing and feeding of Gentile crowds show that God's compassion extends to all people. Followers of Jesus are called to share that compassion across ethnic, cultural, and social boundaries. The chapter invites believers to welcome outsiders, care for the needy, and recognize that God's table is open to everyone who comes to Him in faith.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You see past my words and outward habits to the condition of my heart. Forgive me for the times I have honored You with my lips while my heart was far from You. Show me where I have let traditions or appearances take the place of real love for You and for others, especially those in my own family.

Create in me a clean heart. Remove the evil thoughts, impurity, dishonesty, and harsh words that come from within, and fill me with Your Spirit. Give me the persistent, humble faith of the Canaanite woman, who kept asking and trusted that even a crumb of Your mercy was enough. Help me to welcome those whom others overlook and to share Your compassion with people of every background. Thank You that Your table is open to all who come to You in faith. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
