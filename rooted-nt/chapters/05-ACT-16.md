# Acts 16
theme: Led by the Spirit into Europe: Lydia, a Freed Slave Girl, and a Jailer Saved
main: Paul and Silas, now joined by Timothy, revisit the churches and are guided by the Holy Spirit through closed doors until a night vision calls them to Macedonia. In Philippi, the Lord opens the heart of Lydia, a businesswoman, and she and her household believe. Paul frees an enslaved girl from a spirit of divination, and her owners, angry at losing their income, have Paul and Silas beaten and jailed. At midnight they pray and sing hymns; an earthquake opens the doors, and the terrified jailer asks what he must do to be saved. He and his household believe in the Lord Jesus and are baptized, rejoicing.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 16 begins Paul's second missionary journey, around A.D. 49–52. In this chapter, the gospel crosses from Asia into Europe for the first time in Acts, as Paul and his companions travel to Macedonia, in what is now northern Greece. The church planted in Philippi would become one of Paul's most beloved congregations; years later he wrote them the letter we call Philippians, full of joy and affection.

This chapter also contains the first of the "we" passages in Acts (16:10–17), in which Luke writes in the first person. This suggests that Luke joined Paul at Troas and traveled with him to Philippi.

### Timothy Joins Paul (16:1–5)

In Lystra, where Paul had been stoned on his first journey, he met [[v1|a certain disciple was there, named Timothy, the son of a Jewess who believed, but his father was a Greek]]. Timothy's mother Eunice and grandmother Lois were believers who had taught him the Scriptures from childhood (2 Timothy 1:5; 3:15). [[v2|The brothers who were at Lystra and Iconium gave a good testimony about him]].

Paul wanted Timothy to join him. [[v3|he took and circumcised him because of the Jews who were in those parts, for they all knew that his father was a Greek]]. This may seem surprising, since Paul had just fought at the Jerusalem Council against requiring circumcision for Gentiles. But Timothy's situation was different. He had a Jewish mother, and by Jewish standards he was considered Jewish. An uncircumcised Jew would have been a barrier to Paul's ministry in synagogues. Paul circumcised Timothy not for salvation but to remove an obstacle to the gospel. Paul explained his approach: [[1 Corinthians 9:20|To the Jews I became as a Jew, that I might gain Jews]]. By contrast, Paul refused to circumcise Titus, a Gentile, when others insisted it was necessary (Galatians 2:3).

Timothy became one of Paul's closest companions. Paul later called him [[1 Timothy 1:2|my true child in faith]], and two New Testament letters are addressed to him.

As they traveled, they delivered the decisions of the Jerusalem Council, and [[v5|the assemblies were strengthened in the faith, and increased in number daily]].

### The Spirit's Guidance and the Macedonian Call (16:6–10)

Paul's team tried to go in several directions but were prevented. This guidance is studied below.

### Lydia's Conversion in Philippi (16:11–15)

The team sailed from Troas to Neapolis and traveled to [[v12|Philippi, which is a city of Macedonia, the foremost of the district, a Roman colony]]. Philippi was named after Philip II of Macedon, the father of Alexander the Great. As a Roman colony, it was settled by retired Roman soldiers and enjoyed special privileges. Its citizens were proud of their Roman identity.

Paul's usual practice was to begin at the synagogue. But Philippi apparently had no synagogue, which required ten Jewish men. So on the Sabbath, the team [[v13|went outside of the city by a riverside, where we supposed there was a place of prayer]]. They found a group of women gathered for prayer. Lydia's conversion is studied below.

### A Slave Girl Set Free (16:16–24)

As the team was going to the place of prayer, they met [[v16|a certain girl having a spirit of divination]]. The Greek literally says she had "a spirit of python," a reference to the serpent associated with the oracle of Apollo at Delphi, where prophets were believed to speak the god's messages. She [[v16|brought her masters much gain by fortune telling]]. She was a slave, exploited by her owners for profit.

She followed Paul and his companions, shouting, [[v17|These men are servants of the Most High God, who proclaim to us a way of salvation!]] Her words were true, but coming from a spirit of divination, they could confuse people, linking the gospel with fortune-telling. "Most High God" could also be understood by pagans as Zeus or another deity. This went on for many days. Finally, Paul, [[v18|becoming greatly annoyed]], turned and said to the spirit, [[v18|I command you in the name of Jesus Christ to come out of her!]] It left her that very hour.

The girl was freed, but her owners were furious, [[v19|when her masters saw that the hope of their gain was gone]]. They cared more about their profits than about the girl's freedom. They dragged Paul and Silas to the magistrates and accused them: [[v20|These men, being Jews, are agitating our city]] [[v21|and advocate customs which it is not lawful for us to accept or to observe, being Romans]]. The accusation appealed to anti-Jewish prejudice and Roman pride. The real reason, lost profits, was hidden.

The crowd joined the attack, and the magistrates had Paul and Silas stripped and beaten with rods, a Roman punishment. Paul later mentioned that he had been beaten with rods three times (2 Corinthians 11:25). After a severe beating, they were thrown into prison, and the jailer put them in the inner cell with their feet fastened in stocks.

### Midnight Songs and an Earthquake (16:25–34)

What Paul and Silas did in prison, and what followed, is studied below.

### Paul Demands Justice (16:35–40)

In the morning, the magistrates sent officers to release Paul and Silas quietly. But Paul refused: [[v37|They have beaten us publicly without a trial, men who are Romans, and have cast us into prison! Do they now release us secretly? No, most certainly, but let them come themselves and bring us out!]]

Roman citizens had legal rights, including the right to a trial before punishment. Beating a Roman citizen without trial was a serious crime. When the magistrates heard that Paul and Silas were Romans, they were alarmed. They came personally, apologized, escorted them out, and asked them to leave the city.

Why did Paul insist on this? He was not seeking revenge. By having the magistrates publicly acknowledge the injustice, Paul helped protect the young church in Philippi from being treated as a criminal group. Standing up for justice was not contrary to his faith; it served the gospel and protected vulnerable believers.

Paul and Silas went to Lydia's house, encouraged the believers, and departed. The church in Philippi now included a wealthy businesswoman, a freed slave girl (presumably), and a Roman jailer with his household, a vivid picture of how the gospel unites people across social lines.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 16:6–10 — Doors Closed and a Door Opened

verse: Acts 16:6-10

#### Simple Explanation

Paul, Silas, and Timothy had been visiting the churches from Paul's first journey. Now they wanted to take the gospel to new regions. But something unusual happened.

[[v6|When they had gone through the region of Phrygia and Galatia, they were forbidden by the Holy Spirit to speak the word in Asia]]. "Asia" here refers to the Roman province on the western coast of what is now Turkey, including the great city of Ephesus. It seemed like a natural place to go next. But the Holy Spirit said no. Luke does not say how; perhaps through a prophecy, circumstances, or an inner conviction.

[[v7|When they had come opposite Mysia, they tried to go into Bithynia, but the Spirit didn’t allow them]]. They tried another direction, north toward Bithynia. Again, the Spirit, here called [[v7|the Spirit]] (some manuscripts read "the Spirit of Jesus"), prevented them.

Two doors had closed. They traveled west to Troas, a port city on the Aegean Sea. Then: [[v9|A vision appeared to Paul in the night. There was a man of Macedonia standing, begging him and saying, “Come over into Macedonia and help us.”]]

[[v10|When he had seen the vision, immediately we sought to go out to Macedonia, concluding that the Lord had called us to preach the Good News to them]]. Notice the word "we." Luke joins the story here. Notice also the word "concluding." The team discussed the vision together and concluded that God was calling them to Macedonia. They acted immediately.

The Spirit's "no" to Asia was not permanent. Later, Paul spent nearly three years in Ephesus, and the gospel spread throughout Asia (19:10). God's timing was different from Paul's.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses show that God guides His people both by closing doors and by opening them. Sometimes His guidance comes as a clear call; sometimes it comes as a series of closed doors. Proverbs says, [[Proverbs 16:9|A man’s heart plans his course, but the LORD directs his steps]].

They also show that guidance often comes while believers are moving. Paul and his team were actively trying to serve God. They did not sit and wait for a vision; they made plans and took steps. God redirected them along the way.

Finally, these verses show the importance of community in discernment. Paul received the vision, but the team together "concluded" what it meant. God often guides through the shared wisdom of believers.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers who face closed doors. When plans fail or opportunities disappear, it can be discouraging. But God may be closing one door to lead toward another. Believers can trust that God is directing their steps, even through disappointment.

They also encourage believers to keep moving in faith. Paul did not stop serving when doors closed. He kept going until God made the direction clear. Believers can make plans and take faithful steps, staying open to God's redirection.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to seek guidance together. Important decisions can be discussed with trusted believers who can help discern God's will through prayer, Scripture, and wise counsel.

### Acts 16:14 — The Lord Opened Her Heart

verse: Acts 16:14

#### Simple Explanation

In Philippi, there was no synagogue, so Paul and his companions went to a place by the river where they thought people would gather to pray. They found a group of women and sat down to talk with them.

[[v14|A certain woman named Lydia, a seller of purple, of the city of Thyatira, one who worshiped God, heard us]]. Lydia was from Thyatira, a city in the province of Asia known for its purple dye. She was a businesswoman who sold expensive purple cloth, a luxury item worn by the wealthy and by royalty. She was likely wealthy and independent, since she had a household of her own.

Lydia [[v14|worshiped God]]. Like Cornelius, she was a Gentile who believed in the God of Israel. She was spiritually seeking, gathering with others to pray.

[[v14|The Lord opened her heart to listen to the things which were spoken by Paul]]. Lydia listened to Paul's message, but her response was not merely the result of Paul's persuasive speaking. The Lord opened her heart. God worked within her to help her understand and receive the gospel.

Then [[v15|When she and her household were baptized]], she immediately showed hospitality: [[v15|If you have judged me to be faithful to the Lord, come into my house and stay]]. She insisted, and they accepted. Her house became the first meeting place of the church in Philippi (16:40).

Interestingly, Paul's vision was of "a man of Macedonia," but the first convert in Macedonia was a woman from Asia, the very province where the Spirit had forbidden them to preach.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that conversion is the work of God. Paul preached, but the Lord opened Lydia's heart. Jesus had said, [[John 6:44|No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him]]. Faith comes when God opens hearts to receive His word.

It also shows the importance of faithful preaching. God opened Lydia's heart "to listen to the things which were spoken by Paul." God uses the message of the gospel, shared by His people, to bring people to faith. Paul writes that faith comes by hearing the word of Christ (Romans 10:17).

Finally, the verse shows that the gospel transforms people's use of their resources. Lydia immediately opened her home to the missionaries. Her conversion led to hospitality and generosity. Throughout the New Testament, believers' homes became centers of church life.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to share the gospel with confidence, knowing that God is the one who opens hearts. Believers do not need to rely on their own persuasiveness. They can share faithfully and pray for God to work.

It also encourages believers to pray for those who are seeking. Lydia was already worshiping God and gathering to pray. Many people today are spiritually seeking. Believers can pray that God would open their hearts to the gospel.

Finally, the verse invites believers to use their homes and resources for God's kingdom. Lydia's home became a place of hospitality and fellowship. Believers can open their homes to others, offering welcome, meals, and a place for believers to gather.

### Acts 16:25 — Praying and Singing at Midnight

verse: Acts 16:25

#### Simple Explanation

Paul and Silas had been through a terrible day. They had freed a slave girl from a spirit of divination, and her owners had dragged them before the magistrates. A crowd attacked them. They were stripped, beaten severely with rods, and thrown into the inner prison, the darkest and most secure part, with their feet fastened in wooden stocks. Their backs were bleeding. They were in pain, in darkness, unable to move.

[[v25|But about midnight Paul and Silas were praying and singing hymns to God]]. In the middle of the night, in pain and chains, they prayed and sang. They did not complain, curse their captors, or despair. They worshiped God.

The hymns they sang may have been psalms, many of which speak of trusting God in times of trouble. For example, [[Psalm 34:1|I will bless the LORD at all times. His praise will always be in my mouth]].

[[v25|and the prisoners were listening to them]]. The other prisoners heard them. In that dark place, they heard prayers and songs instead of groans and curses. Paul and Silas's worship was a witness.

Then [[v26|Suddenly there was a great earthquake, so that the foundations of the prison were shaken; and immediately all the doors were opened, and everyone’s bonds were loosened]].

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that joy in God does not depend on circumstances. Paul later wrote to the Philippians, the church founded in this very city, [[Philippians 4:4|Rejoice in the Lord always! Again I will say, “Rejoice!”]] He had learned this joy in the Philippian jail.

It also shows that worship in suffering is a powerful witness. The prisoners were listening. People notice how believers respond to hardship. Praise in the midst of pain points to a God who is worthy of trust even in the darkest places.

Finally, the verse shows that God hears the prayers and praises of His people. The earthquake came as Paul and Silas worshiped. God is present with His people in their suffering and is able to act on their behalf.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to turn to God in prayer and praise during difficult times. When pain, fear, or injustice comes, believers can bring their hurts to God and choose to worship Him. This does not mean pretending everything is fine; many psalms of praise also include honest lament. It means trusting God enough to praise Him even in the darkness.

It also reminds believers that others are watching. Family members, coworkers, and friends notice how believers respond to suffering. Faithful worship in hard times can draw others to God.

Finally, the verse offers hope to those in "midnight" moments, times of deep darkness, whether illness, loss, depression, or imprisonment. God is present in those moments. Believers can pray and sing, trusting that He hears and is able to bring light and freedom.

### Acts 16:30–31 — [[Acts 16:30|What Must I Do to Be Saved?]]

verse: Acts 16:30-31

#### Simple Explanation

A great earthquake had shaken the prison. All the doors flew open, and all the prisoners' chains came loose. The jailer woke up and saw the doors open. He assumed the prisoners had escaped. Under Roman law, a jailer who lost his prisoners could face execution. In despair, he drew his sword to kill himself.

But Paul shouted, [[v28|Don’t harm yourself, for we are all here!]] None of the prisoners had escaped. Paul cared for the man who had locked him in the stocks.

The jailer called for lights, rushed in, and fell trembling before Paul and Silas. Then he brought them out and asked, [[v30|Sirs, what must I do to be saved?]] This was the most important question anyone could ask. The jailer may have heard the slave girl's words that these men proclaimed "a way of salvation" (16:17), or heard Paul and Silas praying and singing. Now, shaken by the earthquake and by the prisoners' refusal to escape, he wanted to know how to be saved.

Paul and Silas answered, [[v31|Believe in the Lord Jesus Christ, and you will be saved, you and your household]]. The answer was simple. Salvation comes through believing in Jesus as Lord and Christ. No rituals, payments, or achievements were required.

The promise included "your household." This does not mean the jailer's family would be saved automatically through his faith. Rather, the same offer was extended to everyone in his household. Paul and Silas [[v32|spoke the word of the Lord to him, and to all who were in his house]]. The household heard the message and believed.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses summarize the gospel in its simplest form: believe in the Lord Jesus Christ, and you will be saved. Salvation is not earned; it is received by faith. Paul writes, [[Romans 10:9|if you will confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord, and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved]].

They also show the transformation that comes with faith. That same night, the jailer [[v33|washed their stripes]], caring for the wounds of the men he had imprisoned. He was baptized with his household. He brought them into his home, gave them food, and [[v34|rejoiced greatly with all his household, having believed in God]]. The man who had been about to take his own life was now filled with joy.

Finally, these verses show that no one is beyond the reach of the gospel. The jailer was a Roman official, probably a retired soldier, part of the system that had beaten and imprisoned God's servants. Yet he was saved. God's grace reaches all kinds of people.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses give the clearest answer to life's most important question. Anyone who asks, "What must I do to be saved?" can receive the same answer: believe in the Lord Jesus Christ. Believers can share this simple message with confidence.

They also show that faith changes lives. The jailer's faith led to compassion, hospitality, and joy. Believers can let their faith shape how they treat others, especially those they once opposed or ignored.

Finally, these verses encourage believers to care even for those who mistreat them. Paul could have escaped but chose to stay, saving the jailer's life. Believers can show grace and compassion to those who wrong them, opening the way for them to encounter Christ.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 16 shows how God guides His people. Paul's team faced closed doors before receiving a clear call to Macedonia. Believers today can trust God's guidance through both closed and open doors, continuing to serve faithfully while staying open to His redirection, and seeking discernment together with other believers.

The chapter also shows that God opens hearts. Lydia listened, and the Lord opened her heart to receive the gospel. Believers can share their faith with confidence, praying for God to work in the hearts of those who hear. Like Lydia, those who believe can use their homes and resources to serve God's people.

Acts 16 shows the power of worship in suffering. Paul and Silas, beaten and chained, prayed and sang at midnight. Their worship became a witness. Believers facing their own dark moments can turn to God in prayer and praise, trusting that He hears them and is present with them.

Finally, the chapter shows the simple, life-changing power of the gospel. The jailer asked, "What must I do to be saved?" and received the answer: "Believe in the Lord Jesus Christ." The gospel freed a slave girl from spiritual bondage, brought a businesswoman to faith, and transformed a jailer and his household. Believers can share this message with all kinds of people, and they can stand for justice, as Paul did, especially for the vulnerable.
