# 1 Peter 2
theme: Living Stones, a Chosen People, and the Example of Christ
main: Peter urges believers to rid themselves of malice, deceit, hypocrisy, envy, and slander, and like newborn babies to crave the pure milk of God's word so they may grow. Coming to Christ, the living Stone rejected by people but chosen and precious to God, they are being built as living stones into a spiritual house and a holy priesthood offering spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God. For those who believe, Christ is the precious cornerstone; for those who reject Him, He is a stone that makes them stumble. Believers are a chosen people, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God's own possession, called out of darkness into His marvelous light to declare His praises; once they were not a people, but now they are God's people and have received mercy. As foreigners and exiles, they must abstain from sinful desires and live such good lives among unbelievers that, though slandered, their good deeds will lead others to glorify God. For the Lord's sake they are to submit to human authorities, live as free people who are servants of God, and honor everyone. Servants are to respect their masters, even harsh ones, since enduring unjust suffering with a clear conscience pleases God. In this they follow Christ, who suffered for them, leaving an example: He did not sin, did not retaliate, but entrusted Himself to the righteous Judge, and He bore our sins in His body on the cross so that we might die to sin and live for righteousness. By His wounds we are healed, and we have returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of our souls.

## Chapter Background and Story

In chapter 1, Peter reminded believers of their living hope and called them to holiness and love. In chapter 2, he describes their identity as God's people and how that identity should shape their lives in a hostile society.

The believers Peter wrote to were often slandered as "evildoers" (v12). Their neighbors may have accused them of being antisocial, disloyal to the empire, or immoral, because they no longer joined in civic religious festivals and pagan practices. Peter's answer is twofold. First, believers must remember who they are: God's chosen, precious people, with a dignity no one can take away. Second, they must live honorably, so that slander is shown to be false and others are drawn to glorify God.

The second half of the chapter begins a section on how believers should relate to authorities and social structures (2:13–3:7). This section includes instructions for servants, many of whom were enslaved people in Roman households, about enduring unjust treatment. These instructions must be read carefully in their historical setting, as explained below. Peter grounds everything in the example of Christ, who suffered unjustly for others.

### Craving Spiritual Milk (2:1–3)

[[v1|Putting away therefore all wickedness, all deceit, hypocrisies, envies, and all evil speaking]]. Before growth, there must be removal. These sins, malice, deceit, hypocrisy, envy, and slander, destroy the sincere love Peter called for in 1 Peter 1:22. [[v2|as newborn babies, long for the pure spiritual milk, that with it you may grow]], [[v3|if indeed you have tasted that the Lord is gracious]]. Babies instinctively crave milk. Believers, born again through God's word (1:23), should crave that word with the same intensity. "Pure spiritual milk" is God's word, the nourishment that helps them grow. Peter echoes Psalm 34:8: [[Psalm 34:8|Oh taste and see that the LORD is good]]. Those who have tasted God's goodness will want more.

### Living Stones (2:4–8)

The image of living stones and a spiritual house is studied below. [[v6|Because it is contained in Scripture, “Behold, I lay in Zion a chief cornerstone, chosen and precious. He who believes in him will not be disappointed.”]] Peter quotes Isaiah 28:16. Christ is the cornerstone of God's new temple, chosen and precious.

[[v7|For you who believe therefore is the honor, but for those who are disobedient, “The stone which the builders rejected has become the chief cornerstone,”]] [[v8|and, “a stumbling stone and a rock of offense.” For they stumble at the word, being disobedient, to which also they were appointed]]. Peter quotes Psalm 118:22, which Jesus applied to Himself (Mark 12:10), and Isaiah 8:14. The same stone that is a foundation for believers is a stumbling stone for those who reject it. People stumble because they refuse to obey the word. "To which also they were appointed" has been understood in different ways: some see it as God's sovereign appointment of judgment for unbelief, others as the appointed consequence of disobedience. Either way, unbelief brings its own downfall.

### Submission to Authorities (2:13–17)

[[v13|Therefore subject yourselves to every ordinance of man for the Lord’s sake: whether to the king, as supreme]], [[v14|or to governors, as sent by him for vengeance on evildoers and for praise to those who do well]]. Believers are to respect and obey civil authorities "for the Lord's sake," as part of their witness and obedience to God. The emperor at the time was likely Nero. Government's God-given role is to punish wrongdoing and commend good (Romans 13:1–7). Submission to authorities has limits: when rulers command what God forbids, believers must obey God rather than people (Acts 5:29).

[[v15|For this is the will of God, that by well-doing you should put to silence the ignorance of foolish men]]. Good conduct silences false accusations. [[v16|Live as free people, yet not using your freedom for a cloak of wickedness, but as bondservants of God]]. Believers are truly free in Christ, but their freedom is not an excuse for evil. They use it to serve God.

[[v17|Honor all men. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the king]]. This short verse summarizes Christian social ethics. Every person deserves honor, because each bears God's image. Fellow believers deserve love. God deserves reverent fear. And the king deserves honor, but notice that only God receives "fear." The emperor is honored, but God alone is worshiped.

### Servants and Unjust Suffering (2:18–20)

[[v18|Servants, be in subjection to your masters with all respect, not only to the good and gentle, but also to the wicked]]. "Servants" here refers to household slaves, who made up a large part of the Roman population and had few rights. Many early Christians were slaves. Peter addresses them directly as moral agents, members of God's people with dignity and responsibility.

[[v19|For it is commendable if someone endures pain, suffering unjustly, because of conscience toward God]]. [[v20|For what glory is it if, when you sin, you patiently endure beating? But if when you do well, you patiently endure suffering, this is commendable with God]]. Peter acknowledges that some masters were harsh and that slaves were sometimes beaten unjustly. In a situation they could not escape, believing slaves could endure with a clear conscience before God, and God would see and commend them.

These verses must be read with great care. Peter is giving pastoral comfort to people trapped in an unjust system that they had no power to leave. He is not approving of slavery, and he is not saying that abuse is acceptable or that victims should stay in abusive situations when they can escape. Scripture elsewhere condemns slave-trading (1 Timothy 1:10), encourages slaves to gain freedom when possible (1 Corinthians 7:21), and calls masters to treat slaves justly (Colossians 4:1). Tragically, these verses have sometimes been misused to tell victims of domestic abuse or exploitation to stay and endure. That is a serious misuse of Scripture. God is a God of justice who hears the cry of the oppressed. Those who are being abused should seek safety and help, and those who know of abuse should act to protect victims. What Peter's words do offer is the assurance that God sees unjust suffering, that it is never meaningless to Him, and that those who suffer can find strength in Christ, who suffered unjustly too.

## Important Verses Explained

### 1 Peter 2:4–5 — Living Stones in a Spiritual House

verse: 1 Peter 2:4-5

#### Simple Explanation

"Come to him, a living stone." Jesus is described as a "living stone." Stones are normally lifeless, but Christ is alive, risen from the dead. He is the foundation stone of God's new temple.

"Rejected indeed by men, but chosen by God, precious." People rejected Jesus; the religious leaders condemned Him and the crowds called for His crucifixion. But God chose Him and values Him as precious.

Verse 5: "You also as living stones are built up as a spiritual house." Believers, too, are living stones. As they come to Christ, they are built together into a "spiritual house," a temple where God dwells. The church is not a building of stone but a living community.

"To be a holy priesthood, to offer up spiritual sacrifices, acceptable to God through Jesus Christ." Believers are not only the temple; they are also its priests. Every believer has access to God and the privilege of serving Him. Their sacrifices are "spiritual": praise, prayer, good works, generosity, and lives offered to God (Romans 12:1; Hebrews 13:15–16). These are acceptable to God because they are offered through Jesus Christ.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This image would have been powerful for Peter, whose name means "rock" or "stone." Jesus had said, [[Matthew 16:18|On this rock I will build my assembly]]. Peter now applies the image of stones to all believers. The church is built on Christ, the cornerstone, and every believer is a stone in the building.

In the Old Testament, God dwelt in the tabernacle and then in the temple in Jerusalem. Only priests from Aaron's family could serve there. Now God dwells in His people, and every believer is a priest. This is sometimes called "the priesthood of all believers." It does not eliminate the need for leaders and teachers, but it affirms that every believer has direct access to God and a role in His service.

The phrase "living stones" also emphasizes that the church is not static. It is growing, alive, and being built. Each believer contributes to the whole.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can see themselves as part of something larger than themselves. They are living stones in God's spiritual house, connected to other believers and built together on Christ.

They can also embrace their priestly calling. Every believer can approach God directly in prayer and can offer Him spiritual sacrifices: worship, praise, acts of kindness, generosity, and faithful service in daily life.

Finally, those who feel rejected by others can remember that Christ was also rejected by people but chosen and precious to God. Believers, too, are precious to Him.

### 1 Peter 2:9–10 — A Chosen People, a Royal Priesthood

verse: 1 Peter 2:9-10

#### Simple Explanation

In contrast to those who stumble (v8), Peter describes the identity of believers with titles once given to Israel (Exodus 19:5–6; Deuteronomy 7:6; Isaiah 43:20–21).

"But you are a chosen race." Believers are chosen by God. Their identity does not depend on ethnicity but on God's choice.

"A royal priesthood." They are priests who serve the King, and they share in His royal dignity.

"A holy nation." They are set apart for God, a people distinct from the world.

"A people for God’s own possession." They belong to God. They are His treasured people.

"That you may proclaim the excellence of him who called you out of darkness into his marvelous light." The purpose of this identity is to declare God's praises, to make known His wonderful character and works. God has brought them out of the darkness of sin and ignorance into the light of His truth and love.

Verse 10: "In the past, you were not a people, but now are God’s people, who had not obtained mercy, but now have obtained mercy." Peter echoes Hosea 1:6–10 and 2:23. Many of his readers were Gentiles who had once been outside God's people. Now, by His mercy, they belong to Him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses describe the dignity and purpose of God's people. Believers who were slandered, rejected, and treated as outsiders by their society are told that in God's eyes they are chosen, royal, holy, and treasured. Their identity is defined not by how the world sees them but by how God sees them.

The titles applied to believers were first given to Israel at Mount Sinai. In Christ, Gentile believers share in these privileges alongside Jewish believers. Christians have understood the relationship between Israel and the church in different ways, but Paul affirms that Gentile believers are grafted into God's people (Romans 11:17) without replacing Israel, whose calling remains (Romans 11:29).

The purpose of this identity is mission: to "proclaim the excellence" of God. Believers are not chosen merely for their own benefit but to be a light to the world, declaring God's goodness by their words and lives.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers who feel insignificant, rejected, or overlooked can rest in their true identity. They are chosen, royal, holy, and God's own possession.

This identity also gives purpose. Believers can look for ways to declare God's praises in their families, workplaces, and communities, sharing the good news of how He brought them out of darkness into light.

Finally, believers can remember the mercy they have received. They were once outside God's people, but now they belong. That memory can keep them humble and eager to welcome others.

### 1 Peter 2:11–12 — Good Conduct Among the Nations

verse: 1 Peter 2:11-12

#### Simple Explanation

"Beloved, I beg you as foreigners and pilgrims." Peter speaks tenderly ("beloved") and urgently ("I beg you"). He reminds believers that they are "foreigners and pilgrims," temporary residents in this world, with their true home in God's kingdom.

"To abstain from fleshly lusts which war against the soul." Sinful desires are like an enemy army attacking the soul. Believers must resist them.

Verse 12: "having good behavior among the nations." "The nations" means unbelievers, the surrounding society. Believers are to live honorably, with conduct that is good, attractive, and admirable.

"So in that of which they speak against you as evildoers." Unbelievers were slandering them, accusing them of wrongdoing.

"They may see your good works and glorify God in the day of visitation." Over time, believers' good deeds will be seen, and some who once accused them may come to glorify God. "The day of visitation" may mean the day God visits people with salvation, when they come to faith, or the final day of judgment, when all will acknowledge God.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses describe the believer's posture in a hostile world. They are not to retreat or retaliate, but to live in such a way that their lives speak for themselves. Jesus taught, [[Matthew 5:16|Let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven]].

The phrase "foreigners and pilgrims" connects with the theme of exile that runs through 1 Peter. Believers live in the world but are not shaped by it. They have different values, priorities, and hopes. This difference may bring misunderstanding, but it also makes their lives a witness.

The early church grew remarkably in the face of persecution, partly because of the way Christians lived: caring for the poor, the sick, and abandoned infants, showing love across social barriers, and enduring suffering with grace. Their good conduct drew many to faith.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Believers can ask how their conduct appears to those outside the faith. Are they known for honesty, kindness, generosity, and integrity? Or does their behavior give others reasons to reject the gospel?

When believers face criticism or misunderstanding because of their faith, they can respond not with anger but with consistent goodness, trusting that God will vindicate them in His time.

Finally, believers can resist sinful desires, recognizing them as enemies of the soul, and pursue lives that honor God and draw others to Him.

### 1 Peter 2:21–24 — Following in His Steps

verse: 1 Peter 2:21-24

#### Simple Explanation

"For you were called to this, because Christ also suffered for us, leaving you an example, that you should follow his steps." Following Christ includes the possibility of suffering. Christ suffered "for us," on our behalf. He also left "an example," literally a pattern used to teach children to write letters by tracing over them. Believers are to trace His steps.

Verse 22: "who didn't sin, 'neither was deceit found in his mouth.'" Quoting Isaiah 53:9, Peter emphasizes Christ's innocence. He suffered unjustly.

Verse 23: "When he was cursed, he didn't curse back. When he suffered, he didn't threaten, but committed himself to him who judges righteously." Jesus did not retaliate against those who insulted and tortured Him. He did not threaten revenge. Instead, He entrusted Himself to God, the righteous Judge, who would make all things right.

Verse 24: "He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree." Christ's suffering was not only an example; it was a sacrifice. He carried our sins in His own body on the cross ("the tree").

"That we, having died to sins, might live to righteousness." The purpose was transformation. Through His death, believers are freed from sin's power to live righteous lives.

"You were healed by his wounds." Quoting Isaiah 53:5, Peter declares that Christ's wounds bring healing, especially the healing of the soul from sin. For enslaved readers, who knew the pain of beatings, the image of Christ's wounds bringing healing would have been especially moving.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses combine two aspects of Christ's suffering: example and substitution. As an example, Christ shows believers how to respond to unjust suffering: without sin, without retaliation, entrusting themselves to God. As a substitute, Christ bore believers' sins, doing for them what they could not do for themselves. The example would be crushing without the substitution; the substitution gives power to follow the example.

Peter draws heavily on Isaiah 53, the prophecy of the Suffering Servant. Peter, who once rebuked Jesus for speaking of His suffering (Mark 8:31–33), now understands that the cross was the center of God's plan.

Christ's example of not retaliating does not mean passive acceptance of all injustice. Jesus spoke truth boldly, confronted wrongdoing, and challenged those who struck Him unjustly (John 18:22–23). His refusal to retaliate means He did not seek personal revenge but entrusted judgment to God. Believers, too, can seek justice through proper means and protect themselves and others from harm, while refusing personal vengeance.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

When believers are insulted or treated unfairly, they can follow Christ's example: refusing to insult back, refusing to threaten or seek revenge, and entrusting themselves to God, who judges justly. This is difficult, but Christ's strength makes it possible.

At the same time, following Christ's example does not require staying in situations of abuse. Seeking safety and justice is consistent with entrusting oneself to God.

Finally, believers can find healing in Christ's wounds. Whatever wounds they carry, from their own sin or from what others have done to them, Christ's suffering brings healing and restoration. They have returned to the Shepherd and Overseer of their souls (v25), who cares for them completely.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

First Peter 2 tells believers who they are. They are living stones, built together on Christ, the living Stone, into a spiritual house. They are a holy priesthood, offering spiritual sacrifices to God. They are a chosen people, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God's own possession, called out of darkness into His marvelous light. Though the world may reject or slander them, they are precious to God.

This identity gives believers purpose: to grow by craving the pure milk of God's word, to declare God's praises, and to live as foreigners and pilgrims, resisting sinful desires. Their good conduct among unbelievers can silence false accusations and lead others to glorify God.

Peter calls believers to respect human authorities for the Lord's sake, within the limits of obedience to God, to use their freedom to serve God, and to honor everyone. His instructions to servants who suffered unjustly offer comfort to people in situations they could not escape, assuring them that God sees and values their faithful endurance. These words must never be used to keep anyone in abuse; God is on the side of the oppressed, and seeking safety is right.

At the heart of the chapter is Christ, who suffered unjustly, did not retaliate, and entrusted Himself to the righteous Judge. He bore our sins in His body on the cross so that we might die to sin and live for righteousness. By His wounds believers are healed. Whatever they face, they can follow in His steps and rest in the care of the Shepherd and Overseer of their souls.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, You are the living Stone, rejected by people but chosen and precious to God. Thank You for making me a living stone in Your spiritual house and a priest who can offer spiritual sacrifices to God. Give me a hunger for the pure milk of Your word, that I may grow. Thank You that I am chosen, part of a royal priesthood and a holy nation, Your own possession, called out of darkness into Your marvelous light. Help me to declare Your praises. As a foreigner and pilgrim in this world, help me to resist sinful desires and to live in a way that leads others to glorify You. When I am insulted or treated unjustly, help me to follow Your example, refusing to retaliate and entrusting myself to the righteous Judge. Protect all who suffer abuse and lead them to safety. Thank You for bearing my sins in Your body on the cross. By Your wounds I am healed. You are the Shepherd and Overseer of my soul. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
