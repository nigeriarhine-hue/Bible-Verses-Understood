# Mark 7
theme: Clean Hearts, Open Ears, and Mercy Beyond Israel
main: When Pharisees criticize Jesus' disciples for eating without ritual handwashing, Jesus exposes how human tradition can override God's commands and teaches that real defilement comes from the heart. He then travels into Gentile territory, where He honors the faith of a Greek woman and heals a deaf man with the word "Ephphatha," prompting the crowd to declare that He has done all things well.

## Chapter Background and Story

Mark 7 follows the feeding of the five thousand and Jesus walking on the water (Mark 6). It parallels Matthew 15, but Mark adds explanations for his mostly Gentile readers, who would not have known Jewish customs, and he includes a healing found in no other Gospel. The chapter has a clear movement. It begins with a dispute about purity among Jews from Jerusalem and ends with Jesus ministering to Gentiles, people considered unclean by strict Jews. The teaching in the first half explains the actions in the second half: if uncleanness is a matter of the heart rather than of food or ethnicity, then Gentiles can receive God's mercy too.

### Handwashing and the Tradition of the Elders (7:1–13)

Pharisees and some scribes who had come from Jerusalem gathered around Jesus. They noticed that some of His disciples were eating with [[v2|defiled, that is unwashed, hands]]. Mark explains the custom for his readers: the Pharisees and many Jews would not eat unless they had washed their hands in a ceremonial way, following [[v3|the tradition of the elders]]. When they came from the marketplace, where they might have contact with Gentiles or unclean things, they washed before eating. They also observed many other washings of cups, pitchers, and bronze kettles.

This washing was not about hygiene. The law of Moses required priests to wash before serving at the altar (Exodus 30:17–21). The Pharisees extended such purity rules to ordinary people in daily life, hoping to make all Israel holy. The "tradition of the elders" was the body of oral interpretation that applied the written law to everyday situations. Pharisees believed these traditions were authoritative.

They asked Jesus why His disciples did not follow these traditions. Jesus answered by quoting Isaiah 29:13: [[v6|This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me]]. Then He made His central accusation, studied below: [[v8|For you set aside the commandment of God, and hold tightly to the tradition of men]].

Jesus gave an example. Moses commanded, "Honor your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12), and declared that anyone who cursed his parents deserved death (Exodus 21:17). Honoring parents included supporting them financially in old age. But the scribes taught that if someone declared his money or property [[v11|Corban]], meaning dedicated to God, he was no longer obligated to use it to help his parents. Mark translates the Hebrew term for his readers. A person could keep using the property himself while claiming it was devoted to God, and the vow was considered binding even if it harmed his parents. In this way, a human tradition cancelled one of the Ten Commandments. Jesus said, [[v13|You do many things like this]].

### What Truly Defiles (7:14–23)

Jesus called the crowd and said, [[v15|There is nothing from outside of the man that going into him can defile him; but the things which proceed out of the man are those that defile the man]]. (Verse 16 is missing from many early manuscripts.)

When He went into a house, the disciples asked Him about this saying. Jesus was surprised they did not understand. He explained that food enters the stomach, not the heart, and passes out of the body. Mark adds a significant comment: [[v19|making all foods clean]]. In the WEB, these words are printed as part of Jesus' sentence; many translations treat them as Mark's explanation of what Jesus' teaching implied. Either way, the point is clear. The food laws of Leviticus 11, which had distinguished Israel from other nations, were being fulfilled and set aside in Jesus. The early church came to understand this fully later (Acts 10; Romans 14:14).

Then Jesus listed what comes from within the human heart: [[v21|evil thoughts, adulteries, sexual sins, murders, thefts]], and [[v22|covetings, wickedness, deceit, lustful desires, an evil eye, blasphemy, pride, and foolishness]]. Mark's list is longer than Matthew's (see Matthew 15:19, studied in that commentary). It includes both actions and attitudes: greed, deceit, envy (an "evil eye"), slander, arrogance, and moral foolishness. Jesus concluded that these evil things come from inside and defile a person.

### The Syrophoenician Woman (7:24–30)

Jesus then went to the region of Tyre, a Gentile city on the Mediterranean coast. He entered a house and did not want anyone to know He was there, perhaps seeking rest from the crowds, but He could not stay hidden. A woman whose little daughter had an unclean spirit heard about Him and came and fell at His feet. Mark describes her carefully: she was [[v26|a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race]]. "Greek" means she was a Gentile, culturally Greek; "Syrophoenician" means she was from Phoenicia in the Roman province of Syria. She begged Jesus to drive the demon out of her daughter.

Jesus said, [[v27|Let the children be filled first, for it is not appropriate to take the children’s bread and throw it to the dogs]]. The "children" were Israel; "dogs" was a term sometimes used for Gentiles. Mark alone includes the word "first," which suggests that Jesus' ministry was directed first to Israel, but not exclusively. The word for "dogs" refers to small household dogs, which softens the image, but the statement still tested the woman.

She answered with humility and insight: [[v28|Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs]]. She accepted that Israel had priority, but she trusted that there was enough mercy to overflow even to her. Jesus replied, [[v29|For this saying, go your way. The demon has gone out of your daughter]]. She went home and found her child lying on the bed, the demon gone. (Matthew's account of this encounter, which emphasizes her "great faith," is studied at Matthew 15:28.)

### The Healing of a Deaf Man (7:31–37)

Jesus then traveled north to Sidon and then southeast to the Sea of Galilee, into the region of the Decapolis, the same Gentile region where the man freed from a legion of demons had been telling everyone what Jesus had done for him (Mark 5:20). Some people brought to Jesus a man who was deaf and could hardly speak, and they begged Jesus to place His hand on him.

Jesus took the man aside, away from the crowd. He put His fingers into the man's ears, spat, and touched his tongue. Then He looked up to heaven, sighed deeply, and said, [[v34|Ephphatha!]], an Aramaic word that Mark translates, [[v34|Be opened!]] Immediately the man's ears were opened, his tongue was released, and he began to speak plainly. This healing is studied below.

Jesus commanded them not to tell anyone, but the more He commanded them, the more they proclaimed it. People were overwhelmed with amazement and said, [[v37|He has done all things well. He makes even the deaf hear and the mute speak!]] This exclamation is studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Mark 7:8 — Setting Aside God's Command for Human Tradition

verse: Mark 7:8

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words to Pharisees and scribes who had traveled from Jerusalem, likely to investigate His ministry. They criticized His disciples for eating without performing the ritual handwashing required by the tradition of the elders. To them, this showed disrespect for the religious customs that had been handed down for generations.

Jesus first quoted Isaiah, who condemned people who honored God with their lips while their hearts were far from Him, and who taught human rules as if they were God's commands. Then He made His charge directly: [[v8|For you set aside the commandment of God, and hold tightly to the tradition of men]].

The words "set aside" mean to leave behind or abandon. "Hold tightly" means to grasp firmly. The Pharisees had their priorities reversed. They gripped human traditions tightly while letting go of God's actual commands. Jesus mentions [[v8|the washing of pitchers and cups]] as examples of the traditions they emphasized, and He adds that they did many similar things.

In the next verses, Jesus gave a specific example: the Corban practice, which allowed a person to declare his resources devoted to God and thereby avoid supporting his parents. This tradition directly violated the command to honor father and mother. Jesus said that by such practices they were [[v13|making void the word of God by your tradition]].

The Pharisees likely did not think they were rejecting God's commands. They believed their traditions protected and honored the law. But over time, the traditions had grown so important that they overshadowed and even contradicted the very law they were meant to serve.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that God's Word must have authority over all human traditions. Traditions are not bad in themselves. Paul tells the Thessalonians to hold to the traditions they had been taught by the apostles (2 Thessalonians 2:15). Traditions can preserve wisdom, provide structure for worship, and help people live faithfully. But they must always be tested by Scripture. When traditions contradict or replace God's commands, they become a form of disobedience.

The verse also shows how religion can drift from its purpose. The Pharisees' traditions began with sincere intentions, but they gradually became ends in themselves. Their focus on external purity led them to neglect inner purity and practical love, such as caring for parents. This danger exists in every religious community. Practices that once expressed faith can become substitutes for it.

Finally, Jesus' example of the Corban vow shows that God cares deeply about how people treat their families. Religious devotion that neglects practical love for one's family is not true devotion to God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers and churches to examine their traditions. Every church and family has customs about worship, dress, schedules, music, and practices. These can be good, but believers can ask whether any of them have become more important than God's clear commands, such as loving one another, caring for the poor, showing mercy, and honoring parents.

It also warns against using religion to avoid responsibility. Some people use church activities or religious commitments as reasons to neglect family, ignore the needy, or avoid difficult obligations. Jesus' teaching shows that God's commands to love and care for others cannot be cancelled by religious activity.

Finally, believers can let Scripture be the standard for their beliefs and practices. When disagreements arise in churches about traditions, believers can return to what God's Word actually says, distinguishing between what God commands and what people have added. This allows for freedom in secondary matters and faithfulness in what matters most.

### Mark 7:34 — "Ephphatha! Be Opened!"

verse: Mark 7:34

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes the healing of a deaf man in the Decapolis, a largely Gentile region east of the Sea of Galilee. Some people brought to Jesus a man who was deaf and had difficulty speaking. They asked Jesus to lay His hand on him. This healing appears only in Mark's Gospel.

Jesus' actions were unusually personal. First, He [[v33|took him aside from the multitude privately]]. Rather than making the man a public spectacle, Jesus gave him private attention. For a deaf man who could not hear what people were saying, this personal approach would have been reassuring.

Then Jesus communicated in ways the man could understand without hearing. He put His fingers into the man's ears, showing that He was going to do something about his hearing. He spat and touched the man's tongue, showing that He would heal his speech. (In the ancient world, saliva was sometimes thought to have healing properties, but here it served as a sign the man could feel.)

Then Jesus, [[v34|Looking up to heaven]], showed that the power came from God. He [[v34|sighed]], a deep groan that may express compassion for the man's suffering, sorrow over a world broken by sin and disease, or earnest prayer. Then He said, [[v34|Ephphatha!]], an Aramaic word meaning [[v34|Be opened!]] Mark preserves the actual word Jesus spoke, likely as Peter remembered it, and translates it for his readers.

Immediately the man's ears were opened, his tongue was loosened, and he began to speak clearly. Jesus' word had power over the man's body.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the tenderness of Jesus' compassion. He did not simply heal from a distance or treat the man as a project. He took him aside, communicated in ways he could understand, and touched him personally. Jesus' sigh reveals that He felt the weight of human suffering. Hebrews 4:15 says that Jesus is able to sympathize with our weaknesses.

The healing also fulfills prophecy. Isaiah 35:5–6 promises that when God comes to save His people, [[Isaiah 35:5|Then the eyes of the blind will be opened, and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped]] and the tongue of the mute will shout for joy. Jesus' healing shows that God's promised salvation was arriving, and that it was reaching Gentiles as well as Jews.

Finally, the word "Ephphatha" has spiritual significance. Throughout Mark, the disciples struggle to hear and understand Jesus (Mark 8:18 asks, "Having ears, don't you hear?"). The healing of the deaf man becomes a picture of what Jesus wants to do for everyone: open their ears to hear His word and loosen their tongues to speak His praise. In some early church baptismal services, the word "Ephphatha" was spoken over those being baptized as a prayer that their ears would be opened to God.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to pray for spiritual ears to hear. Many people hear about God without truly understanding. Believers can ask Jesus to open their own ears to His word and to open the ears of friends and family who do not yet understand the gospel.

It also offers a model of compassionate care. Jesus gave the deaf man personal attention, communicated in ways he could understand, and treated him with dignity. Believers can follow this example, especially with people who have disabilities or who feel overlooked. Taking time to understand someone's needs and communicating with care reflects Christ's love.

Finally, Jesus' sigh shows that it is right to feel sorrow over suffering. Believers can bring their grief over a broken world to God in prayer, as Jesus did, looking up to heaven and trusting God's power to restore.

### Mark 7:37 — "He Has Done All Things Well"

verse: Mark 7:37

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records the reaction of the crowd after Jesus healed the deaf man in the Decapolis. Jesus had taken the man aside, touched his ears and tongue, sighed, and said, "Ephphatha!" Immediately the man could hear and speak clearly. Jesus then commanded the people not to tell anyone, but they spread the news even more widely.

Mark describes their reaction: [[v37|They were astonished beyond measure]]. Mark uses a strong expression, literally "exceedingly overwhelmed." Then they said, [[v37|He has done all things well. He makes even the deaf hear and the mute speak!]]

The phrase "He has done all things well" echoes Genesis 1:31, where God looked at everything He had made and saw that it was [[Genesis 1:31|very good]]. Jesus' work was restoring creation to its intended goodness, reversing the effects of sin and suffering.

The second part of their statement, "He makes even the deaf hear and the mute speak," echoes Isaiah 35:5–6, the prophecy of what God would do when He came to save His people. These people, many of them Gentiles in the Decapolis, may not have recognized the full significance of their words, but they were declaring that the signs of God's coming salvation were present in Jesus.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse expresses a truth about Jesus' character and work: everything He does is good. His teaching, His healing, His welcoming of outcasts, and ultimately His death and resurrection all reflect the goodness of God. Jesus came not only to forgive sins but to restore creation. His healings were signs of the new creation that will be completed when He returns, when [[Revelation 21:5|Behold, I am making all things new]].

The verse also shows that Jesus' fame spread despite His attempts to keep His miracles quiet. He did not seek publicity, but the goodness of His work could not be hidden. This tension runs throughout Mark's Gospel. Jesus wanted people to understand Him rightly, not just to be impressed by miracles. Yet His compassion produced effects that people could not stop talking about.

Finally, it is significant that these words of praise came from a largely Gentile region. The people of Jesus' hometown had rejected Him (Mark 6:1–6), and the religious leaders from Jerusalem had criticized Him (7:1–13). But in Gentile territory, people declared that He had done all things well. God's mercy was reaching beyond Israel's borders.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to trust that Jesus does all things well, even when they do not fully understand His ways. In times of difficulty, it can be hard to see God's goodness. Remembering the testimony of Scripture and the ways Jesus has worked in the past can strengthen confidence that He is good and that He will complete His good work.

It also encourages believers to praise Jesus openly for what He has done. The crowd could not keep quiet about His goodness. Believers can share with others how Jesus has worked in their lives, giving Him credit and pointing others to Him.

Finally, this verse reminds believers that Jesus' work includes restoration of the whole person and the whole creation. Followers of Jesus can participate in this restoring work by caring for the sick, supporting people with disabilities, and showing compassion in practical ways, as signs of the goodness of His kingdom.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Mark 7 challenges believers to examine the relationship between outward religion and inward reality. The Pharisees were careful about ritual washing but had allowed their traditions to override God's command to care for their parents. Believers can ask whether their own religious habits or church traditions have become more important than God's clear commands to love, show mercy, and care for family. Traditions should serve faith, not replace it.

Jesus' teaching that defilement comes from the heart calls for honest self-examination. The list of evils in verses 21–22 includes not only obvious sins but also greed, deceit, envy, slander, pride, and foolishness. Believers cannot become clean by external effort alone. They need God to cleanse and renew their hearts, which He does through Christ and the work of the Holy Spirit.

The Syrophoenician woman and the healing of the deaf man show that God's mercy reaches beyond the boundaries people draw. Jesus traveled into Gentile territory and welcomed those whom many considered unclean. Believers can follow His example by welcoming people from different backgrounds and cultures, recognizing that the gospel is for everyone.

Finally, the healing of the deaf man reveals Jesus' tenderness and His power to open ears and loosen tongues. Believers can pray for spiritual understanding for themselves and others, treat people with personal compassion and dignity, and join the crowd in declaring that Jesus has done all things well.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, forgive me for the times I have held on to human traditions while setting aside what You have clearly commanded. Help me to test my habits and customs by Your Word, and to honor my family and care for others as You desire. Search my heart and cleanse it from the evil thoughts, greed, deceit, envy, pride, and foolishness that come from within.

Thank You that Your mercy reaches people of every nation, including those whom others consider outsiders. Give me the humble, persistent faith of the Syrophoenician woman. Say "Ephphatha" over my heart, Lord, and open my ears to hear Your word and my mouth to speak Your praise. Thank You that You have done all things well. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
