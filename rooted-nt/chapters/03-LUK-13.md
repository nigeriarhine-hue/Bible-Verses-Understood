# Luke 13
theme: Time to Repent, Freedom for the Bound, and the Narrow Door
main: When people ask about victims of violence and disaster, Jesus refuses to treat tragedy as proof of greater guilt and instead calls everyone to repent, illustrating God's patience with the parable of a fruitless fig tree given one more year. He frees a woman bent over for eighteen years and defends healing her on the Sabbath. He describes God's kingdom as growing from small beginnings, urges people to strive to enter through the narrow door while it is open, and laments over Jerusalem.

## Chapter Background and Story

Luke 13 continues Jesus' journey toward Jerusalem, and its themes follow naturally from the end of Luke 12, where Jesus urged the crowds to read the signs of the times and to settle accounts before reaching the judge. The chapter returns again and again to urgency: the time to repent is now, God's patience is real but not endless, and the door to the kingdom will not stay open forever. At the same time, the healing of the bent woman shows the compassion that lies behind every warning.

### Tragedy and Repentance (13:1–5)

Some people told Jesus about Galileans [[v1|whose blood Pilate had mixed with their sacrifices]]. No other ancient source records this event, but it fits what is known of Pilate, who sometimes used violent force against crowds. Apparently Roman soldiers had killed some Galilean pilgrims while they were offering sacrifices at the temple, so that their blood mingled with the blood of their animals.

Many people in that time believed that disaster was always a direct punishment for a particular sin. Job's friends assumed this about Job; the disciples assumed it about a man born blind (John 9:2). Jesus addressed this assumption directly, and then mentioned a second tragedy: eighteen people killed when a tower in Siloam, in the southern part of Jerusalem near the pool of Siloam, collapsed on them. One tragedy was caused by human cruelty, the other by an accident. In both cases, Jesus said the victims were not worse sinners than others. This teaching is studied below.

### The Parable of the Barren Fig Tree (13:6–9)

Jesus then told a parable found only in Luke. A man had a fig tree planted in his vineyard. For three years he came looking for fruit and found none. Fig trees normally began bearing within a few years of planting, so three years without fruit suggested the tree was not going to produce. He told the gardener to cut it down: [[v7|Why does it waste the soil?]] A fruitless tree took up space and drew nutrients from ground that could support something productive.

But the gardener asked for one more year to dig around it and fertilize it. If it bore fruit, good; if not, it could be cut down. This parable is studied below. It follows naturally from Jesus' call to repent: God is patient, giving time and care to bring about fruit, but the time of patience will end.

### Healing a Woman Bent Over (13:10–17)

On a Sabbath, Jesus was teaching in a synagogue. This is the last time Luke records Jesus teaching in a synagogue. A woman was there who had been crippled by [[v11|a spirit of infirmity eighteen years]]. She was bent over and could not straighten up at all. Luke the physician describes her condition carefully; it may have been a severe spinal disorder, and Luke connects it with a spiritual cause.

When Jesus saw her, He called her forward and said, [[v12|Woman, you are freed from your infirmity]]. He laid His hands on her, and immediately she straightened up and began praising God.

The synagogue leader was indignant because Jesus had healed on the Sabbath. Notice that he did not speak directly to Jesus. He spoke to the crowd: [[v14|There are six days in which men ought to work. Therefore come on those days and be healed, and not on the Sabbath day!]] He treated healing as work that violated the Sabbath (Exodus 20:9–10).

Jesus answered, [[v15|You hypocrites!]] He pointed out that each of them would untie his ox or donkey on the Sabbath and lead it to water. Jewish teachers allowed this kind of care for animals. Then He drew the conclusion that is studied below. His opponents were humiliated, and the whole crowd rejoiced at all the wonderful things He was doing.

### The Mustard Seed and the Yeast (13:18–21)

Jesus asked what God's kingdom was like. It is like a mustard seed planted in a garden that grows into a tree large enough for birds to nest in its branches. It is also like yeast that a woman mixed into about sixty pounds of flour until all of it was leavened. Placed right after the healing of one bent woman in an ordinary synagogue, these parables suggest that the kingdom's work, though it may look small, is growing and will transform everything. (See Matthew 13 and Mark 4:30–32.)

### The Narrow Door (13:22–30)

Luke reminds readers that Jesus was traveling through towns and villages, teaching, as He made His way to Jerusalem. Someone asked, [[v23|Lord, are they few who are saved?]] This was a debated question among Jewish teachers. Jesus did not give a number. Instead, He turned the question into a personal challenge, studied below.

He warned that once the owner of the house gets up and shuts the door, people will stand outside knocking and saying, "Lord, open to us!" He will answer, [[v25|I don’t know you or where you come from]]. They will protest, [[v26|We ate and drank in your presence, and you taught in our streets]]. Casual acquaintance with Jesus, having been around Him or heard Him teach, is not the same as knowing Him. He will tell them to depart.

There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth when they see Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and all the prophets in God's kingdom while they themselves are thrown out. Meanwhile, people will come [[v29|from the east, west, north, and south]], Gentiles from every direction, and sit down at the feast in God's kingdom. [[v30|Behold, there are some who are last who will be first, and there are some who are first who will be last]].

### Herod and the Lament Over Jerusalem (13:31–35)

At that time, some Pharisees came and told Jesus to leave, because Herod Antipas wanted to kill Him. Not all Pharisees were hostile to Jesus; these may have been genuinely warning Him, or they may have wanted Him out of their area. Herod had already executed John the Baptist.

Jesus replied, [[v32|Go and tell that fox]]. A fox was seen as a sly, destructive animal, and perhaps also a small, insignificant one compared to a lion. Jesus was not intimidated. He would continue driving out demons and healing people today and tomorrow, and on the third day He would reach His goal. He must keep going, [[v33|for it can’t be that a prophet would perish outside of Jerusalem]]. Jesus spoke with sorrowful irony: Jerusalem was the city where prophets were killed. His death would happen there, on God's timetable, not Herod's.

Then Jesus lamented over Jerusalem, longing to gather its children as a hen gathers her chicks under her wings, though they were unwilling. Their house would be left desolate, and they would not see Him until they said, [[v35|Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord!]] (This lament is studied at Matthew 23:37.) In Luke, these words come before Jesus' entry into Jerusalem, where the crowds will indeed shout these words of Psalm 118 (Luke 19:38).

## Important Verses Explained

### Luke 13:2–3 — Tragedy and the Call to Repent

verse: Luke 13:2-3

#### Simple Explanation

These verses are Jesus' response to people who told Him about a tragic event. Some Galilean worshipers had been killed by Pilate's soldiers while offering sacrifices, so that their blood mixed with the blood of their animals. The people who reported this may have expected Jesus to condemn Pilate, or they may have been wondering what these Galileans had done to deserve such a fate.

Jesus addressed the assumption behind their report: [[v2|Do you think that these Galileans were worse sinners than all the other Galileans, because they suffered such things?]] Many people in that time believed that terrible suffering was proof of terrible sin. If something awful happened to someone, they must have deserved it.

Jesus answered His own question firmly: [[v3|I tell you, no]]. The victims were not worse sinners than other people. Their suffering was not a sign of special guilt. Jesus then mentioned another tragedy, the collapse of the tower in Siloam that killed eighteen people, and gave the same answer: they were not worse offenders than everyone else in Jerusalem.

But Jesus did not stop there. He turned the question back to His listeners: [[v3|but unless you repent, you will all perish in the same way]]. Instead of speculating about why others suffered, His listeners needed to examine their own hearts. Everyone is a sinner who needs to repent. The tragedies were reminders that life is uncertain and death can come suddenly. The proper response was not to judge the victims but to turn to God while there was still time.

The phrase "perish in the same way" may also have had a more specific meaning. If the nation continued on its path of rejecting God and pursuing violent rebellion against Rome, many would die by Roman swords and falling buildings, as happened when Jerusalem was destroyed in A.D. 70.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses correct a common and harmful way of thinking about suffering. Scripture does teach that sin has consequences, and sometimes suffering results from specific wrongdoing. But Jesus clearly rejects the idea that every tragedy is a sign of special guilt. The book of Job makes the same point: Job suffered greatly, yet God declared him righteous. In John 9:3, Jesus said of the man born blind that neither he nor his parents had sinned to cause his blindness.

At the same time, Jesus refuses to let His listeners avoid their own need for repentance. All people are sinners (Romans 3:23), and all need to turn to God. Tragedies remind everyone that life is fragile and that they will one day stand before God.

Finally, these verses show Jesus' compassion and honesty together. He did not blame victims, and He did not give easy explanations for suffering. But He did speak urgently about the need for repentance, because He cared about His listeners' eternal destiny.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses warn believers against blaming people for their suffering. When someone experiences illness, accident, violence, or natural disaster, believers should not assume that they are being punished for some hidden sin. Such assumptions can deeply wound people who are already hurting. Instead, believers can respond with compassion, prayer, and practical help.

They also invite believers to examine their own lives. When tragedies happen, it is natural to ask why. Jesus redirects the question: "What does this mean for me?" Tragedies remind people that life is short and uncertain, and they call everyone to be ready to meet God.

Finally, these verses encourage believers not to delay repentance. Many people intend to make things right with God someday. Jesus' words show that there may not be a "someday." Now is the time to turn to Him.

### Luke 13:8 — One More Year: The Patience of God

verse: Luke 13:8

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the gardener's plea in the parable of the barren fig tree. Jesus told this parable right after calling His listeners to repent. In the story, a man had a fig tree in his vineyard. Fig trees were often planted in vineyards, where the soil was good. For three years the owner came looking for figs and found none. He told the gardener, [[v7|Cut it down! Why does it waste the soil?]] The tree was taking up good ground and nutrients without producing anything.

The gardener answered, [[v8|Lord, leave it alone this year also, until I dig around it and fertilize it]]. He asked for one more year. During that year, he would give the tree special care: digging around its roots to loosen the soil and let in water and air, and adding manure to nourish it. Then, as the next verse says, [[v9|If it bears fruit, fine; but if not, after that, you can cut it down]].

In the Old Testament, the fig tree and the vineyard were images for Israel (Isaiah 5:1–7; Hosea 9:10). In the context of Jesus' ministry, the parable described Israel, and especially Jerusalem, which had received God's care through the prophets and now through Jesus Himself, yet had not produced the fruit of repentance. Some interpreters connect the "three years" with the approximate length of Jesus' ministry, though the parable does not say this directly.

The gardener's plea shows God's patience and mercy. Judgment was deserved, but more time and more care were given. Yet the parable does not end with a guarantee. It leaves the outcome open: will the tree bear fruit?

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals God's patience with people who have not yet responded to Him. Peter writes that God [[2 Peter 3:9|is patient with us, not wishing that anyone should perish, but that all should come to repentance]]. God's delay in judgment is not weakness or indifference; it is an opportunity for repentance.

The gardener's work also shows that God does not merely wait passively. He digs and fertilizes. He actively works to bring about fruit through His Word, His Spirit, circumstances, and the people He sends. Many believers can look back and see how God patiently worked in their lives before they responded to Him.

At the same time, the parable warns that God's patience has a limit. The tree is given one more year, not unlimited time. Grace should not be taken for granted. The opportunity to repent and bear fruit is real, and it is urgent.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to respond to God's patience with repentance and fruitfulness. If God has been patiently working in their lives, perhaps through difficulties, sermons, conversations, or quiet conviction, they can respond now rather than delay.

It also offers hope to those who feel spiritually unfruitful. God has not given up on them. He continues to work, digging around and nourishing their lives. Believers can cooperate with His work by spending time in His Word, prayer, and fellowship, allowing Him to bring fruit.

Finally, this verse encourages believers to be patient with others, as God is patient. Like the gardener, they can pray for and care for people who have not yet borne fruit, rather than giving up on them quickly.

### Luke 13:16 — A Daughter of Abraham Set Free

verse: Luke 13:16

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the climax of Jesus' response to a synagogue leader who objected to Him healing on the Sabbath. Jesus had been teaching in a synagogue when He saw a woman who had been bent over for eighteen years, unable to straighten up. He called her forward, declared her free from her disability, and laid His hands on her. Immediately she stood up straight and praised God.

The synagogue leader was indignant and told the crowd that there were six days for work, so people should come to be healed on those days and not on the Sabbath. Jesus called His critics hypocrites and pointed out that they untied their oxen and donkeys on the Sabbath to lead them to water. Then He said, [[v16|Ought not this woman, being a daughter of Abraham whom Satan had bound eighteen long years, be freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day?]]

Several phrases are significant. [[v16|this woman, being a daughter of Abraham]]. The phrase "son of Abraham" was common for Jewish men, but "daughter of Abraham" was rare. Jesus honored her as a full member of God's covenant people, worthy of care and dignity. If an ox deserves to be untied on the Sabbath, surely a daughter of Abraham deserves more.

[[v16|whom Satan had bound eighteen long years]]. Jesus revealed that her condition was connected to Satan's oppression. Luke had described her as having "a spirit of infirmity." The word "bound" connects with the image of untying animals.

[[v16|be freed from this bondage on the Sabbath day?]] Jesus argued that the Sabbath was the most fitting day for her release. The Sabbath celebrated God's rest and Israel's freedom from slavery in Egypt (Deuteronomy 5:15). Setting a captive free was exactly in keeping with the Sabbath's purpose.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that Jesus came to set people free from every kind of bondage. In His first sermon at Nazareth, He announced that He was sent [[Luke 4:18|to proclaim release to the captives]] and [[Luke 4:18|to deliver those who are crushed]]. The healing of the bent woman was a living fulfillment of that mission.

It also shows Jesus' compassion for those who suffer long. The woman had been bound for eighteen years. Jesus noticed her, called her, touched her, and restored her. He also restored her dignity, calling her a daughter of Abraham in front of the whole synagogue.

Finally, the verse exposes the danger of valuing religious rules above people. The synagogue leader was more concerned about the Sabbath rules than about the woman's suffering. Jesus showed that God's commands are meant to bring life and freedom, not to keep people bound.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages anyone who has been burdened for a long time, whether by illness, depression, addiction, grief, or spiritual oppression. Jesus sees them and has the power to set them free. They can bring their burdens to Him, trusting His compassion and authority.

It also reminds believers to honor people's dignity. Jesus called the woman a "daughter of Abraham," affirming her value. Believers can treat people who are suffering or marginalized with respect and remind them of their worth before God.

Finally, this verse challenges believers to examine whether their religious practices ever stand in the way of compassion. Churches can make sure that their traditions and schedules serve people rather than burden them, and that they are quick to help those in need.

### Luke 13:24 — Strive to Enter Through the Narrow Door

verse: Luke 13:24

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is Jesus' answer to a question asked as He traveled through towns and villages on His way to Jerusalem. Someone asked, [[v23|Lord, are they few who are saved?]] This was a common topic of discussion among Jewish teachers. Some believed that all Israel would share in the age to come; others believed only a faithful remnant would.

Jesus did not answer the question directly. Instead, He turned it into a personal challenge: [[v24|Strive to enter in by the narrow door, for many, I tell you, will seek to enter in and will not be able]].

[[v24|Strive]] translates a Greek word from which we get "agonize." It was used for athletes competing in contests. Jesus calls for serious, wholehearted effort. Entering the kingdom is not something to approach casually.

[[v24|the narrow door]]. A narrow door allows only one person at a time and requires effort to pass through. In Matthew 7:13–14, Jesus speaks of a narrow gate and a hard road leading to life. The narrow door represents the way of repentance and faith in Jesus Himself. In John 10:9, Jesus says, [[John 10:9|I am the door]].

[[v24|for many, I tell you, will seek to enter in and will not be able]]. In the following verses, Jesus explains why. There will come a time when the owner of the house shuts the door. Then those outside will knock and claim that they ate and drank with Him and heard Him teach in their streets. But He will say He does not know them. Many will try to enter too late, or on their own terms, relying on casual association with Jesus rather than genuine relationship with Him.

Jesus' answer shifted the focus from curiosity about others to urgency about oneself. The important question is not "How many will be saved?" but "Am I entering?"

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse teaches that salvation requires a personal, wholehearted response to Jesus. The word "strive" does not mean that people earn salvation by effort; salvation is God's gift of grace (Ephesians 2:8–9). Rather, it means taking God's invitation seriously, turning from sin, and committing oneself to Jesus with urgency and sincerity.

It also warns against false security. Those outside the door claimed familiarity with Jesus: they had eaten with Him and heard Him teach. But familiarity is not the same as faith. Many people today know about Jesus, attend church occasionally, or grew up in Christian families, but have never personally entered through the narrow door.

Finally, the following verses show that the kingdom will include people from every direction: east, west, north, and south. Many who considered themselves insiders will be outside, while outsiders from the nations will sit at the feast. God's kingdom will surprise people.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites each person to make sure they have entered through the narrow door. Instead of speculating about others' salvation, believers can examine their own relationship with Jesus. Do they truly know Him, trust Him, and follow Him?

It also encourages believers not to delay. The door will not stay open forever. Those who have been putting off a decision to follow Jesus can respond now while the opportunity remains.

Finally, this verse calls believers to humility and hope. They should not assume that religious familiarity guarantees salvation, and they should rejoice that God's kingdom will include people from every nation who come to Him in faith. Believers can share the invitation widely, urging others to enter while the door is open.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Luke 13 brings together urgent warnings and tender compassion. Jesus' response to the tragedies of the slain Galileans and the fallen tower teaches believers not to blame victims for their suffering, while reminding everyone that life is uncertain and repentance cannot wait. Believers can respond to tragedy with compassion for those who suffer and with honest self-examination.

The parable of the barren fig tree reveals God's patience. He gives time and care to bring about fruit. Believers can respond to that patience by repenting and bearing fruit, and they can show similar patience toward others who have not yet responded to God.

The healing of the bent woman shows Jesus' compassion for those who have suffered for a long time and His power to set people free. It also challenges any religious attitude that values rules above people. Believers can honor the dignity of those who suffer and make sure their practices help rather than hinder compassion.

The parables of the mustard seed and yeast encourage believers that God's kingdom grows from small beginnings. The teaching about the narrow door calls each person to enter through Jesus with wholehearted commitment, not relying on familiarity with religion. And the warning about Herod and the lament over Jerusalem show Jesus' courage in following God's plan and His grief over those who refuse to come to Him. Believers can follow Him with courage and share His longing for people to be gathered into His care.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, keep me from judging those who suffer as if their pain proved their guilt. Give me compassion for the hurting, and help me to hear Your call to repentance in my own life. Thank You for Your patience with me, like the gardener who asked for one more year. Work in my life to make me fruitful, and help me to respond to Your care without delay.

Thank You for setting free the woman who was bent over for eighteen years. Set me free from whatever binds me, and help me to treat every person with the dignity You give them. Help me to strive to enter through the narrow door, to truly know You and not merely know about You. Give me courage to follow Your path as You followed the Father's plan, and share with me Your longing to gather people under Your wings. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
