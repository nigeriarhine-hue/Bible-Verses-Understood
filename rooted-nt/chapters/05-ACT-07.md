# Acts 7
theme: Stephen's Witness: God Is Not Confined, His People Resist, and the Martyr Sees Jesus Standing
main: Accused of speaking against the temple and the Law, Stephen answers the council with a sweeping review of Israel's history. He shows that God met His people far from the temple, through Abraham, Joseph, and Moses, and that Israel has repeatedly rejected the deliverers God sent, just as the council has now betrayed and murdered the Righteous One. Enraged, they drag him out to stone him. Full of the Holy Spirit, Stephen sees Jesus standing at God's right hand, commits his spirit to Him, and prays for his killers' forgiveness, while a young man named Saul looks on.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 7 contains the longest speech in the book of Acts. Stephen had been charged with speaking against "this holy place and the law" and claiming that Jesus would destroy the temple and change the customs of Moses (6:13–14). Rather than defending himself point by point, Stephen retells the story of Israel. His speech makes two main points. First, God has never been confined to one place; He appeared to Abraham in Mesopotamia, was with Joseph in Egypt, spoke to Moses in the wilderness at the burning bush, and declared through the prophets that no building can contain Him. Second, Israel has a long history of rejecting the leaders God sent to rescue them: Joseph was sold by his brothers, Moses was pushed away, and the prophets were persecuted. In rejecting Jesus, the council was repeating the pattern.

Stephen is not attacking the Law or the temple as such. He honors Moses as God's deliverer and the Law as "living revelations" (7:38). His point is that the true worship of God involves receiving the One God sends, and that the temple was always meant to point beyond itself.

### Abraham: God Speaks Outside the Land (7:1–8)

The high priest asked, [[v1|Are these things so?]] Stephen began respectfully: [[v2|Brothers and fathers, listen]]. Then he spoke of [[v2|The God of glory]] appearing to Abraham [[v2|when he was in Mesopotamia]], far from the promised land. God called Abraham to leave his land and go to a place He would show him.

God gave Abraham a promise but [[v5|no inheritance in it, no, not so much as to set his foot on]]. Abraham lived by faith in a promise not yet fulfilled. God told him that his descendants would be strangers and slaves in a foreign land for four hundred years, and then God would bring them out to worship Him. God also gave Abraham the covenant of circumcision.

### Joseph: Rejected by His Brothers, but God Was with Him (7:9–16)

Stephen continued with Joseph, studied below. Jacob's family came down to Egypt, [[v14|seventy-five souls]]. (Genesis 46:27 in the Hebrew text says seventy; the Greek translation of Genesis, which Stephen as a Greek-speaking Jew would have used, says seventy-five, counting additional descendants of Joseph.) Jacob and the patriarchs died in Egypt and were buried in Shechem. Stephen's summary combines details of the burial places of Abraham and Jacob (Genesis 23; 33:19; 50:13; Joshua 24:32), a compressed way of telling the story that is common in speeches. Notably, Shechem was in Samaria, outside Judea; even the burial places of the patriarchs were not in Jerusalem.

### Moses: Deliverer Rejected (7:17–43)

Stephen spent the most time on Moses, since he had been accused of speaking against him. He described Moses' birth in a time of oppression, his rescue by Pharaoh's daughter, and his education in all the wisdom of Egypt. At forty, Moses visited his fellow Israelites and defended one who was being mistreated. [[v25|He supposed that his brothers understood that God, by his hand, was giving them deliverance; but they didn’t understand]]. When he tried to make peace between two Israelites, one pushed him away: [[v27|Who made you a ruler and a judge over us?]] Moses fled to Midian.

Forty years later, God appeared to Moses in a burning bush in the wilderness near Mount Sinai. God said, [[v33|Take off your sandals, for the place where you stand is holy ground]]. Stephen's point is clear: holy ground was found in the desert, far from the temple, wherever God chose to reveal Himself.

Then Stephen drove home the parallel: [[v35|This Moses whom they refused, saying, ‘Who made you a ruler and a judge?’—God has sent him as both a ruler and a deliverer]]. The one the people rejected was the one God sent to save them. Moses himself had promised, [[v37|The Lord our God will raise up a prophet for you from among your brothers, like me]]. Stephen's hearers would have understood the implication: Jesus is that Prophet, rejected by His people but sent by God.

Even after the exodus, Israel rejected Moses in their hearts and turned back toward Egypt. They made a golden calf and [[v41|rejoiced in the works of their hands]]. God gave them over to idolatry, as the prophet Amos later described (Amos 5:25–27).

### Tabernacle and Temple (7:44–50)

Stephen then addressed the temple. Israel had the tabernacle in the wilderness, made according to the pattern God showed Moses. It traveled with them into the land under Joshua and remained until the days of David. David wanted to build a dwelling for God, [[v47|But Solomon built him a house]]. Stephen's conclusion about the temple is studied below.

### "You Always Resist the Holy Spirit" (7:51–53)

Stephen then turned directly to his accusers with strong words: [[v51|You stiff-necked and uncircumcised in heart and ears, you always resist the Holy Spirit! As your fathers did, so you do]]. "Stiff-necked" was God's own description of Israel at the golden calf (Exodus 33:5). "Uncircumcised in heart" echoes the prophets' call for inner renewal (Jeremiah 9:26; Deuteronomy 10:16). The council members had the outward sign of the covenant but not its inner reality.

[[v52|Which of the prophets didn’t your fathers persecute? They killed those who foretold the coming of the Righteous One, of whom you have now become betrayers and murderers]]. They had received the Law, delivered through angels, but had not kept it.

These are harsh words, spoken by a Jewish believer to the leaders of his own people, in the tradition of the prophets who called Israel to repentance. They are not a condemnation of the Jewish people as a whole, and they must never be used to justify hatred or contempt for Jews.

### The Stoning of Stephen (7:54–60)

[[v54|Now when they heard these things, they were cut to the heart, and they gnashed at him with their teeth]]. The same phrase "cut to the heart" described the crowd at Pentecost who repented (2:37). Here, conviction led to rage instead. Stephen's vision is studied below.

[[v57|But they cried out with a loud voice and stopped their ears, then rushed at him with one accord]]. They covered their ears, refusing to hear what they considered blasphemy. They dragged him out of the city and stoned him. Luke adds an important detail: [[v58|The witnesses placed their garments at the feet of a young man named Saul]]. This is the first mention of the man who would later become the apostle Paul. Stephen's final words are studied below.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 7:9–10 — God Was with Joseph

verse: Acts 7:9-10

#### Simple Explanation

In his speech to the council, Stephen retold the story of Joseph, one of the twelve sons of Jacob. Joseph was his father's favorite son, and his brothers hated him for it. Stephen said, [[v9|The patriarchs, moved with jealousy against Joseph, sold him into Egypt]]. The founders of Israel's twelve tribes betrayed their own brother out of envy (Genesis 37).

But then come the key words: [[v9|God was with him]]. Joseph was far from home, sold as a slave, falsely accused, and thrown into prison. Yet God had not abandoned him. Genesis repeats this phrase several times: "The LORD was with Joseph" (Genesis 39:2, 21, 23).

[[v10|and delivered him out of all his afflictions, and gave him favor and wisdom before Pharaoh, king of Egypt. He made him governor over Egypt and all his house]]. God rescued Joseph from his troubles, gave him wisdom to interpret Pharaoh's dreams, and raised him to be the second most powerful man in Egypt. Through Joseph, God saved his family, including the brothers who had betrayed him, from famine.

Stephen's point was twofold. First, God was with Joseph in Egypt, far from the promised land and long before there was a temple. God's presence is not limited to one place. Second, Joseph was rejected by his brothers but became their savior. This pattern points to Jesus, who was rejected by His own people but became the Savior of the world.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

The story of Joseph is one of Scripture's clearest pictures of how God works through suffering. Joseph later told his brothers, [[Genesis 50:20|As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to save many people alive]]. The same is true of the cross: what humans intended for evil, God used to bring salvation.

The passage also reveals the presence of God with His people in every circumstance. Joseph was in a pit, in slavery, in prison, and in a palace, and God was with him in each place. Jesus promised His followers, [[Matthew 28:20|I am with you always, even to the end of the age]].

Finally, Joseph's story shows a recurring pattern in Israel's history: the deliverer God sends is often rejected first. Joseph, Moses, the prophets, and finally Jesus were all rejected by those they came to save. Stephen was showing the council that their rejection of Jesus fit this tragic pattern.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses encourage believers who feel abandoned, betrayed, or far from home. Joseph's circumstances were terrible, yet God was with him. Believers going through hardship can trust that God's presence does not depend on their circumstances.

They also give hope that God can bring good out of evil. Believers may suffer because of the jealousy, cruelty, or betrayal of others. This does not make the wrong done to them good, and it does not mean they should stay in harmful situations. But God can work even through painful experiences to accomplish His purposes and bring blessing.

Finally, Joseph's story encourages believers to forgive. Joseph eventually forgave the brothers who sold him. Through Christ's strength, believers can release bitterness toward those who have wronged them, while trusting God to bring justice and redemption.

### Acts 7:48–50 — The Most High Doesn't Dwell in Temples Made with Hands

verse: Acts 7:48-50

#### Simple Explanation

Stephen had been accused of speaking against the temple. In his speech, he traced the history of God's dwelling place among His people. Israel had the tabernacle in the wilderness, a portable tent built according to God's pattern. It traveled with them for centuries. King David wanted to build a permanent house for God, and his son Solomon built the temple in Jerusalem.

Then Stephen said, [[v48|However, the Most High doesn’t dwell in temples made with hands]]. The temple was a gift from God and a place where He made His presence known. But God cannot be confined to any building. He is the "Most High," above all creation.

Stephen quoted Isaiah 66:1–2: [[v49|heaven is my throne, and the earth a footstool for my feet. What kind of house will you build me?’ says the Lord. ‘Or what is the place of my rest?]] [[v50|Didn’t my hand make all these things?]] God made the whole universe. How could any human building contain Him?

Even Solomon understood this. When he dedicated the temple, he prayed, [[1 Kings 8:27|But will God in very deed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the heaven of heavens can’t contain you; how much less this house that I have built!]]

Stephen was not saying the temple was evil or worthless. He was saying that the religious leaders had made the temple into something it was never meant to be, treating it as though God's presence were limited to it and as though defending the building were more important than receiving God's Messiah.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This passage prepares the way for one of the great themes of the New Testament: God's presence is no longer centered on a building but on a Person and a people. Jesus said, [[John 2:19|Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up]], speaking of His body. He told the Samaritan woman that true worshipers would worship the Father in spirit and truth, not only in Jerusalem (John 4:21–24).

After Pentecost, God's presence dwells in His people by the Holy Spirit. Paul writes, [[1 Corinthians 3:16|Don’t you know that you are God’s temple and that God’s Spirit lives in you?]] The church, made of people from every nation, is God's temple.

Finally, the passage warns against confusing the symbol with the reality. Buildings, rituals, and traditions can be good gifts that point to God. But when they become more important than God Himself, or than obedience to Him, they become idols.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses remind believers that God can be worshiped anywhere. Church buildings are valuable places for gathering, but God is present wherever His people call on Him: in homes, workplaces, hospitals, prisons, and fields. Believers can live with a sense of His presence in every place.

They also caution believers against placing too much importance on buildings and traditions. Churches can become so focused on preserving their buildings, programs, or customs that they lose sight of their mission. God's presence is with His people, not confined to their structures.

Finally, these verses invite believers to see themselves as God's dwelling place. If the Spirit lives in believers, then their bodies and their communities are holy. This calls them to honor God with how they live and how they treat one another.

### Acts 7:55–56 — Jesus Standing at the Right Hand of God

verse: Acts 7:55-56

#### Simple Explanation

Stephen had finished his speech by accusing the council of resisting the Holy Spirit and murdering the Righteous One. The council members were furious. They gnashed their teeth at him in rage. In that moment of hatred and danger, Stephen's eyes were lifted to heaven.

[[v55|But he, being full of the Holy Spirit, looked up steadfastly into heaven and saw the glory of God, and Jesus standing on the right hand of God]]. Stephen was given a vision of heaven. He saw God's glory and Jesus at the place of highest honor and authority.

He told the council what he saw: [[v56|Behold, I see the heavens opened and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God!]] The title "Son of Man" comes from Daniel 7:13–14, where one like a son of man comes before God and receives everlasting dominion. Jesus had used this title for Himself, and at His own trial before this very council, He had said, [[Luke 22:69|From now on, the Son of Man will be seated at the right hand of the power of God]]. Now Stephen declared that he could see it.

Notice that Stephen saw Jesus "standing," not sitting. Elsewhere, Jesus is described as seated at God's right hand (Psalm 110:1; Hebrews 1:3). Why was He standing? Interpreters have suggested several reasons. Jesus may have been standing to welcome His faithful witness into heaven. He may have been standing as an advocate or witness in Stephen's defense, as Stephen stood before an earthly court. Or He may have been standing as a judge ready to act. Many find the first two explanations especially moving: the risen Lord rose to receive His servant.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This vision shows that Jesus is alive, exalted, and actively concerned for His people. Stephen was condemned by an earthly court, but heaven's verdict was different. The Son of Man stood in his defense. Jesus had promised, [[Luke 12:8|everyone who confesses me before men, the Son of Man will also confess before the angels of God]].

It also shows that the Holy Spirit strengthens believers in their darkest moments. Stephen was "full of the Holy Spirit." The Spirit opened his eyes to see beyond his circumstances to the glory of God and the reigning Christ.

Finally, Stephen's vision confirms the claim that so enraged the council. Jesus, whom they had condemned, was exactly where He said He would be: at the right hand of God. The council could not bear to hear it, and they covered their ears.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This passage brings comfort to believers who suffer for their faith. When the world condemns them, Jesus stands with them. They are not alone. The Lord they serve sees their faithfulness and is ready to welcome them.

It also encourages believers to look up in difficult times. Stephen fixed his eyes on heaven. Believers facing trials can lift their eyes to Jesus, remembering that He reigns and that their future is secure with Him. Hebrews urges believers to run with endurance, [[Hebrews 12:2|looking to Jesus, the author and perfecter of faith]].

Finally, this passage gives believers hope in the face of death. For Stephen, death was the moment of seeing Jesus and being welcomed by Him. Believers can face their own deaths with the same hope, knowing that Jesus is waiting for them.

### Acts 7:59–60 — "Lord, Don't Hold This Sin Against Them"

verse: Acts 7:59-60

#### Simple Explanation

The council members rushed at Stephen, dragged him out of the city, and began to stone him. Stoning was the penalty for blasphemy under the Law (Leviticus 24:16), though the Romans did not normally permit the Jewish council to carry out executions. This appears to have been a violent mob action as much as a legal sentence.

As the stones struck him, Stephen prayed: [[v59|Lord Jesus, receive my spirit!]] These words echo Jesus' prayer from the cross: "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (studied at Luke 23:46). Jesus addressed the Father; Stephen addressed Jesus. Stephen entrusted his spirit to Jesus, showing that he regarded Jesus as Lord over life and death.

Then [[v60|He kneeled down and cried with a loud voice, “Lord, don’t hold this sin against them!”]] Even as he was being killed, Stephen prayed for those who were killing him. This echoes Jesus' prayer from the cross: "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing" (studied at Luke 23:34). Stephen had learned from his Master not only how to live but how to die.

[[v60|When he had said this, he fell asleep]]. Luke describes Stephen's death as falling asleep. This was a common way for early Christians to describe the death of believers, because they knew death was not the end. Those who "sleep" in Christ will awaken at the resurrection (1 Thessalonians 4:13–16).

Stephen became the first Christian martyr. The word "martyr" comes from the Greek word for "witness." Stephen bore witness to Jesus with his life and his death.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

Stephen's death reflects the death of Jesus. Both were falsely accused, both were condemned by the same council, both committed their spirits to God, and both prayed for their executioners. Stephen's life shows what it means to follow Christ fully, even to death.

His prayer for forgiveness reveals the heart of the gospel. Jesus taught His followers, [[Matthew 5:44|love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you]]. Stephen lived out this command in the most extreme circumstances.

God answered Stephen's prayer in a remarkable way. Standing nearby, approving of his death, was Saul of Tarsus (8:1). Within a short time, Saul would meet the risen Jesus and become the apostle Paul. Many have seen Saul's conversion as, in part, an answer to Stephen's prayer. Augustine famously said that if Stephen had not prayed, the church would not have had Paul.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses challenge believers to forgive those who wrong them. Stephen forgave the people who were killing him. Most believers will never face such extreme circumstances, but they do face hurt, injustice, and betrayal. With Christ's help, they can pray for those who wrong them and release bitterness. Forgiveness does not mean pretending wrong was not done, excusing abuse, or remaining in danger; it means entrusting justice to God and refusing to hold on to hatred.

They also give believers peace about death. Stephen entrusted his spirit to Jesus and "fell asleep." For believers, death is not the end but a passage into the presence of Christ. Believers can face death with trust, committing themselves to the Lord.

Finally, Stephen's prayer reminds believers that God can transform even the fiercest enemies of the gospel. Saul was present at Stephen's death and approved of it, yet God turned him into the greatest missionary of the early church. Believers can pray for those who oppose the faith, trusting that God can change their hearts.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 7 reminds believers that God is not confined to any place. He met Abraham in Mesopotamia, was with Joseph in Egypt, spoke to Moses in the wilderness, and now dwells with His people by His Spirit. Believers can trust that God is with them wherever they are, and they can worship Him in every place, not only in church buildings.

The chapter also warns against resisting God. Stephen showed that Israel had a long history of rejecting the deliverers God sent, from Joseph to Moses to the prophets to Jesus. Believers today can also be tempted to resist God's Spirit, holding on to traditions, preferences, or pride rather than receiving what God is doing. Stephen's speech calls for humble, open hearts.

Acts 7 shows the courage of faithful witness. Stephen spoke the truth even when it cost him his life. Believers today may face pressure to remain silent about Christ. Stephen's example encourages them to speak with conviction, grounded in Scripture and filled with the Spirit.

Finally, the chapter shows how a believer can face death. Stephen saw Jesus standing at God's right hand, entrusted his spirit to Him, and prayed for his killers. Believers can face suffering and death with the same hope and forgiveness, knowing that Jesus stands with them and that God can bring life even from the death of His servants.

## Chapter Prayer

Most High God, heaven is Your throne and the earth is Your footstool. No building can contain You, yet You choose to dwell with Your people. Thank You for being with Joseph in his suffering, with Moses in the wilderness, and with me wherever I go. Forgive me for the times I have resisted Your Holy Spirit and held on to my own ways. Give me an open and humble heart. Lord Jesus, thank You that You reign at the right hand of God and that You stand with Your people when the world condemns them. When I face opposition, lift my eyes to see You. Teach me to forgive those who hurt me, as Stephen forgave those who killed him. When my time comes to die, help me to say, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit." In Jesus’ name, Amen.
