# Acts 25
theme: "I Appeal to Caesar": God's Plan Moves Through Courts and Kings
main: The new governor Festus arrives, and the Jewish leaders immediately ask him to bring Paul to Jerusalem, planning to ambush him. Festus holds a hearing in Caesarea instead, where Paul insists he has done nothing against the Law, the temple, or Caesar. When Festus, seeking to please the leaders, suggests a trial in Jerusalem, Paul exercises his right as a Roman citizen and appeals to Caesar. Festus later explains the case to King Agrippa II, puzzled that the dispute concerns "one Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul affirmed to be alive," and Paul is brought before a glittering assembly of rulers.

## Chapter Background and Story

Acts 25 begins a new phase of Paul's imprisonment. After two years in custody under Felix, Paul's case is taken up by a new governor, Porcius Festus, who ruled Judea from about A.D. 59 to 62. Festus seems to have been a more honest administrator than Felix, though he too was concerned with keeping peace with the Jewish leaders. He died in office after only a few years.

The chapter also introduces King Herod Agrippa II, the son of Herod Agrippa I (who killed James in Acts 12), and his sister Bernice. Agrippa II ruled territories to the north and east of Judea and had authority over the temple, including the right to appoint the high priest. He was considered an expert in Jewish customs. Bernice lived with her brother, and their relationship was the subject of gossip in Rome. She later had a relationship with the Roman general Titus, who destroyed Jerusalem in A.D. 70.

Through these chapters, Luke shows the fulfillment of Jesus' words that Paul would bear His name before kings (9:15), and Jesus' promise that Paul would testify in Rome (23:11).

### Festus Visits Jerusalem (25:1–5)

Three days after arriving in the province, Festus went to Jerusalem. The high priest and the leading Jews immediately brought their charges against Paul. Even after two years, their hatred had not cooled. They asked Festus [[v3|asking a favor against him, that he would summon him to Jerusalem, plotting to kill him on the way]]. This was the same kind of ambush plot that had been planned two years earlier (23:12–15).

Festus refused. He said Paul would remain in Caesarea, and that the leaders could come there and present their accusations. Without knowing it, Festus protected Paul's life.

### The Hearing before Festus (25:6–12)

After about ten days in Jerusalem, Festus returned to Caesarea and the next day convened the court. The Jewish leaders surrounded Paul, [[v7|bringing against him many and grievous charges which they could not prove]]. Paul's defense is studied below.

[[v9|But Festus, desiring to gain favor with the Jews, answered Paul and said, “Are you willing to go up to Jerusalem and be judged by me there concerning these things?”]] Festus wanted to please the Jewish leaders at the beginning of his rule. He may not have known about the plot, but Paul understood the danger. A trial in Jerusalem would be an opportunity for his enemies. Paul's response, his appeal to Caesar, is studied below.

Festus conferred with his council and then declared, [[v12|You have appealed to Caesar. To Caesar you shall go]]. The emperor at this time was Nero, who had ruled since A.D. 54. In the early years of his reign, Nero's government was considered fair and stable. His persecution of Christians came later, after the great fire of Rome in A.D. 64.

### Festus Consults King Agrippa (25:13–22)

Some days later, King Agrippa and Bernice arrived in Caesarea to welcome the new governor. Festus explained Paul's case to Agrippa. He described how the Jewish leaders had asked for Paul's condemnation, and how he had told them that Roman law required the accused to face his accusers and defend himself. This principle of fair trial is one of the great contributions of Roman law: [[v16|it is not the custom of the Romans to give up any man to destruction before the accused has met the accusers face to face and has had opportunity to make his defense]]. Festus's puzzled summary of the case is studied below.

Agrippa said, [[v22|I also would like to hear the man myself]]. Like his great-uncle Herod Antipas, who had wanted to see Jesus (Luke 23:8), Agrippa was curious about Paul. Festus arranged a hearing for the next day.

### Paul before Agrippa and Bernice (25:23–27)

The scene of Paul's appearance before this assembly is studied below. Festus introduced the case, explaining that he had found nothing deserving of death, but that he needed something to write to the emperor: [[v27|For it seems to me unreasonable, in sending a prisoner, not to also specify the charges against him]]. The governor was in an awkward position: he was sending a prisoner to Caesar without a clear charge. He hoped Agrippa, with his knowledge of Jewish matters, could help him.

## Important Verses Explained

### Acts 25:8 — Neither Against the Law, the Temple, nor Caesar

verse: Acts 25:8

#### Simple Explanation

Paul stood before the new governor, Festus, surrounded by the Jewish leaders who had come from Jerusalem. They brought many serious charges against him, but they could not prove any of them. Then Paul made his defense.

[[v8|while he said in his defense, “Neither against the law of the Jews, nor against the temple, nor against Caesar, have I sinned at all.”]] Paul's defense covered three areas.

"Against the law of the Jews": Paul had been accused of teaching people to abandon the Law of Moses. But Paul had not done so. He taught that Gentiles did not need to keep the Law to be saved, but he respected the Law and did not forbid Jews from keeping it. He had even participated in a purification rite in the temple (21:26).

"Against the temple": Paul had been accused of bringing a Gentile into the temple and defiling it. This was false. He had been in the temple as a ritually purified worshiper.

"Against Caesar": This was the most dangerous charge from Rome's perspective. Paul's accusers may have suggested that he was a troublemaker who stirred up riots, or that by proclaiming Jesus as King, he was disloyal to Caesar. But Paul had not plotted rebellion or broken Roman law.

Paul's defense was simple and direct: he was innocent in every area. He had nothing to hide.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the integrity of Paul's life. He could stand before religious and political authorities and declare that he had not wronged anyone. Peter later wrote that believers should live so that, though they are accused of evil, [[1 Peter 2:12|they may see your good works and glorify God in the day of visitation]].

It also shows that Christianity is not inherently a threat to legitimate authority. Paul honored the Law, respected the temple, and did not rebel against Caesar. Christians are called to be good citizens, obeying the law and honoring rulers, as long as doing so does not require disobeying God (Romans 13:1–7; Acts 5:29).

Finally, the verse shows that faithfulness to Christ may still bring accusation. Paul was innocent, yet he was accused repeatedly. Jesus warned that His followers would be falsely accused (Matthew 5:11). Innocence does not always prevent opposition.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers to live lives of integrity in every area: religious, social, and civic. Believers can seek to live so that, if accused, they can honestly say they have done no wrong.

It also encourages believers to be good citizens. Following Christ includes respecting laws, paying taxes, and contributing to the common good. Believers can show that faith in Jesus makes them better neighbors, not worse.

Finally, the verse comforts believers who are falsely accused. Paul faced repeated unjust charges, yet he responded calmly and truthfully. Believers can trust that God sees the truth and that their integrity will ultimately be vindicated.

### Acts 25:11 — "I Appeal to Caesar!"

verse: Acts 25:11

#### Simple Explanation

Festus asked Paul whether he would be willing to go to Jerusalem and be tried there. Festus wanted to please the Jewish leaders, but Paul knew the danger. Two years earlier, more than forty men had sworn to kill him (23:12). If he went to Jerusalem, he might not survive the journey, and even if he did, he would not receive a fair trial.

Paul answered firmly: [[v10|I am standing before Caesar’s judgment seat, where I ought to be tried. I have done no wrong to the Jews, as you also know very well]]. Paul reminded Festus that he was a Roman citizen standing in a Roman court. He had done nothing wrong, and Festus knew it.

[[v11|For if I have done wrong and have committed anything worthy of death, I don’t refuse to die]]. Paul was not trying to escape justice. If he were guilty of a capital crime, he would accept the penalty. He was willing to die if he deserved it.

[[v11|but if none of those things is true that they accuse me of, no one can give me up to them]]. But since he was innocent, no one had the right to hand him over to his enemies as a political favor.

[[v11|I appeal to Caesar!]] Roman citizens had the right to appeal their case to the emperor, the highest court in the empire. Once a citizen appealed, the provincial governor had to send the case to Rome. This appeal removed Paul from the jurisdiction of the local authorities and guaranteed that he would go to Rome.

Paul's appeal was not only a legal maneuver. Jesus had promised that Paul would testify in Rome (23:11). The appeal was the means by which this promise would be fulfilled.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that God works through legal systems and human institutions. Paul's Roman citizenship and the right of appeal became God's means of bringing him to Rome. God is sovereign over all things, including governments and laws. Proverbs says, [[Proverbs 21:1|The king’s heart is in the LORD’s hand like the watercourses. He turns it wherever he desires]].

It also shows that seeking justice is not contrary to faith. Paul was willing to suffer for Christ, but he did not accept injustice passively. He used the legal protections available to him. Believers may use legitimate means to protect themselves and others from injustice.

Finally, the verse shows Paul's willingness to accept just consequences. He did not claim exemption from the law. He said that if he were guilty, he would accept death. Believers are not above the law; they are called to accept just consequences for wrongdoing while resisting injustice.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers who face injustice to seek fair treatment through legitimate means. Using legal rights, seeking protection, or appealing unfair decisions can be consistent with faithfulness to Christ. This includes seeking safety and legal protection in situations of abuse.

It also encourages believers to trust that God can use ordinary circumstances, such as legal processes, to accomplish His purposes. Believers may not see how God is working, but He can use even difficult situations to move His plans forward.

Finally, the verse reminds believers to be willing to accept responsibility for their actions. Paul did not ask to be spared if he was guilty. Believers can take responsibility for their wrongs while standing firm when they are innocent.

### Acts 25:19 — "One Jesus, Who Was Dead, Whom Paul Affirmed to Be Alive"

verse: Acts 25:19

#### Simple Explanation

King Agrippa and his sister Bernice had come to Caesarea to welcome the new governor, Festus. During their visit, Festus told Agrippa about Paul's case. He explained that the Jewish leaders had accused Paul, but that when they brought their charges, they did not accuse him of the crimes Festus expected.

[[v19|but had certain questions against him about their own religion]]. Festus saw the dispute as a religious disagreement among Jews. The word translated "religion" can also mean "superstition"; Festus may have been speaking politely to Agrippa, who was Jewish, but his view was that this was not a matter for Roman law.

[[v19|and about one Jesus, who was dead, whom Paul affirmed to be alive]]. Festus summarized the heart of the controversy. The dispute was about a man named Jesus. Everyone agreed that He had died. But Paul insisted that He was alive.

Festus's words are a remarkable summary of the Christian message from the perspective of an outsider. He did not understand it, and he was perplexed by it. But he recognized that the central issue was the resurrection of Jesus. Everything depended on whether Jesus was dead or alive.

The phrase "one Jesus" shows that Festus knew little about Him. To the Roman governor, Jesus was just another name. But to Paul, Jesus was the risen Lord of all.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that the resurrection is the center of the Christian faith. The early church's message was not merely that Jesus was a good teacher or that He died as a martyr. It was that He is alive. Paul wrote, [[1 Corinthians 15:14|If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain, and your faith also is in vain]].

It also shows that the resurrection forces a decision. Either Jesus is dead, or He is alive. If He is dead, Christianity is false. If He is alive, He is Lord, and everything changes. Festus did not know what to make of it. Every person must decide what they believe about the risen Jesus.

Finally, the verse shows that even outsiders recognized the core of the Christian message. The resurrection was not a later addition to Christianity. From the beginning, followers of Jesus proclaimed that He had risen from the dead.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites everyone to consider the central question: Is Jesus dead, or is He alive? This question matters more than any other. Believers can share the evidence for the resurrection and the difference it makes.

It also encourages believers to keep the resurrection at the center of their faith. The resurrection is not merely a doctrine to affirm on Easter; it is the foundation of hope, forgiveness, and new life every day.

Finally, the verse encourages believers to be patient with those who are perplexed by the gospel. Festus did not understand Paul's message. Many people today find the resurrection hard to believe. Believers can explain it patiently and pray for God to open their understanding.

### Acts 25:23 — Great Pomp and a Prisoner in Chains

verse: Acts 25:23

#### Simple Explanation

King Agrippa had expressed interest in hearing Paul. Festus arranged a hearing for the next day. Luke describes the scene vividly.

[[v23|So on the next day, when Agrippa and Bernice had come with great pomp]]. The word "pomp" (in Greek, phantasia, from which we get "fantasy") describes a grand display, a parade of wealth and status. Agrippa and Bernice arrived in their royal robes, with attendants and ceremony.

[[v23|and they had entered into the place of hearing with the commanding officers and the principal men of the city]]. The audience hall was filled with the most powerful people in Caesarea: the military commanders (there were five cohorts stationed in Caesarea, each with a commander) and the leading citizens of the city.

[[v23|at the command of Festus, Paul was brought in]]. Into this glittering assembly came Paul, a prisoner in chains (26:29). He had no wealth, no title, and no political power. He had been in prison for two years.

The contrast is striking. The powerful people in the room thought they were examining Paul. But in God's eyes, Paul was the one with the most important message, and they were the ones being given an opportunity to hear the truth. Jesus had said that Paul would bear His name [[Acts 9:15|before the nations and kings]]. This prophecy was being fulfilled.

Within a few years, Festus would be dead, Agrippa's kingdom would be gone, and the glory of this assembly would be forgotten. But Paul's message would spread throughout the world.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows the contrast between worldly glory and true greatness. The world is impressed by wealth, power, and display. But God values faithfulness and truth. Paul wrote, [[2 Corinthians 4:18|while we don’t look at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen. For the things which are seen are temporal, but the things which are not seen are eternal]].

It also shows that God gives opportunities to hear the gospel to people of every status. Agrippa, Bernice, Festus, the commanders, and the leading citizens all heard Paul's message. God reaches out to the powerful as well as the poor.

Finally, the verse shows the fulfillment of God's promises. Jesus had promised that Paul would testify before kings. Even in chains, Paul was exactly where God wanted him to be.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse encourages believers not to be impressed by worldly status. Wealth, power, and fame are temporary. Believers can value what God values: faithfulness, humility, and truth.

It also encourages believers who feel powerless. Paul was a prisoner in chains, yet he had the most important message in the room. Believers do not need status or power to have a meaningful witness. God can use them in any situation.

Finally, the verse invites believers to see every situation as an opportunity for witness. Paul was in chains before a king, yet he used the moment to share the gospel. Believers can look for opportunities to speak about Jesus wherever they find themselves, whether in humble or exalted settings.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Acts 25 shows the importance of integrity. Paul could declare that he had done nothing wrong against the Law, the temple, or Caesar. Believers can seek to live lives of integrity in every area, so that accusations against them have no basis.

The chapter also shows that it can be right to seek justice through legitimate means. Paul appealed to Caesar to avoid an unjust trial and an ambush. Believers facing injustice can use legal rights and protections, while being willing to accept just consequences for any real wrongdoing.

Acts 25 highlights the center of the Christian faith: Jesus, who was dead, is alive. Even a perplexed Roman governor recognized that this was the heart of the matter. Believers can keep the resurrection at the center of their faith and witness, and patiently help others consider this all-important question.

Finally, the chapter contrasts worldly glory with true greatness. Agrippa and Bernice entered with great pomp, while Paul came in chains. Yet Paul had the message of eternal life. Believers can refuse to be impressed by status, trust that God's promises are being fulfilled even in difficult circumstances, and look for opportunities to share Christ before people of every rank.
