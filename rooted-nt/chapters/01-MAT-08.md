# Matthew 8
theme: The Authority of Jesus Over Sickness, Storm, and Evil
main: After teaching with authority on the mountain, Jesus demonstrates that same authority in action. He cleanses a leper, heals a Gentile officer's servant from a distance, restores Peter's mother-in-law, calms a violent storm, and frees two men from demons. Along the way He shows that faith means trusting His word, and that following Him comes before every other claim.

## Chapter Background and Story

Matthew 8 begins immediately after the Sermon on the Mount. At the end of chapter 7, the crowds were astonished that Jesus taught [[Matthew 7:29|with authority]]. Matthew now shows that Jesus' authority is not only in His words but in His deeds. Chapters 8 and 9 together contain ten miracle stories, grouped carefully. They show Jesus' power over disease, nature, demons, and even death, and they reveal how different people respond to Him. Matthew's arrangement is more thematic than strictly chronological; he gathers similar events together to show who Jesus is.

The people Jesus helps in this chapter are striking: a leper, a Roman centurion, and a woman. In Jewish society these were all people at the margins of religious life in different ways. Lepers were ritually unclean and excluded from community; Gentiles were outside God's covenant people; and women had limited status in public religious life. Jesus' compassion reaches them first.

### The Cleansing of a Leper (8:1–4)

As Jesus came down the mountain, a man with leprosy approached, knelt before Him, and said, [[v2|Lord, if you want to, you can make me clean]]. In the Bible, "leprosy" referred to a range of skin diseases, not only what is today called Hansen's disease. Under the law of Moses (Leviticus 13–14), anyone with such a condition was declared unclean and had to live apart from others, wearing torn clothes and calling out "Unclean!" to warn people away. The disease brought physical suffering, social isolation, and religious exclusion. Touching a leper made a person ritually unclean.

The man did not doubt Jesus' power; he wondered whether Jesus was willing. Jesus answered by doing what no one else would do: He touched him. [[v3|I want to. Be made clean.]] Instead of Jesus becoming unclean, the man became clean. Jesus then told him to tell no one but to go to the priest and offer the sacrifice prescribed in Leviticus 14. This would officially restore the man to the community and serve [[v4|as a testimony to them]], evidence to the priests of what had happened. Jesus' instruction to keep quiet reflects His concern, seen throughout the Gospels, that people would misunderstand His mission if they saw Him only as a miracle-worker.

### The Centurion's Faith (8:5–13)

In Capernaum, a centurion came to Jesus. A centurion was a Roman army officer in charge of about a hundred soldiers. He may have served Herod Antipas rather than Rome directly, but either way he was a Gentile and a representative of foreign power. He told Jesus that his servant was at home paralyzed and suffering terribly. Luke tells us that this centurion was respected by the local Jewish leaders because he had built their synagogue (Luke 7:1–10).

Jesus immediately offered to come. The centurion replied, [[v8|Lord, I’m not worthy for you to come under my roof. Just say the word, and my servant will be healed]]. Devout Jews generally avoided entering Gentile homes because it would make them ritually unclean. The centurion may have been sparing Jesus that problem, but he also expressed deep humility. He explained his reasoning: as an officer, he understood authority. He gave orders and soldiers obeyed. He believed Jesus had the same kind of authority over sickness, so that a word from Him would be enough.

Jesus was amazed. He said He had not found such great faith anywhere in Israel. Then He made a startling prediction: many would come from east and west, meaning Gentiles from distant nations, and would feast with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven, while [[v12|the children of the Kingdom]], those who assumed they belonged because of their heritage, would be thrown out. The image of a banquet with the patriarchs draws on prophetic hopes such as Isaiah 25:6–8. "Weeping and gnashing of teeth" describes grief and anguish at being excluded. Jesus then told the centurion to go, and the servant was healed at that very hour.

### Healing at Peter's House (8:14–17)

Jesus went to Peter's house in Capernaum (archaeologists have uncovered a house there that early Christians venerated as Peter's home). Peter's mother-in-law was in bed with a fever. Jesus touched her hand, the fever left, and she got up and began to serve Him. Her immediate service shows both the completeness of the healing and a fitting response to Jesus.

That evening, people brought many who were demon-possessed and sick. Jesus drove out the spirits with a word and healed all the sick. Matthew sees this as fulfilling Isaiah 53:4: [[v17|He took our infirmities and bore our diseases]]. Isaiah 53 describes the Suffering Servant who bears the sins and griefs of others. Matthew shows that Jesus' healing ministry is part of His work as that Servant, a foretaste of the complete restoration His death and resurrection will bring.

### The Cost of Following Jesus (8:18–22)

When Jesus saw the crowds, He gave orders to cross to the other side of the lake. Before they left, two people spoke to Him about following Him. A scribe said enthusiastically, [[v19|Teacher, I will follow you wherever you go]]. Jesus replied that foxes have dens and birds have nests, but [[v20|the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head]]. "Son of Man" is the title Jesus used most often for Himself. It could simply mean "a human being," but it also recalls Daniel 7:13–14, where [[Daniel 7:13|one like a son of man]] receives everlasting dominion from God. Jesus' point to the scribe was that following Him meant sharing a life without security or comfort.

Another disciple said, [[v21|Lord, allow me first to go and bury my father]]. Burying one's parents was a sacred duty in Jewish culture. The man's father may not yet have died; the phrase could mean, "Let me wait until my father dies and I have fulfilled my family obligations." Either way, Jesus' reply was shocking: [[v22|Follow me, and leave the dead to bury their own dead]]. He meant that those who are spiritually dead could take care of such matters, while the call to follow Him took priority. Jesus was not teaching disrespect for parents; elsewhere He rebuked those who neglected them (Matthew 15:3–6). He was teaching that His claim comes before even the most important human obligations.

### Calming the Storm (8:23–27)

Jesus got into a boat, and His disciples followed Him. Matthew's wording links this scene to the preceding sayings: this is what following Jesus looks like. A violent storm arose on the lake, so that the waves covered the boat. The Sea of Galilee lies about seven hundred feet below sea level, surrounded by hills, and winds can sweep down suddenly and stir up dangerous storms. Several of the disciples were experienced fishermen, and they were terrified. Meanwhile, Jesus was asleep.

They woke Him, crying, [[v25|Save us, Lord! We are dying!]] Jesus first addressed their fear: [[v26|Why are you fearful, O you of little faith?]] Then He rose and rebuked the wind and waves, and there was a great calm. The disciples were amazed: [[v27|What kind of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?]] In the Old Testament, it is God who rules the raging sea and stills its waves (Psalm 89:9; 107:28–29). The disciples were beginning to realize that Jesus was far more than a teacher or healer.

### The Demon-Possessed Men in the Gadarene Region (8:28–34)

On the other side of the lake, in the region Matthew calls the country of the Gergesenes (other manuscripts and Gospels read Gadarenes or Gerasenes; the names refer to towns in the same largely Gentile region), two demon-possessed men came out of the tombs to meet Jesus. They were so violent that no one could pass that way. Mark and Luke focus on one of the men, while Matthew mentions both.

The demons recognized Jesus immediately: [[v29|What do we have to do with you, Jesus, Son of God? Have you come here to torment us before the time?]] They knew that God had appointed a time of final judgment for evil spirits, and they were alarmed that Jesus had come to confront them already. A large herd of pigs was feeding nearby, which confirms that this was Gentile territory, since Jews did not raise pigs. The demons begged to be sent into the pigs. Jesus said one word, "Go!" The demons left the men and entered the pigs, and the whole herd rushed down the steep bank into the lake and drowned.

The herdsmen ran into the town and reported everything. The whole town came out to meet Jesus, but instead of welcoming Him, they begged Him to leave their region. They had seen His power, but they were more concerned about their economic loss and their fear than about the freedom of two tormented men. The chapter ends with this sad response: people who preferred to keep their lives as they were rather than have Jesus among them.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 8:3 — [[Matthew 8:3|I Want To. Be Made Clean]].

verse: Matthew 8:3

#### Simple Explanation

This verse describes the first miracle Matthew records after the Sermon on the Mount. A man with leprosy came to Jesus as He came down the mountain with great crowds following. Under the law of Moses, such a man was considered unclean and was required to live apart from others (Leviticus 13:45–46). He would not have been allowed to approach crowds, and certainly not to come near a teacher. His approach was itself an act of desperate faith.

The man knelt before Jesus and said, [[v2|Lord, if you want to, you can make me clean]]. He was confident in Jesus' power but unsure of His willingness. Perhaps years of rejection had taught him to expect that no one wanted to come near him.

Jesus' response came in action before words: He [[v3|stretched out his hand and touched him]]. This touch was remarkable. According to the law, touching an unclean person made the one who touched unclean. Jesus could have healed with a word, as He would for the centurion's servant. Instead, He chose to touch a man who may not have been touched by anyone in a long time. Then He answered the man's exact question: "I want to." He was willing. "Be made clean." Immediately the leprosy disappeared.

The direction of contagion was reversed. Normally uncleanness spread from the unclean to the clean. With Jesus, cleanness spread from Him to the unclean. He was not defiled; the man was healed.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals the heart of Jesus toward the excluded and the ashamed. The leper's question, "if you want to," is one many people ask about God. They believe God is powerful, but they wonder whether He cares about them. Jesus' answer, in word and touch, is clear: He is willing.

In the Old Testament, leprosy was not only a physical condition but a picture of uncleanness that separated a person from God's presence and His people. Throughout Scripture, God's holiness required separation from uncleanness. Jesus shows that God's holiness is not merely a barrier that excludes; in Christ, it becomes a power that cleanses and restores. This anticipates the deeper cleansing from sin that Jesus came to bring. As 1 John 1:7 says, [[1 John 1:7|the blood of Jesus Christ, his Son, cleanses us from all sin]].

Jesus' instruction to go to the priest also shows that He respected the law of Moses and wanted the man fully restored to community life. Healing was not only about the body; it included restored relationships and full participation in worship.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites anyone who feels unclean, ashamed, or unworthy to come to Jesus with confidence. Whatever sense of defilement a person carries, whether from their own sin, from what others have done to them, or from circumstances that have made them feel excluded, Jesus is willing to cleanse and restore. The question is not whether He is able or willing; it is whether we will come.

It also challenges believers to imitate Jesus' compassion. In every community there are people who feel untouchable: the sick, the stigmatized, the lonely, and those whose past makes others keep their distance. Followers of Jesus can show His love through practical presence, kindness, and inclusion, while taking appropriate care for health and safety.

Finally, Jesus' concern for the man's restoration to community reminds believers that healing includes belonging. The church is called to welcome people who have been restored by Christ and help them find their place among God's people.

### Matthew 8:10 — Great Faith From an Unexpected Place

verse: Matthew 8:10

#### Simple Explanation

This verse records Jesus' reaction to the Roman centurion in Capernaum. The centurion had asked Jesus to heal his servant, who was paralyzed and suffering. When Jesus offered to come to his house, the centurion said he was not worthy to have Jesus under his roof. He asked Jesus simply to say the word, explaining that he understood authority: as an officer, he gave orders and they were carried out. He believed Jesus' word had that same authority over sickness.

Matthew says that Jesus [[v10|marveled]]. Only twice in the Gospels is Jesus said to be amazed: here at a Gentile's faith, and in Mark 6:6 at the unbelief of the people in His hometown of Nazareth. Jesus turned to the crowd following Him and said that He had not found faith like this anywhere in Israel.

What made this faith great? First, it was humble: the centurion, a man of power and position, said he was not worthy. Second, it was confident: he believed that Jesus' word alone was enough, without any need for Jesus to be physically present. Third, it was perceptive: he recognized that Jesus' authority came from God, just as his own authority came from Rome. He understood that sickness had to obey Jesus' command.

The original listeners would have been surprised. They might have expected great faith from religious leaders or devout Israelites, not from a Gentile officer associated with foreign occupation. Jesus' praise turned their expectations upside down, and His next words warned that many Gentiles would share in the kingdom while some who assumed they belonged would be excluded.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse shows that faith, not ethnicity or religious status, is what connects a person to God's kingdom. The centurion had no covenant privileges as a Gentile, yet he received Jesus' blessing because he trusted Him. This anticipates the great theme of the New Testament that Gentiles are welcomed into God's people through faith in Christ (Romans 1:16; Ephesians 2:11–22).

The verse also teaches what faith is. Faith is not a feeling of certainty that something will happen, or a technique for obtaining results. It is trust in the person and authority of Jesus. The centurion's faith rested entirely on who Jesus was and what His word could do.

At the same time, Jesus' warning in the following verses shows that privilege without faith is dangerous. Those who grew up with God's promises can take them for granted and miss the One the promises point to. Religious heritage is a gift, but it must lead to personal trust in Christ.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse invites believers to trust the authority of Jesus' word. Much of Christian life involves trusting His promises when we cannot see Him physically present or the outcome yet. Scripture records His words so that believers can rely on them, just as the centurion relied on Jesus' spoken command.

It also teaches humility in faith. The centurion did not think he deserved Jesus' help; he trusted Jesus' goodness and power. Believers can come to God not on the basis of their own worthiness but on the basis of His grace.

Finally, the centurion's request was on behalf of someone else, a servant who in that culture would have had little status. His faith expressed itself in compassion. Believers can bring the needs of others to Jesus in prayer and act on their behalf, especially for those who have no one else to speak for them.

### Matthew 8:26 — Fear and Faith in the Storm

verse: Matthew 8:26

#### Simple Explanation

This verse comes in the middle of the storm on the Sea of Galilee. Jesus had told His disciples to cross the lake, and they followed Him into the boat. Suddenly a violent storm arose, so severe that the waves were covering the boat. The disciples, some of whom were experienced fishermen who knew these waters, believed they were going to die. Meanwhile, Jesus was asleep, likely exhausted after a long day of teaching and healing.

The disciples woke Him, crying, [[v25|Save us, Lord! We are dying!]] Their prayer was urgent and sincere. They turned to the right person. Yet Jesus' first words were a gentle rebuke: [[v26|Why are you fearful, O you of little faith?]] Note the order in Matthew's account: Jesus speaks to their fear before He calms the storm. The phrase "little faith" appears several times in Matthew (6:30; 14:31; 16:8; 17:20). It does not mean no faith; the disciples did turn to Jesus. It means faith that was not strong enough to overcome their fear. They had seen His power, yet in the crisis they acted as if He might let them drown.

Then Jesus [[v26|got up, rebuked the wind and the sea, and there was a great calm]]. The word "rebuked" is the same word used when Jesus rebukes demons. He spoke to the storm as to a force under His command. The sudden calm, rather than a gradual lessening of the wind, showed that this was not a natural change in the weather.

The disciples' response was awe: [[v27|What kind of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?]] In the Old Testament, only God controls the seas (Psalm 107:23–30). The disciples were beginning to see that Jesus shared God's own authority over creation.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse reveals that following Jesus does not guarantee calm waters. The disciples were in the storm precisely because they obeyed Jesus' instruction to cross the lake. Faithfulness can lead into difficulty. Yet Jesus was with them in the boat, and He was not anxious. His sleep reflected perfect trust in His Father's care, the same trust He described in Matthew 6.

The calming of the storm also reveals Jesus' identity. He commands the forces of nature as only the Creator can. Matthew wants readers to share the disciples' question, "What kind of man is this?" and to arrive at the answer: He is the Son of God, with authority over all creation.

Jesus' rebuke of the disciples' fear is not harsh condemnation. It is a teacher's challenge, calling them to grow. Faith does not mean never feeling afraid, but it means that fear does not have the final word because we know who is with us.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

Many believers face storms: illness, financial crisis, family conflict, grief, or danger. This verse reminds them that Jesus is present in those storms. Like the disciples, they can cry out to Him honestly. He welcomes such prayers.

It also invites believers to examine their fear. Fear often reveals what we believe about God when we are under pressure. If we believe He has forgotten us or does not care, fear will dominate. If we remember His power and His presence, faith can steady us even before circumstances change.

Finally, this story encourages growth in faith over time. The disciples' faith was small, but Jesus kept teaching them. Believers can learn from each storm, recalling how God has been faithful in the past and building confidence for the future. Faith grows as we see, repeatedly, that the One in the boat with us has authority over the wind and the waves.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 8 shows Jesus' authority over every kind of brokenness: disease, distance, demonic oppression, and the forces of nature. This means that no situation is outside His reach. Believers can bring every need to Him, confident that He is both able and willing to act, even when His answer comes in a different way or at a different time than they expect.

The chapter also shows the kinds of people Jesus welcomes. He touched a leper, praised the faith of a Gentile soldier, and healed a woman in her home. He crossed into Gentile territory to free two men whom everyone else feared. His compassion broke through social and religious barriers. Believers today are called to the same kind of love, reaching out to people who are excluded, feared, or overlooked, and treating them with dignity.

Faith is a central theme throughout the chapter. The leper believed in Jesus' power; the centurion trusted His word; the disciples had "little faith" in the storm. Faith grows when it rests on who Jesus is rather than on our circumstances or feelings. Believers can strengthen their faith by remembering His authority, trusting His promises in Scripture, and bringing their fears honestly to Him in prayer.

The chapter also includes a sober reminder about the cost of discipleship. Jesus told one would-be follower that He had nowhere to lay His head and told another that following Him came before even family obligations. Following Jesus requires putting Him first. And the chapter ends with a town that asked Jesus to leave because His presence had disrupted their economy. Every reader must decide whether to welcome Jesus' authority, even when it costs something, or to send Him away in order to keep life as it is.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for Your authority over sickness, storms, and every power of evil. Thank You that You were willing to touch the man no one else would touch, and that You still welcome those who feel unclean or unworthy. Cleanse me, Lord, and help me to show Your compassion to people others avoid.

Give me faith like the centurion's, humble and confident in Your word. When storms rise in my life and I feel afraid, remind me that You are with me in the boat and that the wind and waves obey You. Help my small faith to grow. Teach me to follow You even when it costs me comfort or security, and keep me from ever asking You to leave because Your presence disrupts what I want to protect. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
