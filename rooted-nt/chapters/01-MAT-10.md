# Matthew 10
theme: Sent Out as Sheep Among Wolves
main: Jesus chooses twelve apostles, gives them His authority, and sends them to announce the kingdom to Israel. He prepares them honestly for rejection and persecution, teaches them to be wise and innocent, assures them of the Father's detailed care, and calls them to love Him above every other loyalty, promising that even the smallest act of kindness toward His messengers will be rewarded.

## Chapter Background and Story

At the end of Matthew 9, Jesus looked at the crowds with compassion, seeing them as [[Matthew 9:36|harassed and scattered, like sheep without a shepherd]]. He told His disciples to pray that the Lord of the harvest would send out workers. Matthew 10 shows the answer to that prayer: Jesus Himself sends out the Twelve. This chapter is the second of five major teaching sections in Matthew (after the Sermon on the Mount in chapters 5–7). It is often called the Mission Discourse.

The chapter has two horizons. Some instructions apply specifically to the Twelve's immediate mission in Galilee, such as going only to Israel and taking no money. Other parts look further ahead, describing persecution before governors and kings, which happened after Jesus' resurrection as the gospel spread through the Roman world. Jesus prepares His followers not only for a short preaching tour but for the long road of witness that lay ahead.

### The Twelve Apostles (10:1–4)

Jesus called His twelve disciples and gave them authority over unclean spirits and over every disease. Here Matthew calls them "apostles" for the only time in his Gospel. The word means "those who are sent," like official messengers or ambassadors. The number twelve is deliberate. Israel was made up of twelve tribes, and by choosing twelve, Jesus signals that He is forming a renewed people of God.

The list includes Simon Peter, named [[v2|The first]], not because he was greater than the others but because he was the leading spokesman. It includes two sets of brothers (Peter and Andrew, James and John), Matthew the tax collector, and Simon the Zealot. The Zealots were Jewish nationalists who opposed Roman rule, sometimes violently, while tax collectors cooperated with the ruling powers. Bringing both into one group shows the reconciling power of Jesus' call. The list ends with Judas Iscariot, [[v4|who also betrayed him]], a reminder from the very beginning that the story leads to the cross.

### Instructions for the Mission (10:5–15)

Jesus sent the Twelve with specific instructions. They were not to go to Gentiles or Samaritans but [[v6|to the lost sheep of the house of Israel]]. This was not because Jesus did not care about Gentiles; He had just healed a centurion's servant (Matthew 8). It reflected God's plan to offer the kingdom first to Israel, as Paul later put it, [[Romans 1:16|for the Jew first, and also for the Greek]]. After the resurrection, Jesus will send His disciples to all nations (Matthew 28:19).

Their message was the same as Jesus': [[v7|The Kingdom of Heaven is at hand!]] Their actions would show the kingdom's presence: healing the sick, cleansing lepers, casting out demons, and (in some manuscripts) raising the dead. They were to minister without charge: [[v8|Freely you received, so freely give]].

They were to travel light, taking no money, extra clothing, sandals, or staff beyond what they had. They were to depend on the hospitality of those who received them, since [[v10|the laborer is worthy of his food]]. In each town they were to find a worthy household, someone open to their message, and stay there. Their greeting of "peace" (the Hebrew "shalom," meaning wholeness and well-being from God) would rest on a receptive household or return to them if rejected. If a town refused to hear them, they were to shake the dust off their feet as they left, a symbolic act showing that the town was responsible for its own choice. Jews returning from Gentile lands sometimes shook off the dust so as not to bring unclean soil into Israel; doing this to a Jewish town would be a startling warning. Jesus said that such a town would face a judgment worse than Sodom and Gomorrah, cities destroyed for their wickedness (Genesis 19), because it had rejected a greater revelation.

### Warnings of Persecution (10:16–25)

Jesus then spoke honestly about the dangers ahead: [[v16|Behold, I send you out as sheep among wolves]]. His followers would be handed over to local councils and flogged in synagogues. They would stand before governors and kings, giving testimony about Jesus. The book of Acts records exactly such events: Peter and John before the Sanhedrin (Acts 4), Paul before governors Felix and Festus and King Agrippa (Acts 24–26).

Jesus told them not to worry about what to say when arrested, because [[v20|it is not you who speak, but the Spirit of your Father who speaks in you]]. This promise applies to moments of persecution, not to normal preparation for teaching. He warned that persecution would divide families, with relatives betraying one another. They would be hated by everyone because of Jesus' name, but [[v22|he who endures to the end will be saved]].

When persecuted in one town, they were to flee to the next. Jesus added a difficult saying: [[v23|you will not have gone through the cities of Israel until the Son of Man has come]]. Christians have interpreted this in several ways. Some think it refers to Jesus' resurrection and vindication, others to the judgment that came on Jerusalem in A.D. 70, and others to Jesus' final return, meaning that the mission to Israel will continue until the end. The main point is clear: the disciples' mission would be urgent and unfinished, and they should keep moving rather than giving up.

Jesus reminded them that a disciple is not above his teacher. If people called Jesus "Beelzebul" (a name for Satan, meaning something like "lord of the house" or "lord of the flies"), as the Pharisees had in effect done in Matthew 9:34, His followers should expect similar treatment.

### Do Not Fear (10:26–33)

Three times in this section Jesus says, "Don't be afraid." First, they should not fear because the truth will come out; what is hidden will be revealed. What He taught them privately, they were to proclaim openly, from the housetops (flat roofs from which public announcements could be made).

Second, they should not fear those who can kill the body but cannot kill the soul. Instead, they should fear God, [[v28|him who is able to destroy both soul and body in Gehenna]]. Persecutors have limited power; God has ultimate authority. Proper reverence for God frees people from paralyzing fear of others.

Third, they should not fear because the Father cares for them in detail. Not even a sparrow, sold cheaply in the market, falls to the ground apart from the Father's will. The very hairs of their heads are counted. They are worth more than many sparrows. Then Jesus gave a promise and a warning: those who acknowledge Him before others, He will acknowledge before His Father; those who deny Him, He will deny.

### Not Peace but a Sword (10:34–39)

Jesus then said something that may seem surprising: [[v34|I didn’t come to send peace, but a sword]]. He was not encouraging violence; in Matthew 26:52 He tells Peter to put away his sword. He meant that His coming would divide people, even within families, because some would follow Him and others would oppose Him. He quoted Micah 7:6, which describes family members turning against each other. Jesus brings peace with God, but loyalty to Him can create conflict with those who reject Him.

He then declared that anyone who loves father, mother, son, or daughter more than Him is not worthy of Him. This does not mean disciples should stop loving their families; Scripture commands honoring parents and caring for one's household. It means that Jesus must have first place. Anyone who does not [[v38|take his cross and follow after me]] is not worthy of Him. This is the first time Jesus mentions the cross in Matthew. In the Roman world, a condemned man carried his own crossbeam to the place of execution. Taking up one's cross meant being willing to suffer and even die for Jesus' sake. Then came the paradox: those who try to save their lives will lose them, but those who lose their lives for Jesus will find them.

### Receiving Christ's Messengers (10:40–42)

Jesus ended with encouragement. Whoever receives His disciples receives Him, and whoever receives Him receives the Father who sent Him. Those who welcome a prophet or a righteous person will share in their reward. Even giving [[v42|just a cup of cold water]] to one of His "little ones," His humble followers, will not go unrewarded. The mission would face rejection, but it would also find welcome, and every act of kindness toward Christ's messengers would be remembered by God.

## Important Verses Explained

### Matthew 10:16 — Wise as Serpents, Harmless as Doves

verse: Matthew 10:16

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words as He sends the Twelve on their mission. He has just told them where to go, what to say, and how to travel, and now He turns to the dangers they will face. The word "Behold" draws attention to a serious warning: [[v16|I send you out as sheep among wolves]].

Sheep are defenseless animals; wolves are predators. Jesus is honest that His followers would be vulnerable. He does not send them as soldiers with weapons or as powerful leaders with protection, but as people who would be exposed to hostility. Notice also who is doing the sending: "I send you." They go under His authority and His care, even into danger.

Then Jesus tells them how to conduct themselves: [[v16|be wise as serpents and harmless as doves]]. In the ancient world, serpents were proverbial for shrewdness and caution (Genesis 3:1 describes the serpent as more subtle than any other animal). Jesus is not asking His disciples to be deceptive, but to be alert, careful, and wise about danger. Doves were seen as gentle, simple, and innocent. The word translated "harmless" means pure, unmixed, without hidden motives. Disciples must be both shrewd and innocent: wise enough to avoid unnecessary danger and recognize deception, and pure enough that they never resort to wrongdoing.

The following verses show what this looks like. They must beware of people who would hand them over to councils. They should flee from one town to the next when persecuted, not seeking martyrdom unnecessarily. Yet they must also remain faithful and speak boldly when called to testify.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse holds together two qualities that are often separated. Some people are wise and shrewd but lose their integrity, becoming manipulative or cynical. Others are innocent and well-meaning but naive, easily deceived or harmed. Jesus calls His followers to both wisdom and innocence. Paul expresses a similar balance: [[Romans 16:19|I desire to have you wise in that which is good, but innocent in that which is evil]].

The image of sheep among wolves also reveals the nature of Christian witness. Christ's followers do not advance His kingdom by force, manipulation, or retaliation. They go in vulnerability, relying on God's protection and the truth of the message. This follows the pattern of Jesus Himself, the Lamb of God, who conquered not by violence but by laying down His life.

At the same time, Jesus' call to wisdom shows that faithfulness does not mean recklessness. Believers are not required to put themselves in harm's way needlessly. Jesus Himself withdrew from danger at times (Matthew 12:15), and He tells His disciples to flee persecution. Wisdom and courage work together.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to engage the world with both discernment and integrity. In workplaces, communities, and online spaces, followers of Jesus will encounter hostility, manipulation, and deception. Being wise as serpents means paying attention, understanding how people and systems work, recognizing danger, and making careful decisions. It includes knowing when to speak, when to remain silent, and when to step away from a harmful situation.

Being harmless as doves means refusing to use dishonest tactics, even when others do. Believers should not lie, manipulate, retaliate, or compromise their character to protect themselves or gain advantage. Their motives should be pure, and their words trustworthy.

This balance also applies to safety. Wisdom may mean setting boundaries with people who are harmful, seeking protection, or removing oneself from dangerous situations. Innocence means doing so without bitterness or revenge. Believers can ask God for both qualities, especially when they face situations where they feel vulnerable.

### Matthew 10:29–31 — The Father Who Counts Every Hair

verse: Matthew 10:29-31

#### Simple Explanation

Jesus speaks these words as part of His warnings about persecution. He has just told His disciples that they will be handed over to councils, flogged, brought before rulers, betrayed by family members, and hated because of Him. Then He tells them not to fear those who can kill the body but cannot kill the soul. These verses give a further reason not to be afraid.

Sparrows were among the cheapest items sold in the marketplace. Poor people bought them for food. Jesus says that two sparrows sold for an "assarion," a small copper coin worth about one-sixteenth of a day's wage. (Luke 12:6 says five were sold for two coins, meaning a fifth one was thrown in for free when buying four.) These birds were almost worthless by human standards. Yet [[v29|Not one of them falls to the ground apart from your Father’s will]]. Nothing happens to even the smallest creature outside of God's knowledge and sovereign care.

Jesus then says, [[v30|But the very hairs of your head are all numbered]]. Most people do not know how many hairs they have; God does. This vivid exaggeration shows that God's knowledge of His children is complete and detailed.

The conclusion follows: [[v31|Therefore don’t be afraid. You are of more value than many sparrows]]. If God watches over sparrows, He certainly watches over His children, who are far more valuable to Him.

Notice that Jesus does not promise that the disciples will never suffer. A sparrow may still fall. The disciples may still be persecuted or even killed. The promise is that nothing happens to them apart from the Father, and that they remain precious to Him through everything.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

These verses teach the doctrine of God's providence, His wise and caring rule over all things, including the smallest details of life. God is not a distant creator who set the world running and stepped away. He is present and attentive to every creature. This truth is a source of great comfort, especially in times of danger and uncertainty.

The passage also affirms the value of each person. In a world where people are often treated as insignificant, Jesus insists that each of His followers is known and valued by the Father. This value does not come from status, achievement, or usefulness but from God's love.

At the same time, this passage does not explain why God allows suffering or how His sovereignty relates to human evil. Persecutors are responsible for their actions, and Jesus calls their actions wicked. Yet even through suffering, God is present and working. Paul expresses the same confidence: nothing can separate believers from the love of God in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:35–39).

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

These verses invite believers to bring their fears to a Father who knows every detail of their lives. Fear often grows when we feel unseen or alone. Remembering that God knows even the number of our hairs can calm anxious hearts and strengthen trust.

They also encourage courage in faithful living. If God's care surrounds His children, they can face opposition, criticism, or hardship without being controlled by fear. This does not mean they will avoid all suffering, but it means suffering will not have the final word.

Finally, these verses remind believers to value others as God does. If God cares for sparrows and counts the hairs of His children's heads, believers should treat every person, especially those considered unimportant, with dignity and care.

### Matthew 10:39 — Losing Life to Find It

verse: Matthew 10:39

#### Simple Explanation

This verse is the climax of Jesus' teaching on the cost of discipleship. He has just said that following Him may divide families, that anyone who loves family more than Him is not worthy of Him, and that anyone who does not take up their cross and follow Him is not worthy of Him. Now He states a paradox that summarizes it all: [[v39|He who seeks his life will lose it; and he who loses his life for my sake will find it]].

The word "life" here (Greek "psyche") can mean physical life, but also the self, the soul, or one's whole existence. Jesus is saying that people who make preserving their own lives, comfort, and security their highest goal will ultimately lose what matters most. Those who are willing to give up their lives for Jesus' sake, whether through martyrdom or through daily self-denial, will find true and eternal life.

For the disciples, this was not abstract. Jesus had just spoken of persecution, flogging, and death. Some of them would indeed die for their faith. Tradition holds that most of the apostles were martyred. Jesus was preparing them to see that losing their lives for Him would not be the end, but the way to true life.

The phrase "for my sake" is essential. Jesus is not praising suffering for its own sake or encouraging people to throw their lives away. He is speaking about loss that comes from loyalty to Him.

#### Deeper Spiritual Meaning

This verse expresses one of the central paradoxes of the Christian faith. The way to life passes through death; the way to gain is through loss. Jesus Himself lived out this principle. He gave up His life on the cross and was raised to life. His followers share in this pattern. Jesus repeats this saying in Matthew 16:25, and it appears in all four Gospels, showing how important it was in His teaching.

The verse also challenges the human instinct for self-preservation. Every person naturally seeks to protect and promote themselves. Jesus says that this instinct, when it becomes the center of life, leads to loss. True life is found by giving oneself to Christ and to others in love.

This teaching includes both the future and the present. Ultimately, those who lose their lives for Jesus will receive eternal life. But even now, those who surrender their lives to Him discover a deeper joy, purpose, and freedom than self-centered living can provide.

#### Applying This Teaching to Life Today

This verse calls believers to examine what they are holding onto. Comfort, reputation, financial security, control, and personal plans can become things we seek to protect at any cost. Jesus invites His followers to hold these loosely and to be willing to give them up when loyalty to Him requires it.

For most believers, losing one's life for Jesus happens through daily choices: putting His will above personal preference, serving others when it is inconvenient, choosing integrity over advantage, and speaking up for the truth when it is costly. These small acts of self-giving are ways of taking up the cross.

This verse also offers hope. When following Jesus brings loss, such as broken relationships, missed opportunities, or hardship, believers can trust that nothing given up for Him is truly lost. He promises that those who lose their lives for His sake will find them.

## How to Apply This Chapter to Life Today

Matthew 10 reminds believers that following Jesus includes being sent by Him. Although the specific instructions for the Twelve's first mission do not all apply directly today, the principles do: Jesus sends His followers into the world with His message, His authority, and His care. Every believer has a part in His mission, whether in their family, workplace, or community.

The chapter also prepares believers for opposition. Jesus did not hide the cost of following Him. He warned that His followers would face rejection, persecution, and even betrayal by family members. Believers today should not be surprised when faithfulness brings difficulty. Instead, they can respond with the wisdom of serpents and the innocence of doves, careful and discerning, yet never compromising their integrity.

Fear is a major theme of the chapter, and Jesus addresses it directly. He tells His followers not to fear those who can only harm the body, and He reminds them of the Father's detailed care. Believers can bring their fears to God, remember His sovereignty and love, and find courage to speak and live faithfully. Reverence for God, rather than fear of people, sets the direction of a disciple's life.

Finally, the chapter calls believers to give Jesus first place in their lives. Family, comfort, and even life itself must come after loyalty to Him. This does not diminish love for family; it places all relationships under His lordship. And Jesus promises that nothing done for His sake is wasted. Even a cup of cold water given in His name will be rewarded. Believers can serve faithfully, knowing that every act of love toward Christ and His people matters to God.

## Chapter Prayer

Lord Jesus, thank You for calling ordinary people to be Your messengers and for sending them out with Your authority and care. Thank You that You were honest about the cost of following You. When I face opposition or misunderstanding because of my faith, help me not to be surprised or discouraged.

Make me wise as a serpent and harmless as a dove, discerning about danger and deception, yet pure in my motives and actions. When I am afraid, remind me that You know every detail of my life and that I am precious to my Father in heaven. Give me courage to acknowledge You before others. Help me to love You above everything and everyone, to take up my cross daily, and to trust that whatever I lose for Your sake, I will find in You. Use even my small acts of kindness to honor You. In Jesus’ name, Amen.
