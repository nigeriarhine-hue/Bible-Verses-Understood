# Ephesians

## About This Letter

Ephesians presents itself as a letter from Paul, written while he was a prisoner for the Lord (Ephesians 3:1; 4:1; 6:20). The traditional view, held by most Christians through history, is that Paul wrote it during his Roman imprisonment around A.D. 60–62, at about the same time as Colossians and Philemon, and that Tychicus carried it to its readers (6:21–22). Some modern scholars think it was written after Paul's death by a close follower who summarized his teaching, because its style and emphases differ somewhat from his earlier letters. Others answer that differences of purpose, setting, and the use of a secretary explain the change in style. Either way, the church has received Ephesians as Scripture from the earliest centuries.

Paul had spent nearly three years in Ephesus, a great city of the Roman province of Asia and home to the famous temple of Artemis (Acts 19). Yet the letter contains few personal greetings, and the words "at Ephesus" in 1:1 are missing from some of the earliest manuscripts. For this reason, many think Ephesians was a circular letter intended for several churches in the region, with Ephesus as the leading city.

## The Shape of the Letter

Ephesians falls into two halves. Chapters 1–3 celebrate what God has done in Christ: every spiritual blessing, chosen and adopted, redeemed and sealed with the Spirit, raised from spiritual death by grace through faith, and brought together, Jew and Gentile, into one new people, one household, and one temple. Paul prays twice for his readers to grasp these riches and the immeasurable love of Christ. Chapters 4–6 show how to live in light of this calling: in unity and maturity as one body, putting off the old self and putting on the new, walking in love and light, being filled with the Spirit, honoring Christ in households, and standing firm in the armor of God.

## Themes to Watch For

Watch for the phrase "in Christ" and its many variations, God's eternal plan to bring all things together under Christ, the riches of grace, salvation as a gift and not by works, the church as Christ's body, bride, household, and temple, the breaking down of the dividing wall between Jew and Gentile, the mystery now revealed, the power of the risen Christ at work in believers, unity in diversity of gifts, the new life of holiness and love, and the reality of spiritual warfare. Ephesians lifts the reader's eyes to the cosmic scope of God's purpose and then brings that vision down to everyday relationships at home, at work, and in the church.
