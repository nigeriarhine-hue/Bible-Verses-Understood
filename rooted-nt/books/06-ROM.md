# Romans

## About This Letter

Paul wrote Romans around A.D. 57, probably from Corinth, near the end of his third missionary journey (Acts 20:2–3; Romans 15:25–26; 16:1, 23). He had not yet visited Rome, but he hoped to stop there on his way to Spain and to receive the church's support for that mission (15:23–24). The church in Rome included both Jewish and Gentile believers. Emperor Claudius had expelled Jews from Rome around A.D. 49 (Acts 18:2), and when they returned after his death in A.D. 54, they found congregations now led largely by Gentiles. Tensions between the two groups may lie behind much of the letter. Paul sent the letter by Phoebe, a servant of the church in Cenchreae (Romans 16:1–2).

## The Shape of the Letter

Romans is the most complete explanation of the gospel in the New Testament. After his greeting, Paul states his theme: the gospel is the power of God for salvation for everyone who believes, revealing God's righteousness "from faith to faith" (1:16–17). He then shows that all people, Gentiles and Jews alike, are under sin (1:18–3:20), and that God's righteousness is given as a gift through faith in Jesus Christ, whose death made atonement for sin (3:21–4:25). He describes the new life that results: peace with God, freedom from the reign of sin and death, and life in the Spirit, ending in the triumphant assurance that nothing can separate believers from God's love (chapters 5–8). Chapters 9–11 address God's faithfulness to Israel and His plan to show mercy to Jews and Gentiles alike. Chapters 12–16 describe how the gospel shapes daily life: living sacrifices, love in the church, relationships with authorities, and welcome across differences.

## Themes to Watch For

Look for the righteousness of God, both His own justice and the right standing He gives to sinners; justification by faith apart from works of the Law; the universal reach of sin and of grace; union with Christ in His death and resurrection; the work of the Holy Spirit; God's faithfulness to His promises to Israel; and the call for Jewish and Gentile believers to welcome one another as Christ has welcomed them. Romans has shaped Christian thought perhaps more than any other book, from Augustine to Martin Luther to John Wesley, each of whom found new life through its pages.
