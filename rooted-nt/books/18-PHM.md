# Philemon

## About This Letter

Philemon is the shortest and most personal of Paul's letters. Paul wrote it from prison, probably in Rome around A.D. 60–62, at the same time as Colossians, and nearly all scholars agree that Paul himself wrote it. It is addressed to Philemon, a believer in Colossae in whose home a church met, and also to Apphia, Archippus, and the whole house church.

The letter concerns Onesimus, a slave who belonged to Philemon. Onesimus had left his master, perhaps running away, perhaps after wronging him in some way, and possibly seeking out Paul to plead his case. While with Paul, Onesimus became a believer and a beloved helper. Under Roman law, a runaway slave could face severe punishment. Paul sends Onesimus back to Philemon, carrying this letter, in which Paul appeals to Philemon to receive Onesimus not merely as a slave but as a beloved brother in the Lord, and offers to pay anything Onesimus owes.

## The Shape of the Letter

After a warm greeting, Paul thanks God for Philemon's love and faith, which have refreshed the hearts of the believers. Rather than commanding, Paul appeals to Philemon on the basis of love, as an old man and a prisoner, on behalf of Onesimus, whose name means "useful," and who has become truly useful. Paul expresses his wish to have kept Onesimus with him but sends him back so that Philemon's kindness may be freely given. He suggests that God may have allowed the separation so that Philemon might have Onesimus back forever, as a brother. Paul asks Philemon to welcome Onesimus as he would welcome Paul himself, to charge any debt to Paul's account, and to do even more than Paul asks. He closes with a request for a guest room and greetings from his companions.

## Themes to Watch For

Watch for the transforming power of the gospel in a single life, reconciliation between people separated by wrongs and by social status, the new family of God in which a slave and his master are brothers, love that appeals rather than commands, forgiveness, the willingness to bear another's debt, and the quiet ways the gospel undermines the foundations of slavery. In a few short lines, Philemon offers a living picture of what Christ has done for us: He stands between us and the one we have wronged and says, "Put that to my account."
