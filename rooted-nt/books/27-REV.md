# Revelation

## About This Book

The book of Revelation opens with its own title: [[Revelation 1:1|This is the Revelation of Jesus Christ]]. The word "revelation" translates the Greek word *apokalypsis*, meaning an unveiling or disclosure. Revelation pulls back the curtain so that God's people can see what is happening behind the events of history: the risen Christ ruling over His church, God reigning from His throne, evil being judged, and all things being made new.

The author calls himself John, a servant of Jesus Christ and a brother and companion of the believers in suffering (1:9). From the second century onward, most Christians identified him with John the apostle, the author of the Gospel and letters of John. Some ancient and modern scholars, noting differences in style, suggest another prophet named John who was well known to the churches of Asia. John received the visions while exiled on Patmos, a small island off the coast of modern Turkey, "because of God’s Word and the testimony of Jesus Christ." Most scholars date the book to the reign of the emperor Domitian, around A.D. 95, though some date it earlier, near the time of Nero in the late 60s.

Revelation was first sent to seven real churches in the Roman province of Asia. These congregations faced pressure from many directions: hostility from neighbors, the demand to honor the emperor as a god, economic pressure to take part in trade guilds tied to idol worship, false teachers within, and, for some, the dangers of comfort and complacency. Revelation was written to strengthen these believers to remain faithful to Jesus, whatever the cost.

## How to Read Revelation

Revelation combines three kinds of writing. It is a letter, addressed to churches with real needs. It is a prophecy, a message from God calling His people to faithfulness. And it is an apocalypse, a type of Jewish and Christian writing that uses vivid symbols and visions to reveal heavenly realities. Readers in John's day were familiar with this kind of imagery from books like Daniel, Ezekiel, and Zechariah. Revelation contains hundreds of allusions to the Old Testament, and much of its imagery is explained by those earlier Scriptures.

Because the book is full of symbols, numbers, and visions, it is important to read it carefully. Some symbols are explained in the text itself, such as the lampstands, which are churches (1:20), or the dragon, which is Satan (12:9). Numbers often carry symbolic meaning: seven suggests completeness, twelve suggests God's people, and a thousand suggests a vast multitude or a long period. Readers do well to ask what each image meant to the original readers before asking how it applies today.

Christians have read Revelation in several main ways. The **preterist** view understands most of the book as describing events in the first century, such as the persecution of the church by Rome and the fall of Jerusalem or of the Roman Empire. The **historicist** view sees Revelation as a broad overview of church history from the first century to the return of Christ. The **futurist** view holds that most of the book, especially chapters 4–22, describes events that will take place in the period leading up to Christ's return. The **idealist**, or spiritual, view sees the book as portraying timeless truths about the conflict between God and evil that apply in every age. Many readers today combine elements of these views, recognizing that Revelation spoke directly to its first readers, reveals patterns that recur throughout history, and points ahead to the final victory of Christ.

Christians also hold different views about the thousand years described in chapter 20, and about how the events of the book relate to one another in time. Sincere and faithful believers differ on these questions. This commentary presents the main interpretations fairly while focusing on what all agree the book clearly teaches.

## The Shape of the Book

Revelation opens with a vision of the risen Christ standing among His churches (chapter 1), followed by His messages to seven churches in Asia (chapters 2–3). John is then taken up into heaven to see God's throne and the Lamb who was slain, who alone is worthy to open the sealed scroll (chapters 4–5). The opening of seven seals, the sounding of seven trumpets, and the pouring out of seven bowls portray God's judgments on a rebellious world, with interludes that show God's protection of His people (chapters 6–16). The central chapters reveal the spiritual conflict behind history: the dragon, the beast from the sea, and the beast from the land, opposed by the Lamb and His faithful followers (chapters 12–14). The fall of Babylon, the great city that represents human pride and opposition to God, is announced and mourned (chapters 17–18). Then heaven rejoices, Christ returns as the rider on the white horse, Satan is bound and finally defeated, and the dead are judged (chapters 19–20). The book ends with a vision of the new heaven and new earth, the holy city, the new Jerusalem, where God dwells with His people forever (chapters 21–22).

## Themes to Watch For

Watch for the sovereignty of God, who sits on the throne; the Lamb who was slain and conquered through His sacrifice; the call to faithful witness and endurance; worship in heaven and on earth; the reality of spiritual conflict; God's righteous judgment on evil; the protection of God's people; the fall of every power that opposes God; the return of Christ; and the hope of a renewed creation where God wipes every tear away. Revelation is not meant to frighten God's people or to fuel speculation, but to give them hope and courage. Its central message is that Jesus Christ has conquered, and that those who belong to Him will share His victory.
