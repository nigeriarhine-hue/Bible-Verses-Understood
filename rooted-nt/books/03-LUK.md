# Luke

## About This Gospel

Luke is the longest of the four Gospels and the first of a two-volume work; its sequel is the book of Acts. Both are addressed to a man named Theophilus (Luke 1:3; Acts 1:1). Early Christian tradition identifies the author as Luke, a physician and traveling companion of the apostle Paul (Colossians 4:14; 2 Timothy 4:11). Luke was likely a Gentile, and he wrote in polished Greek. He tells us that he carefully investigated everything from the beginning, drawing on eyewitnesses, so that his readers could know the certainty of what they had been taught (Luke 1:1–4). Many scholars date the book to the 60s–80s A.D.

## The Shape of the Book

Luke begins with the births of John the Baptist and Jesus, told from Mary's perspective, and records Jesus' ministry in Galilee (chapters 4–9). At Luke 9:51, Jesus "set his face to go to Jerusalem," and much of the middle of the book (9:51–19:27) is a long journey filled with parables and teachings found only in Luke, including the Good Samaritan, the lost sheep, the lost coin, the prodigal son, the rich man and Lazarus, and the Pharisee and the tax collector. The book ends with Jesus' death, resurrection, and ascension in Jerusalem.

## Themes to Watch For

Luke presents Jesus as the Savior of all people, especially those the world overlooks: the poor, women, Samaritans, Gentiles, tax collectors, and sinners. Jesus summarizes His mission: "the Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost" (19:10). Luke emphasizes prayer, showing Jesus praying at every major turning point, and the work of the Holy Spirit. Joy and praise run through the book from the angels' song at Jesus' birth to the disciples' worship at His ascension. Luke also gives careful attention to money and possessions, warning against greed and calling for generosity to the poor.
