# Hebrews

## About This Letter

Hebrews is unlike any other book in the New Testament. It begins without a greeting, like a sermon, and ends with personal notes, like a letter. Its author calls it [[Hebrews 13:22|the word of exhortation]], and it may have been a sermon written down and sent to a congregation the author knew well.

No one knows for certain who wrote Hebrews. The author never names himself. Some in the early church thought Paul wrote it, and for many centuries it was printed among his letters, but its style and approach are quite different from Paul's, and the author says he received the gospel from those who heard the Lord (2:3), which Paul insisted he did not (Galatians 1:12). Other suggestions have included Barnabas, Apollos, Luke, Silas, and Priscilla. The early Christian scholar Origen famously concluded that only God knows who wrote it. What is clear is that the author was a gifted teacher, deeply versed in the Greek Old Testament, who knew Timothy (Hebrews 13:23).

The first readers seem to have been Jewish believers in Jesus, though the letter speaks to all Christians. They had endured persecution in the past, including public insult, imprisonment, and the loss of property (Hebrews 10:32–34), but now they were weary and tempted to drift away, perhaps to return to the familiar practices of Judaism and avoid further suffering. The letter was probably written before A.D. 70, since it speaks of the sacrificial system as though it were still operating, and never mentions the destruction of the temple. Some suggest it was sent to believers in Rome (Hebrews 13:24).

## The Shape of the Letter

Hebrews alternates between teaching and warning. It shows that Jesus is superior to the angels (chapters 1–2), to Moses (chapter 3), and to Joshua, offering a greater rest (chapter 4). It then presents Jesus as the great high priest, sympathetic to our weakness, appointed in the order of Melchizedek (chapters 5–7), who serves in the true heavenly sanctuary, mediates a better covenant, and offered Himself once for all as the perfect sacrifice for sin (chapters 8–10). Chapter 11 celebrates the faith of the Old Testament saints, chapter 12 calls believers to run their race with endurance, looking to Jesus, and to receive God's discipline as His children, and chapter 13 gives practical instructions for life together. Along the way come five solemn warnings against drifting, unbelief, falling away, willful sin, and refusing God's voice.

## Themes to Watch For

Watch for the word "better": Jesus is a better revelation, a better high priest, the mediator of a better covenant with better promises, offering a better sacrifice, and leading His people to a better country. Watch also for the full divinity and full humanity of Christ, His sympathy with human weakness, the once-for-all sufficiency of His sacrifice, access to God's throne of grace, the danger of hardening one's heart, the meaning of faith, endurance in suffering, and the unshakable kingdom. Hebrews shows how the whole Old Testament points to Christ and calls weary believers to hold fast to Him.
